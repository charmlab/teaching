Principal Component Analysis

A digit image from MNIST is 784 numbers, one per pixel. But nobody writes 784 independent decisions when they scribble a 3. The pixels move together, and the real variation lives in a handful of directions: slant, thickness, loop size. PCA is the algorithm that finds those directions. It centers the data, reads its covariance matrix, and rotates into the coordinate system where variance sorts itself from largest to smallest. Keep the top few axes and you have compression, denoising, and visualization from one eigendecomposition. This is the course's first unsupervised lesson: no labels, no targets, just the shape of the data itself.

0. The hook: 784 pixels, a few directions

Start with the destination. Below is a small handwritten-style digit, stored the honest way: one number per pixel, a vector in a space with hundreds of dimensions. Now watch what happens when I throw almost all of those dimensions away. Keep 1 direction and the reconstruction is a blur. Keep 5 and the shape is back. Keep 15 and you would struggle to tell the copy from the original. Slide $K$ yourself:

Fig 0.1, the destination, up front. A 196-pixel digit image is projected onto its top $K$ principal components and reconstructed; the eigendecomposition behind the slider is computed live in your browser from a dataset of digit variants. The variance-kept readout is real: a few directions carry nearly everything.

How is that possible? Because the image dataset only pretends to be high-dimensional. The digits in the demo vary in a few ways: how wide, how tall, how slanted, how thick, where they sit. Every one of the 196 pixels is a function of those few underlying knobs, so the data hugs a low-dimensional sheet inside the 196-dimensional pixel space. The real MNIST digits behave the same way at 784 dimensions: keep 43 principal components, about 5.5% of the dimensions, and you preserve roughly 80% of the total variance.

That is the whole lesson in one sentence: high-dimensional data usually has few real degrees of variation, and PCA finds them by eigendecomposing the covariance matrix. The rest of the lesson earns that sentence. We review what an eigenvector is, meet the covariance matrix again (you built it in the Gaussian Bayes lesson), run the algorithm step by step, prove that its two natural interpretations are the same thing, and then spend the payoff on compression, denoising, and a data-independent rival called random projection.

1. Why reduce dimensions

Data can fail you in two opposite ways. It can carry too little information: a digitized signal is already an approximation of the thing it measured. Or it can carry too much: redundant features and redundant samples that repeat what you already know. Redundancy is everywhere once you look for it:

🌡️
Temperature, twice
A dataset with columns for °C and °F carries one measurement in two features. The second column adds nothing.
🎬
The Netflix table
A 480,189 × 17,770 user-by-movie ratings matrix. Millions of entries, but taste varies along far fewer axes than 17,770.
🙂
Face images
Thousands of pixels per face, yet faces vary mostly in orientation and expression. Few knobs, many pixels.

Why fight the extra dimensions instead of shrugging at them? Four reasons, and you have met the first one before. High-dimensional spaces are hostile: the curse of dimensionality from the kNN lecture means distances concentrate and data gets sparse, so fewer dimensions make neighbourhood-based reasoning work again. Smaller inputs also mean faster and smaller models. Removing redundancy kills multicollinearity, which you saw destabilize regression weights. And humans can only see in 2-D and 3-D, so any honest look at a dataset needs a map down to a plottable space.

Here is the smallest possible case of the disease and the cure. Two features that measure the same thing produce a point cloud that is secretly a line:

Fig 1.1, two features, one measurement. Cities' temperatures recorded in both °C and °F lie exactly on the line $F = 1.8\,C + 32$: a 2-D dataset with 1-D content. Add sensor noise with the slider and the cloud thickens but the verdict barely moves; the share of variance along the principal direction is computed live from the sample covariance.

Selection or extraction

So we want a map from $x \in \mathbb{R}^D$ down to $z \in \mathbb{R}^K$ with $K < D$. Formally, dimensionality reduction maps a dataset $X \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ to an embedding $Z \subset \mathbb{R}^k$ with $k \ll d$ while approximately preserving the structure you care about. There are exactly two strategies:

  • Feature selection: keep a subset of the original features and delete the rest. The survivors keep their meaning: "we kept temperature and dropped humidity".
  • Feature extraction: build new features as transformations of all the old ones, linear or nonlinear. The new coordinates are combinations, like "0.85 × height + 0.53 × weight".

PCA is feature extraction, and it is the linear workhorse of a whole family. Each member of the family answers "what structure should the embedding preserve?" differently: PCA preserves maximum variation, MDS preserves pairwise $\ell_2$ distances, Isomap (Joshua Tenenbaum's method) preserves geodesic distances along the data manifold, LLE (Sam Roweis's method) preserves each point's local neighbourhood relations, random projections preserve pairwise $\ell_2$ distances with high probability, and autoencoders, next lecture, learn a nonlinear compression with a neural network. Today is the PCA day, with a random-projection detour at the end.

2. Eigen-review: stable directions

Matrices are transformations

Before PCA makes sense, one piece of linear algebra needs to feel physical. A matrix is not a spreadsheet of numbers; it is a machine that moves space. Multiply every point of the plane by $A$ and the plane rotates, stretches, or shears. Rotation and scaling are the pure linear moves. Translation is affine rather than linear, and the standard trick to fold it in is homogeneous coordinates: append a 1 to every point and a translation becomes one more matrix multiply. What a single matrix can never do is bend space nonlinearly; for that you must intersperse nonlinearities between linear maps, which is precisely how neural networks are built and why the autoencoder lesson follows this one.

Two more definitions we will need. The inverse $A^{-1}$ is the machine that undoes $A$, when such a machine exists: $A A^{-1} = A^{-1} A = I$. And the null space $\{v : Av = 0\}$ is the set of directions the machine collapses to nothing; a matrix with a nontrivial null space destroys information and has no inverse.

Eigenvectors: the directions a matrix cannot turn

Now the star of the show. Feed a generic vector to $A$ and it comes out pointing somewhere new. But almost every matrix has a few special directions it cannot turn, only stretch or shrink (or reverse). Those are its eigenvectors, and the stretch factor is the eigenvalue:

$$ A v = \lambda v. $$

Read it as a sentence: applying the whole machine $A$ to $v$ does nothing but multiply it by the number $\lambda$. The direction is stable under the transformation. Grab the vector below and feel for the stable directions yourself:

Fig 2.1, eigenvectors as the directions a matrix cannot turn. Drag the black vector $v$ around the circle and watch its image $Av$ (black): for a generic direction the two disagree, but along an eigenvector (dashed lines) they line up and $Av = \lambda v$ exactly, with $\lambda$ read off live. Switch presets: the shear has one repeated eigen-direction, and the rotation has no real eigenvectors at all, every direction turns. The grid morph slider shows the same matrix acting on all of space.

Symmetric matrices are the nice case

Eigendecomposition of a general $d \times d$ matrix costs about $O(d^3)$ and can produce complex eigenvalues, as the rotation preset just showed. But there is a family of matrices where everything is guaranteed to be clean: real symmetric matrices, $A = A^\top$. For them, all eigenvalues are real, and the eigenvectors can be chosen mutually orthogonal, perpendicular axes of a rotated coordinate frame. Stacking those unit eigenvectors as the columns of $Q$ gives the eigendecomposition

$$ A = Q \Lambda Q^\top, \qquad Q^{-1} = Q^\top, $$

with $\Lambda$ the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. The $Q^{-1} = Q^\top$ is worth savouring: inverting an orthogonal matrix is free, just transpose it. Read the decomposition right to left as three moves: rotate into the eigenvector frame ($Q^\top$), stretch each axis by its eigenvalue ($\Lambda$), rotate back ($Q$). Every symmetric matrix is a stretch dressed up in a rotation.

The covariance matrix is exactly this case

Why do we care? Because the matrix at the heart of this lesson is symmetric. Recall from the Gaussian Bayes lesson: the covariance matrix of a dataset has entries

$$ \Sigma_{ij} = \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j), $$

variances on the diagonal, covariances off it. Covariance is symmetric in its arguments, so $\Sigma = \Sigma^\top$, and the guarantee kicks in:

$$ \Sigma = U \Lambda U^\top, $$

real eigenvalues, orthonormal eigenvectors. And for this particular matrix the eigen-pieces have a meaning you can see. The eigenvectors of $\Sigma$ are the principal directions of the data, the perpendicular axes along which it spreads, and each eigenvalue is the amount of variance along its axis. In the Gaussian lesson you drew $\Sigma$ as an ellipse; the eigenvectors are that ellipse's axes and the eigenvalues are its squared radii. Watch the principal directions emerge from raw data spread:

Fig 2.2, the covariance ellipse and its eigenvectors, live. Set the spread of each feature and their correlation; a fresh cloud is sampled, its sample covariance $\Sigma$ is computed, and the closed-form $2 \times 2$ eigendecomposition draws the eigenvectors scaled by $\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ over the 1-and-2 standard-deviation ellipses. Check: what does a zero eigenvalue of Σ mean?

Suppose the covariance matrix of your dataset has $\lambda_3 = 0$. What is the data doing?

Answer. Zero variance along the third principal direction: every data point has exactly the same coordinate along $u_3$. The cloud is perfectly flat in that direction, so the dataset actually lives in a lower-dimensional subspace, and $u_3$ is in the null space of $\Sigma$ (indeed $\Sigma u_3 = 0 \cdot u_3 = 0$). The °C-and-°F cloud of Fig 1.1 at zero noise is precisely this: its $\lambda_2$ is 0 because the second direction holds no information. This is the degenerate limit of the story PCA exploits; PCA just extends the same logic to directions whose variance is small rather than exactly zero.

3. PCA, step by step

Everything is now on the table: data spreads, the covariance matrix measures the spread, and its eigendecomposition hands us orthogonal directions priced by variance. PCA is the five-step recipe that turns those pieces into a dimensionality reducer. Fix the notation first: the data matrix is $X \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times N}$, one column per data point, $D$ features, $N$ points.

Step 1, center. Compute the mean point and subtract it from every column:

$$ \bar{x} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} x^{(n)}, \qquad X_c = X - M, $$

where $M$ stacks $\bar{x}$ in every column. Why bother? Because variance is spread around the mean. Without centering, the first principal direction would chase the mean's offset from the origin instead of the data's true spread; centering removes that bias so the eigenvectors describe shape, not location.

Step 2, covariance. Build the $D \times D$ covariance matrix of the centered data:

$$ \Sigma = \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N} (x^{(n)} - \bar{x})(x^{(n)} - \bar{x})^\top = \frac{1}{N-1} X_c X_c^\top \;\in\; \mathbb{R}^{D \times D}. $$

The $\frac{1}{N-1}$ rather than $\frac{1}{N}$ gives the unbiased estimate, the same convention as the Gaussian lesson.

Step 3, eigendecompose. Since $\Sigma$ is symmetric,

$$ \Sigma = U \Lambda U^\top, \qquad \lVert u_i \rVert = 1, \qquad \lambda_1 \ge \lambda_2 \ge \cdots \ge \lambda_D \ge 0. $$

Sort the eigenpairs by eigenvalue, largest first. $u_1$ is the direction of greatest variance, each later $u_i$ is the direction of greatest remaining variance perpendicular to all the earlier ones, and because the eigenvectors are mutually orthogonal, each principal component is an uncorrelated source of variation. The eigenvectors of the covariance matrix have earned their own name: principal components.

Step 4, choose $K$ and project. Assemble the top $K$ eigenvectors as the columns of $U_K \in \mathbb{R}^{D \times K}$, so $U_K^\top U_K = I_K$, and encode every point by its coordinates along those directions:

$$ z = U_K^\top x_c \in \mathbb{R}^K, \qquad Z = U_K^\top X_c \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times N}. $$

Step 5, reconstruct. To go back to the original space, rebuild from the kept coordinates and restore the mean:

$$ \tilde{X}_c = U_K Z, \qquad \tilde{X} = U_K Z + M. $$

The reconstruction lands on the $K$-dimensional subspace spanned by the kept components: it is the shadow of the data on that subspace, plus the mean put back.

What the algorithm feels like: a rotation

Steps 1 to 3 have a clean geometric reading. Centering moves the cloud to the origin. The eigenvectors define a rotated set of axes. Expressing every point in eigenvector coordinates, $U^\top x_c$, is a pure rotation of the cloud, no information lost, and in the new frame the covariance matrix is diagonal: the coordinates are decorrelated, and the variances on the diagonal are exactly the eigenvalues, sorted. PCA does not squash anything until step 4; first it just turns the data until the correlations disappear. Watch the turn:

Fig 3.1, the PCA rotation. The centered cloud turns until its principal components become the coordinate axes; the sample covariance of the rotated points is recomputed live at every frame, and its off-diagonal entry melts to zero as the frame aligns, leaving the eigenvalues on the diagonal. Five data points with two features, columns of $X$ (this mirrors the assignment's PCA question with different numbers):

$$ X = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 2 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 4 \end{bmatrix} $$

Center. The mean is $\bar{x} = (4, 3)$, so the centered columns are

(-2,-1)  (-1,-1)  (0,0)  (1,1)  (2,1)

Covariance. With $N - 1 = 4$: sum of $x_1^2$ is 10, sum of $x_2^2$ is 4, sum of $x_1 x_2$ is 6, so

$$ \Sigma = \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix} 10 & 6 \\ 6 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & 1.0 \end{bmatrix} $$

Eigendecompose. The characteristic equation $\lambda^2 - 3.5\lambda + 0.25 = 0$ (trace 3.5, determinant 0.25) gives

$$ \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{3.5 \pm \sqrt{3.5^2 - 4(0.25)}}{2} = \frac{3.5 \pm \sqrt{11.25}}{2} \;\Rightarrow\; \lambda_1 \approx 3.427, \quad \lambda_2 \approx 0.073. $$

Solving $(\Sigma - \lambda_1 I)u = 0$ and normalizing to unit length:

$$ u_1 \approx \begin{bmatrix} 0.851 \\ 0.526 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad u_2 \approx \begin{bmatrix} -0.526 \\ 0.851 \end{bmatrix}, $$

orthogonal, as symmetry promised. $u_1$ points along the cloud at about $31.7^\circ$.

Project to 1-D. With $K = 1$, each $z^{(n)} = u_1^\top x_c^{(n)}$:

Z = [-2.227, -1.376, 0, 1.376, 2.227]

Scorecard. Total variance is $\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 = 3.5$ (the trace of $\Sigma$, as it must be). The kept share is $\lambda_1 / 3.5 \approx 97.9\%$, and the reconstruction error will turn out to be exactly the discarded $\lambda_2 \approx 0.073$. Section 4 proves that last claim; Fig 3.2 lets you check it numerically first.

The code

The whole algorithm is a dozen lines of NumPy. Note the row-versus-column convention: like most Python code, this stores data points as rows of X, so the formulas transpose relative to the math above. 

import numpy as np

def pca_fit(X, K):
    # X: N x D, one data point per row
    mean = X.mean(axis=0)
    Xc = X - mean                          # step 1: center
    Sigma = Xc.T @ Xc / (len(X) - 1)       # step 2: covariance, D x D
    lam, U = np.linalg.eigh(Sigma)         # step 3: eigendecompose (symmetric)
    order = np.argsort(lam)[::-1]          # sort eigenvalues, largest first
    lam, U = lam[order], U[:, order]
    return mean, U[:, :K], lam             # mean, top-K components, spectrum

def project(X, mean, UK):
    return (X - mean) @ UK                 # step 4: Z = N x K

def reconstruct(Z, mean, UK):
    return Z @ UK.T + mean                 # step 5: back to D dims, mean restored

def explained_variance_ratio(lam, K):
    return lam[:K].sum() / lam.sum()       # share of variance the top K keep

4. Two views of PCA

So far PCA is "keep the directions with the most variance", and you might reasonably ask why variance deserves the crown. Here is the principled justification, and it is the deepest fact in this lesson. Consider every possible $K$-dimensional linear subspace you could project the centered data onto, and score each one by how much projecting and reconstructing damages the data, measured in squared $\ell_2$ distance. The best subspace, the one minimizing reconstruction error,

$$ U_K^\star = \arg\min_{U_K^\top U_K = I_K} \frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \big\lVert x_c^{(n)} - U_K U_K^\top x_c^{(n)} \big\rVert_2^2, $$

is spanned by the top $K$ eigenvectors of $\Sigma$. The variance-maximizing subspace and the error-minimizing subspace are the same subspace. Maximizing projected variance and minimizing reconstruction error are the same problem.

Why must they coincide? Pythagoras. Each centered point splits into a part that lies on the subspace (the reconstruction) and a part perpendicular to it (the residual), and the two parts are orthogonal, so

$$ \underbrace{\text{total variance}}_{\text{fixed by the data}} \;=\; \underbrace{\text{variance kept on the subspace}}_{\text{maximize this}} \;+\; \underbrace{\text{reconstruction error}}_{\text{or minimize this}}. $$

The total is a property of the data alone; the subspace only chooses how the total splits. Pushing one term up pulls the other down by exactly the same amount. They are one dial with two labels. Grab the dial:

Fig 4.1, the duality, in your hand. Drag the candidate direction (or use the angle slider) and watch the two bars: variance captured along the line rises exactly as the mean squared residual falls, their sum pinned to the total variance at every angle. The maximum of one bar and the minimum of the other land at the same place, $u_1$, and the leftover error there is $\lambda_2$. Snap buttons take you to $u_1$ (the best direction), to $u_2$ (the worst), or to a random one.

The conservation law also hands us the exact price of truncation. Rotate into the eigenvector frame, where the variances along the axes are $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_D$. Keeping the top $K$ axes keeps $\sum_{i \le K} \lambda_i$ of the variance, so what you lose is everything else:

$$ \operatorname{err}_K = \frac{1}{N-1}\big\lVert X_c - U_K U_K^\top X_c \big\rVert_F^2 = \sum_{i=K+1}^{D} \lambda_i, $$

where the Frobenius norm is just the entrywise Euclidean norm of a matrix,

$$ \lVert A \rVert_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} a_{ij}^2} = \sqrt{\operatorname{tr}(A A^\top)}. $$

Read the boxed result again, because it is the workhorse of the next section: the reconstruction error of PCA is the sum of the discarded eigenvalues. No simulation needed, no test set: the spectrum of $\Sigma$ tells you in advance exactly what keeping $K$ components will cost. That is what Fig 3.2's two agreeing readouts were demonstrating, and it is why the worked example could promise an error of $\lambda_2 \approx 0.073$ before reconstructing anything.

5. Choosing K: the scree plot

One decision remains: how many components? The spectrum already contains the answer, because section 4 priced every choice: keeping $K$ components costs exactly $\sum_{i > K} \lambda_i$. So plot the sorted eigenvalues and look at them. That bar chart has a name, the scree plot, after the rubble that piles up at the foot of a cliff: a steep drop of big eigenvalues, then a long flat tail of small ones. The companion curve is cumulative explained variance, the running fraction $\sum_{i \le K} \lambda_i / \sum_i \lambda_i$, and the standard recipe is to pick the smallest $K$ whose cumulative share clears a target like 90%.

The shape of the spectrum decides how well PCA can possibly do. A spectrum that drops off fast is the friendly case: a few components hoard the variance and you keep almost everything for almost nothing. A flat spectrum is the hostile case: variance is spread evenly, every discarded component costs as much as the ones you kept, and no small $K$ is cheap. Compare both regimes:

Fig 5.1, the scree plot as a decision tool. Toggle between a fast-decaying spectrum, where 3 of 10 components clear 90%, and a flat one, where reaching 90% swallows 8 of 10, computed live from the two spectra.

6. Applications: compress, denoise, see

Time to spend what we built. Three applications, all the same eigendecomposition wearing different hats.

Visualization. The most common PCA call in practice is the humblest: $K = 2$. Project any dataset onto its top two components and you get the best possible 2-D shadow, in the variance-preserving sense, of something you could not otherwise look at. MNIST is the standard demo: each $28 \times 28$ digit is a 784-dimensional point, and the 2-D PCA projection already scatters the ten digit classes into visible clumps. The deeper fact is the one from the hook: MNIST's real variation is small, and retaining 43 components, about 5.5% of the 784 dimensions, preserves roughly 80% of the total variance.

Compression. Storing $z$ instead of $x$ costs $K$ numbers instead of $D$, plus the shared dictionary ($U_K$ and the mean) paid once for the whole dataset. The classic image recipe: split the image into $12 \times 12$ patches, treat each patch as a 144-D vector, run PCA over the patch collection, and keep the top $k$ coefficients per patch. Patches of natural images are massively redundant, so their spectrum decays fast, which section 5 just taught you is exactly when PCA compression is cheap.

Denoising. This one sounds like magic until the spectrum explains it. Add pixel noise to an image and the noise spreads its variance almost evenly across all directions, while the signal concentrates its variance in the top few. Project onto the high-variance components, say 15 of them, and reconstruct: you keep nearly all the signal but only the small slice of noise that happened to land in the kept directions. The low-eigenvalue directions, where noise dominates signal, are deleted wholesale. Try both hats on the digit:

Fig 6.1, compression and denoising with one eigendecomposition, on the synthetic digit set from Fig 0.1. Slide $K$ to trade fidelity for size and watch detail return as components are added; the readouts price the trade in real numbers (dimensions kept, variance kept). Then switch on noise: the corrupted image goes through the same project-and-reconstruct pipeline, and at moderate $K$ the reconstruction sits visibly closer to the clean original than the noisy input does, because the discarded low-eigenvalue directions carried mostly noise. The per-pixel errors are computed live.

Check: why does the denoised image beat the noisy input it was built from?

The reconstruction only ever sees the noisy image. How can its output be closer to the clean original than its own input?

Answer. Because the projection throws information away, and the trick is that it throws away unequally. Write the noisy point as signal plus noise. The signal lives almost entirely in the span of the top components, learned from the dataset's structure, so $U_K U_K^\top$ passes it nearly untouched. The noise is direction-agnostic: it spreads its energy roughly evenly over all $D$ directions, so the projection keeps only about $K/D$ of it. Deleting $D - K$ directions therefore deletes almost no signal and most of the noise. The reconstruction is worse than the clean original but better than the noisy input, which is the useful inequality. The same logic said "compression": there the discarded tail was fine detail; here it is noise. What PCA cannot do is separate noise that lands inside the top components, which is why denoising improves the image rather than perfecting it.

7. Random projections and supervised PCA

The data-independent alternative

Everything so far fits the projection to the data: compute $\Sigma$, eigendecompose, keep the top directions. Here is a provocation: what if you skipped all of that and projected onto random directions? Draw a random matrix $R \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$ with entries

$$ r_{ij} \sim \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}} \, \mathcal{N}(0, 1), $$

and map $y = R^\top x$. No covariance, no eigendecomposition, no pass over the data at all: simple and efficient. It sounds like it should be terrible, and the astonishing thing is that it provably is not. The Johnson-Lindenstrauss theorem: for $n$ points and any tolerance $0 < \epsilon < 1$, if

$$ k \;\ge\; k_0 \;=\; 4\left(\frac{\epsilon^2}{2} - \frac{\epsilon^3}{3}\right)^{-1} \log n, $$

then a linear map such as $y^{(i)} = R^\top x^{(i)}$ preserves all pairwise distances to within a factor of $1 \pm \epsilon$, with high probability. Sit with the strangeness of that bound for a second: the required $k$ depends on the number of points, only logarithmically, and not at all on the original dimension $d$. A thousand points in a million dimensions can be squeezed to $k_0 \approx 332$ dimensions at $\epsilon = 0.5$, and every pairwise distance survives to within 50%, no matter what the data looks like.

So when does PCA still earn its keep? When the promise you need is not "distances roughly survive" but "the most structure survives in very few dimensions". The contrast is data-dependence: random projection uses $y = R^\top x$, one matrix for any dataset, while PCA uses $y = U_{1:k}^\top x$, a matrix fitted to this dataset's variance. At the same small $k$, the fitted projection keeps more of what this particular dataset is doing. Feel the gap:

Fig 7.1, principal versus random, same cloud, same budget of one dimension. The top strip is the data projected onto $u_1$; the bottom strip is a projection onto a random unit direction, redrawn on demand. The variance-kept readouts are computed live: the principal projection always captures $\lambda_1$, the best possible, while the random one lands anywhere between $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_1$ depending on the draw. Random projection's real defence is the Johnson-Lindenstrauss regime: many points, moderate $k$, and no time or memory to fit anything.

Supervised PCA

One more variant closes the survey. Standard PCA is unsupervised: it looks only at $X$ and preserves its variance, labels never consulted. That can backfire when the direction that varies most is not the direction that matters: imagine classifying tumours where the dominant variance in the features is patient age, while the diagnostic signal hides in a lower-variance direction. PCA would happily rank the age direction first, because variance is its only value system. Supervised PCA fixes the value system: it folds the target $Y$ into the reduction and seeks components that both explain variance in the predictors $X$ and help predict $Y$. Same spirit, label-aware criterion.

Check your understanding

What is the essential difference between PCA and random projection as dimensionality reducers?

Both are linear maps, a matrix multiply each. The difference is where the matrix comes from: an eigendecomposition of this data, or a random draw that never sees it.
Both halves are backwards. PCA is the variance-optimal linear projection by the section-4 theorem, and random projection is the cheap one: no covariance, no eigendecomposition, just sampling.
Right. Fitted versus drawn. You pay for PCA's optimality with a pass over the data and an $O(d^3)$ eigendecomposition; you pay for random projection's speed with a $k$ that must grow like $\log n / \epsilon^2$ to make the JL guarantee hold.

8. Summary and method card

One idea carried the lesson: high-dimensional data usually has few real degrees of variation, and the covariance matrix knows where they are. Its eigenvectors are the principal directions, its eigenvalues price each direction in variance, and PCA is just the disciplined use of that fact: center, form $\Sigma$, eigendecompose, keep the top $K$, project, reconstruct. The two ways of wanting this, keep the most variance or lose the least reconstruction accuracy, turned out to be one objective, and the leftover error is exactly the sum of the discarded eigenvalues, so the scree plot lets you buy $K$ with your eyes open. The same decomposition compresses, denoises, and draws 2-D maps; random projection undercuts it on cost when Johnson-Lindenstrauss distance preservation is all you need; supervised PCA patches its label-blindness.

Method card · Principal Component Analysis
AssumptionThe interesting structure lives in a few high-variance linear directions, and variation carries relevant information.
Model classAn orthonormal linear projection onto the top eigenvectors of the data covariance matrix.
Learning / selectionCenter the data, eigendecompose the covariance, keep the top $K$ components, with $K$ chosen via the scree plot / explained variance.
PredictionEncode $z = U_K^\top (x - \bar{x})$; reconstruct $\tilde{x} = U_K z + \bar{x}$; error is the sum of discarded eigenvalues.
StrengthsSimple, closed-form, decorrelating, and optimal (minimum reconstruction error) among linear subspaces; enables compression, denoising, and visualization.
LimitationsOnly linear structure; sensitive to feature scaling (standardize first); variance is not always the same as usefulness for a downstream label.
Next stepNonlinear dimensionality reduction with autoencoders.

Where this sits in the course story: the Gaussian Bayes lesson taught you to read a covariance matrix; this lesson taught you to spend one. And the limitation line above is a loaded gun. PCA can only flatten data onto a linear subspace, but plenty of real data lies on curved sheets, a spiral, a swiss roll, the manifold of face images. Rotating axes cannot unroll a curve. The fix is to let the encoder and decoder be neural networks instead of matrices, learn the compression with gradient descent, and that is the next lesson: autoencoders, PCA's nonlinear successor.

9. End-of-lesson quiz

Two quick checks, then four transfer questions that ask you to reason with the machinery, not recall it. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Quiz 1

In one pass: what are the five steps of PCA, in order?

Inverting the covariance is the Gaussian-classifier move (it appears inside the likelihood), not the PCA move. PCA eigendecomposes $\Sigma$, and nothing here classifies.
Right. Center, covariance, eigendecompose, project, reconstruct: $z = U_K^\top x_c$ forward, $\tilde{x} = U_K z + \bar{x}$ back.
Eigendecomposition applies to the square, symmetric covariance matrix, not to the $D \times N$ data matrix itself, and no step of PCA adds noise.
Quiz 2 · coding

A teammate's explained_variance_ratio(lam, K) must return the fraction of total variance kept by the top $K$ components, given the sorted eigenvalue array lam. Which body is correct?

Right. Kept variance over total variance, $\sum_{i \le K} \lambda_i / \sum_i \lambda_i$. On the worked example with $K = 1$: $3.427 / 3.5 \approx 0.979$.
Dividing by the number of eigenvalues gives the average kept eigenvalue, not a fraction of variance; it is not even guaranteed to be at most 1. The denominator must be the total variance lam.sum().
That compares one eigenvalue to the largest one, a decay ratio, useful for eyeballing the spectrum's slope but not the cumulative share the function promises.
Quiz 3 (transfer): why standardize features before PCA?

You run PCA on medical records with height in metres (variance about 0.01) and annual income in dollars (variance about $10^8$). What goes wrong, and what is the fix?

Answer. PCA's only value system is variance, and variance carries units. Income's variance is ten billion times height's purely because dollars are small units and metres are large ones, so $u_1$ will point almost exactly along income and the explained-variance ratio will announce that one component captures nearly everything. True, and useless: the ranking reflects the choice of units, not the structure of the data. Rescale height to centimetres and the "important direction" changes, which no honest analysis should allow. The fix is to standardize first, divide each centered feature by its standard deviation, so every feature enters with variance 1 and components are ranked by shared structure (correlation) rather than by unit size. This is exactly why the method card lists scale sensitivity as a limitation, and why the assignment-style workflow in section 5's quiz standardizes before computing the spectrum. The exception that proves the rule: if the features share meaningful units, like the pixel intensities of Fig 6.1, raw covariance is fine, because there the variance differences are real signal, not unit artifacts.

Quiz 4 (transfer): why does PCA denoise?

Explain, using the eigenvalue spectrum, why projecting a noisy image onto its top principal components and reconstructing removes much of the noise. What kind of noise defeats this?

Answer. Decompose the noisy data as signal plus noise and ask where each puts its variance. The signal is structured: its variance concentrates in a few directions, the top of the spectrum. Isotropic pixel noise is unstructured: it spreads variance evenly across all $D$ directions, adding a thin layer to every eigenvalue. So in the top-$K$ subspace the ratio of signal to noise is high, and in the discarded $D - K$ directions it is dreadful, mostly noise with a whisper of signal. Projecting keeps the good subspace wholesale and deletes the bad one wholesale: nearly all signal survives, only about $K/D$ of the noise does. That is the Fig 6.1 effect, and the source's recipe of keeping around 15 high-variance components is this argument with a number attached. What defeats it: noise that is itself structured along the top components (then deleting directions cannot separate the two), or signal whose fine detail genuinely lives in the low-variance tail, which gets deleted along with the noise, the smoothing you see at very small $K$.

Quiz 5 (transfer): when is a random projection the better tool?

You must reduce 10-million-point, 100,000-dimensional web-scale feature vectors before a nearest-neighbour search, on a deadline, on one machine. Argue for random projection over PCA here, and name what you give up.

Answer. Count PCA's costs at this scale: forming $\Sigma$ is a $100{,}000 \times 100{,}000$ matrix built from a full pass over ten million points, and eigendecomposition costs on the order of $d^3 = 10^{15}$ operations, neither of which fits the deadline or the machine. Random projection needs no pass over the data at all: draw $R$ with entries $\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and multiply. And the task is nearest-neighbour search, whose currency is pairwise distances, which is precisely what Johnson-Lindenstrauss guarantees: with $k \ge 4(\epsilon^2/2 - \epsilon^3/3)^{-1} \log n$, distances survive to $1 \pm \epsilon$ with high probability, and the bound grows only with $\log n$, independent of the 100,000 input dimensions. What you give up: optimality and interpretability. At equal $k$ a fitted PCA basis would keep more variance (Fig 7.1's gap), the random axes mean nothing individually, and $k_0$ can be large when $\epsilon$ is small. Here the trade is obviously right: the guarantee you need is the one random projection sells, at a price you can actually pay.

Quiz 6 (transfer): where PCA hits its ceiling, and what comes next

A dataset of 2-D points lies on a tight spiral winding three times around the origin: essentially 1-D data, a single coordinate (arc length along the spiral) describes every point. What does PCA do with it, and how could a nonlinear method beat PCA here?

Answer. PCA fails honourably. The spiral winds symmetrically around the origin, so the cloud's covariance is nearly round: $\lambda_1 \approx \lambda_2$, the scree plot is flat, and section 5's own diagnostic announces that no 1-D linear projection is cheap. Any single direction PCA picks collapses different arms of the spiral onto the same coordinates, and the 1-D reconstruction error is huge. The failure is baked into the model class: PCA can only rotate axes and flatten onto a linear subspace, and no rotation unrolls a curve. But the data is 1-D, just 1-D along a curved coordinate. A nonlinear method can learn that coordinate: an autoencoder replaces the encode matrix $U_K^\top$ and decode matrix $U_K$ with neural networks, whose interleaved nonlinearities (section 2's point about what single matrices cannot do) let the decoder bend its 1-D latent line back into a spiral. Same encode-compress-decode-reconstruct template, same reconstruction-error objective, curved subspaces now reachable. That is the next lesson, and this spiral is the picture to carry into it.

That is PCA. The covariance matrix you learned to read in the Gaussian lesson turned out to contain a complete instruction manual for compressing its own dataset: eigenvectors say which directions matter, eigenvalues say by how much, and the discarded tail prices the loss before you pay it. One rotation buys decorrelation, compression, denoising, and a way to see high-dimensional data at all. What it cannot buy is a curve, so next lesson the matrices become networks, and the subspace learns to bend.
