Autoencoders and Variational Autoencoders

The PCA lecture ended with a machine: an encoder that squeezes data down to K numbers and a decoder that rebuilds it, both fixed by an eigenvector computation. This closing lesson asks the obvious next question. What if we learned the encoder and decoder instead, and let them curve? The answer is the autoencoder, a network that invents its own compression. And then we push one step further, to the strangest idea in the course: if the compressed space is smooth enough, you can throw away the encoder, sample a random point, decode it, and get data that never existed. A network trained only to copy its input becomes a machine that generates. This lesson builds that machine from the PCA you already know.

0. The hook: learn the compression

Start with the machine we are going to spend the whole lesson refining. Eight numbers go in on the left. They get squeezed through a narrow waist, K numbers wide, and then rebuilt on the right. The network's only job is to make the output match the input. That sounds like a pointless exercise, copying, until you notice the waist: if K is smaller than 8, the network cannot copy. It is forced to find a K-number summary good enough to rebuild from. Play with the waist:

Fig 0.1, the encoder-bottleneck-decoder machine, this lesson's signature shape. Input $x$ flows through the encoder $f$, narrows to the latent code $z$, and the decoder $g_\theta$ rebuilds $\tilde{x}$. The reconstruction error is computed live over a real 160-sample dataset (its 8×8 covariance is eigendecomposed in your browser), so tightening K genuinely costs error and widening K genuinely buys it back.

Two things to hold onto from that toy. First, the error readout: the quality of a compression is measured by how well you can reconstruct the input from it, the exact criterion PCA used. Second, the shape: input tied to output, with a bottleneck in the middle. Everything in this lesson, including the generative finale, is a variation on that shape. Where we end up is worth stating now, so you can watch it approach: by section 6 the latent space in the middle will be so well organized that we can sample a $z$ from a plain Gaussian, decode it, and get a convincing new data point. Compression first, generation last. Here is the road between them.

1. From PCA to autoencoders

First, the vocabulary this lesson runs on. Each term gets its precise meaning before we use it:

Autoencoder (AE)
A network trained to reconstruct its input through a smaller bottleneck.
Encoder $f$
Maps the input to a latent code: $z = f(x)$.
Decoder $g_\theta$
Maps the code back to a reconstruction: $\tilde{x} = g_\theta(z)$.
Bottleneck / latent
The low-dimensional layer in the middle that forces compression.
Reconstruction loss
How far the output is from the input; the training signal.
Variational autoencoder (VAE)
An autoencoder whose encoder outputs a distribution over latent codes, not a single code.
KL divergence
A regularizer pulling the latent distribution toward a standard normal.
Reparameterization trick
Writing a sampled latent as $\mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$ so gradients can flow through the sampling step.

PCA was already an autoencoder

Look back at what the last lecture built. Given centered data, PCA picks the top K eigenvectors $U_K$ of the covariance matrix and then runs every point through two maps:

$$ z = U_K^\top (x - \bar{x}), \qquad \tilde{x} = \bar{x} + U_K z = \bar{x} + U_K U_K^\top (x - \bar{x}). $$

Read the two maps by their roles. The first one compresses: D numbers become K coordinates along the principal directions. The second one rebuilds: those K coordinates become a D-dimensional point again. Encoder, decoder. And PCA's guarantee, from last lecture, is that among all K-dimensional linear subspaces, this pair minimizes the squared reconstruction error, leaving exactly $\sum_{i=K+1}^{D} \lambda_i$ behind, the sum of the discarded eigenvalues.

So PCA is an autoencoder whose encoder and decoder were derived, not learned: write down the covariance, take eigenvectors, done. That derivation is also a cage. The encoder must be linear. The decoder must be linear. The subspace must be flat. Here is the whole strategic move of this lesson: keep the objective, free the maps. Replace the fixed matrices with learned functions $z = f(x)$ and $\tilde{x} = g_\theta(z)$, and train them, by backpropagation like any network from Lecture 10, to minimize the same quantity PCA minimized:

$$ \min_{f,\, g_\theta}\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} \big\lVert x^{(n)} - g_\theta\!\big(f(x^{(n)})\big) \big\rVert_2^2. $$

In code, the whole idea is a few honest lines. Nothing here runs against a framework; it is the shape of the thing: 

import numpy as np

def encode(x, W_f):              # z = f(x): squeeze D numbers into K
    return sigmoid(W_f @ x)

def decode(z, W_g):              # x_tilde = g_theta(z): rebuild K back to D
    return sigmoid(W_g @ z)

def recon_loss(X, W_f, W_g):     # (1/N) * sum ||x - g(f(x))||^2
    err = 0.0
    for x in X:
        x_tilde = decode(encode(x, W_f), W_g)
        err += np.sum((x - x_tilde) ** 2)
    return err / len(X)          # minimize this by backprop

The bottleneck is the entire trick

One question should be nagging you. If $f$ and $g_\theta$ are flexible networks, why would they not just learn the identity function and copy $x$ straight through with zero loss? Because the architecture makes copying impossible: the code $z$ lives in fewer dimensions than $x$. You cannot store 8 independent numbers in 2. The bottleneck is what turns "copy your input" from a triviality into a compression problem, and every variant in this lesson keeps some version of that constraint. Squeezing through the waist is the point; the reconstruction is just how we check what survived the squeeze.

2. Linear, nonlinear, deep

The linear autoencoder rediscovers PCA

Strip the idea to its simplest version to see what learning buys, and what it does not. A linear autoencoder uses plain matrices with no activations: $z = W_f x$, $\tilde{x} = W_g z$, giving the objective

$$ \min_{W_f,\, W_g}\; \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \big\lVert x^{(n)} - W_g W_f\, x^{(n)} \big\rVert_2^2. $$

What does gradient descent find here? Something beautiful: at a global optimum, a K-dimensional linear autoencoder on mean-centered data reconstructs through exactly the PCA projection,

$$ W_g W_f = U_K U_K^\top. $$

The network, knowing nothing about eigenvectors, converges to the same subspace the covariance eigendecomposition hands you in closed form. That is the sanity check that the learned framework is standing on solid ground: when the model class matches PCA's, the optimum matches PCA's answer.

But there is a wrinkle worth internalizing, because it foreshadows how learned representations behave in general. The product is pinned down; the factors are not. For any invertible $A \in \mathbb{R}^{K \times K}$, the pair $W_f = A U_K^\top$ and $W_g = U_K A^{-1}$ gives

$$ W_g W_f = U_K A^{-1} A\, U_K^\top = U_K U_K^\top, $$

the same projection, the same loss. The factorization is not unique: infinitely many encoder/decoder pairs are equally optimal, and the latent coordinates they produce differ by an arbitrary invertible map. PCA's coordinates were special (orthonormal directions, sorted by variance); a linear autoencoder's coordinates are that subspace in some scrambled basis. Learned codes are useful, not canonical.

Worked by hand: four points, one optimal projection

Take the centered dataset $\{(2,2), (-2,-2), (1,-1), (-1,1)\}$. Its covariance matrix (1/N convention) is

$$ C = \frac{1}{4}\sum_n x^{(n)} x^{(n)\top} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & 2.5 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_1 = 4,\; \lambda_2 = 1,\qquad u_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}. $$

With K = 1 the optimal product is

$$ U_1 U_1^\top = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}. $$

Check it: $(2,2)$ projects to $(2,2)$, error 0. $(1,-1)$ projects to $(0,0)$, squared error 2. Average over the four points: $(0 + 0 + 2 + 2)/4 = 1 = \lambda_2$, exactly the discarded eigenvalue, as the PCA error formula promised.

Non-uniqueness, concretely: $W_f = [0.707, 0.707]$ with $W_g = [0.707, 0.707]^\top$ is optimal. So is $W_f = [1.414, 1.414]$ with $W_g = [0.354, 0.354]^\top$ (that is $A = 2$). Multiply either pair: the same matrix of 0.5s. The code doubles, the decoder halves, the reconstruction never notices.

The orange subspace starts at a random init and rotates onto the dashed green PCA direction as you train; the matrix panel shows $W_g W_f$ converging entry by entry to $U_1 U_1^\top$, and the loss converging to $\lambda_2$. Then slide the refactor $A$: both factors change, the product does not move. Non-uniqueness, live.

There is no better linear subspace to find: PCA is provably optimal among them. A linear autoencoder can at best tie it, which is precisely what makes the equivalence a useful sanity check.

Nonlinear: the payoff for learning

If a linear autoencoder can only ever tie PCA, why learn at all? Because the moment we add activations, the tie breaks. A nonlinear autoencoder passes each layer through a squashing function:

$$ z = f(x) = \sigma(W_f x), \qquad \tilde{x} = g_\theta(z) = \sigma(W_g z), $$

with $\sigma$ a sigmoid, tanh, or ReLU, the same nonlinearities that let MLPs escape linear decision boundaries in Lecture 9. The geometric consequence is the headline of this section. PCA learns the best flat K-dimensional subspace. A nonlinear autoencoder learns a curved K-dimensional manifold: a bent, data-shaped surface. If your data hugs a curve, a flat model pays for every bend, and a curved model follows it. Watch the errors:

Fig 2.1, flat subspace against curved manifold, both with K = 1 and both errors computed live. As you bend the data, PCA's best line (recomputed each frame from the actual covariance) is forced to cut across the arc, while the curved model (a circle fitted by least squares, standing in for a nonlinear autoencoder's learned manifold) keeps hugging it. Same latent dimension, wildly different error, and the ratio readout keeps score.

What does nonlinearity cost? PCA had a closed-form global solution and orthogonal, variance-sorted, interpretable coordinates. The nonlinear problem is non-convex: no closed form, no guarantee gradient descent finds the global optimum, and the latent coordinates that emerge are whatever the optimizer wandered into, generally less interpretable. That is the standing trade of this lesson: expressiveness up, guarantees and interpretability down. You saw the first symptom already in the linear case, where even the best-behaved autoencoder scrambles its coordinates by an arbitrary $A$.

Deep: stack the squeeze

Nothing says the encoder must be one layer. A deep autoencoder composes several:

$$ z = f^{L}\!\big(\cdots f^{2}(f^{1}(x))\big), \qquad \tilde{x} = g_{\theta_1}\!\big(g_{\theta_2}(\cdots g_{\theta_L}(z))\big), $$

each encoder layer squeezing further, each decoder layer undoing one stage of the squeeze. Why stack? For the same reason CNNs stacked in Lecture 11: layers buy abstraction. Early layers capture low-level regularities, later layers capture structures of structures, and the innermost code describes the input at the most abstract level the network found. The mechanics of training are pure Lecture 10: it is one network with a funny shape, and backpropagation neither knows nor cares that the target equals the input.

Check: when would you actually prefer PCA to a deep autoencoder?

A deep nonlinear autoencoder is strictly more expressive than PCA. Why would a sane practitioner still reach for PCA first?

Answer. Expressiveness is not free. PCA gives a convex, closed-form, deterministic answer in one eigendecomposition: no learning rate, no initialization luck, no non-convex landscape, and coordinates you can read (direction of most variance, second most, and so on, each with a variance number attached). If the data is near a flat subspace, PCA ties the autoencoder at a fraction of the cost, and Fig 2.1's low-bend regime shows exactly that: the curved model's advantage only appears when the manifold actually bends. The autoencoder earns its complexity when the structure is genuinely nonlinear, when you have the data and compute to train it, and when you need the latent for downstream tasks more than you need to explain it. A good habit: run PCA first, look at the reconstruction error curve, and let the gap between PCA and a small autoencoder tell you whether the manifold is curved enough to bother.

3. Uses and variants: compression, sparse, denoising

Once you can train a network to reconstruct, several practical machines fall out of the same template. Each variant keeps the encoder-bottleneck-decoder shape and changes one thing: what is constrained, or what is corrupted.

Compression

The straightest use: compress the representation while losing as little information as possible, and test the compression by how well the input reconstructs, which is the loss we already train on. This applies wherever the data has structure to exploit: images, video, speech. For images and video specifically, a convolutional autoencoder swaps the dense layers for convolutions on the way down and the mirror operation on the way up, adapting the architecture to the data exactly as Lecture 11's CNNs did for classification. And note, once more, what prevents the network from cheating its way to perfect reconstruction: the bottleneck. The identity function is not available; a summary must be.

Sparse autoencoders

A sparse autoencoder constrains the latent activations rather than shrinking the layer: it encourages most coordinates of each code $z^{(n)} = f(x^{(n)})$ to be zero. Read that carefully, because the target of the constraint is the defining detail: the penalty acts on the activations $z^{(n)}$, per input, not on the weights. A wide latent layer where only a few units fire per input is a different kind of bottleneck, a budget on how much of the code any single input may use, and it pushes each latent coordinate to specialize:

Fig 3.1, sparsity acting on a real code. The signal below is built from two of eight decoder atoms; with no penalty, the solved code spreads across several coordinates, and as you raise λ the optimizer (40 iterations of a soft-thresholding solver, run live per frame) pushes coordinates to exactly zero until only the two true atoms survive. Push further and reconstruction visibly degrades.

Denoising autoencoders

A denoising autoencoder flips the direction of the problem. Instead of protecting a clean input from a bottleneck, it receives a corrupted input $\tilde{x}$, a noisy version of some clean $x$ it never sees, and must output the clean $x$. Training needs pairs of noisy and clean data, and here is the practical trick: when no such pairs exist, simulate the corruption yourself. Take clean training data, add noise to it, feed the noisy version in, and penalize distance to the clean original. The network learns to invert the corruption process. And there is a precise statement of what the ideal denoiser computes: under squared loss, the optimal prediction is the conditional expectation $\mathbb{E}[x \mid \tilde{x}]$, the average of all clean signals that could have produced this corruption.

Fig 3.2, denoising as projection back onto the clean family. The clean signal (dashed grey) lives in a 2-parameter family; you corrupt it with the noise slider (red), and the denoiser maps the corruption back to the nearest family member (orange), computed live by least squares. The readouts compare the corruption's error against the denoised output's error: most of the noise is simply gone, because noise has no room to survive in a 2-parameter code.

Check: why does the denoiser output E[x | x̃], and what does that imply?

The claim: under squared loss, the best possible denoiser outputs $\mathbb{E}[x \mid \tilde{x}]$. Where does that come from, and when is it a weakness?

Answer. Fix a corrupted observation $\tilde{x}$ and ask which single output $a$ minimizes the expected squared error $\mathbb{E}[\lVert x - a\rVert^2 \mid \tilde{x}]$ over the clean signals that could have produced it. Expanding the square and setting the derivative to zero gives $a = \mathbb{E}[x \mid \tilde{x}]$: the conditional mean. That is why denoising works at all, and also where its characteristic artifact comes from: when several distinct clean signals are all plausible explanations, the optimal answer is their average, which can be a blur that looks like none of them. Averages of sharp things are smooth things. Keep that failure in your pocket: the same "collapse many possibilities to one point" problem returns in section 4 as a core criticism of deterministic autoencoders, and distributions, not points, will be the fix.

4. Why deterministic autoencoders fall short

Everything so far maps each input to a single latent point: $z = f(x)$, one arrow, one destination. That design choice looks innocent and causes three distinct problems, and it pays to name all three precisely, because the VAE is engineered to answer each one.

  1. Proximity mismatch. Nothing in the reconstruction loss makes the latent map respect distances: two inputs can be close, $\lVert x^{(1)} - x^{(2)} \rVert$ small, while their codes $\lVert f(x^{(1)}) - f(x^{(2)}) \rVert$ end up far apart, and vice versa. The latent geometry is an accident of training, so "nearby in latent space" does not reliably mean "similar".
  2. No uncertainty. Some inputs are genuinely ambiguous: several quite different reconstructions would each be plausible. A deterministic autoencoder cannot say so. It collapses every possibility to the one point $\tilde{x} = g_\theta(f(x))$, the same collapse-to-average failure the denoising check just showed.
  3. A disjointed latent space. The encoder only ever places codes where training data lands, so codes clump into islands with voids between them. Sample a latent point from any simple distribution and it almost surely falls in a void, off the data manifold, and the decoder, which never trained there, produces garbage.

The third failure is the one that kills the dream from the hook. We wanted to sample codes and decode them into new data. Try it yourself; the void is clickable:

Fig 4.1, the disjointed latent space, live. The scatter shows the 2-D codes a deterministic autoencoder assigns to three families of synthetic glyphs; drag the probe (or use the arrow keys) and every position decodes on the right in real time. Inside a cluster the decode is a clean glyph; in the gaps the decoder splices families into shapes no training input ever had, and the blend-weight bars show exactly why.

The fix, previewed in one sentence so you can hold it while the machinery arrives: a VAE makes every point wider. Instead of a single latent vector, each input maps to a whole distribution over latent vectors. Wide, overlapping codes give the decoder room to generalize between training points, and, if we also herd all those distributions toward one agreed shape, they fill the voids, giving us a latent space we can finally sample from.

5. Probabilistic autoencoders

To encode a distribution, first make the encoder and decoder speak probability. A probabilistic autoencoder turns both maps into conditional distributions:

$$ f:\; p(z \mid x;\, W_f), \qquad g_\theta:\; p(\tilde{x} \mid z;\, W_g). $$

Mechanically the networks barely change. The layers compute exactly as before; what changes is how we read the outputs. The post-activation values are no longer the code itself, they are the parameters of a distribution over codes. Two standard readings, matching the two activation families you know:

  • Sigmoidal activations: each output lies in $(0,1)$ and is read as the parameter of a Bernoulli conditional, for binary-valued $z$ and $\tilde{x}$: the unit outputs the probability that its bit is on.
  • Linear (identity) activations: outputs are unconstrained reals, read as the parameters of a Gaussian conditional, for real-valued $z$ and $\tilde{x}$: means (and variances) of normal distributions.

The Gaussian reading is the one the VAE runs on: the encoder emits a mean vector $\mu$ and a spread vector $\sigma$, and together they define $q(z) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$, a little Gaussian blob in latent space, one per input. This is "make each point wider" made literal, and it is worth seeing before any loss function:

Fig 5.1, point encoder versus distribution encoder on the same data. Toggle to distributions and every code becomes a Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2 I)$; widen σ and the blobs overlap and tile the space between clusters. The coverage readout is computed from the actual mixture density on a grid, and the average KL to $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ is computed from the closed form introduced in section 6.

Blobs alone are half the answer. Wide codes give the decoder room to generalize, but nothing yet forces the blobs to sit anywhere sensible: they could drift apart and recreate the same voids, just fuzzier. The missing ingredient is a rule about where the blobs must collectively live. That rule is the VAE's second loss term, and it is where we go now.

6. The variational autoencoder

A variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling; Max Welling is this lecture's scientist, a central figure in variational inference and deep learning) puts the pieces together. The encoder outputs a distribution $q(z)$ meant to approximate the true posterior $p(z \mid x)$, and the whole model trains end to end by balancing two objectives at once:

  • Reconstruction loss: decode a latent drawn from $q$ and keep the output close to the input. This is the old objective; it keeps the code informative.
  • Regularization loss (KL divergence): keep each $q(z)$ close to the standard normal $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$. This is the new one; it keeps the latent space smooth, centred, and gap-free.

Why the KL term? Because of how we want to generate

The KL term looks arbitrary until you start from the goal. To generate, we treat the decoder as a generative model: draw $z \sim p(z)$ from some simple prior, then draw $x \sim p(x \mid z;\, W_g)$. Notice what we cannot use: $p(z \mid x)$. Generating from the posterior would require feeding in a real $x$, and the output would be a lightly remixed copy of that $x$, which is not generation, and is a privacy problem besides if the training data is sensitive. We need a $z$ that is unconditioned on any real input.

So here is the trick, and it is the conceptual heart of the VAE. During training, pull every encoder distribution $p(z \mid x;\, W_f)$ toward one fixed target, $\mathcal{N}(z;\, 0, I)$. If training succeeds, the codes of the whole dataset collectively fill a standard Gaussian ball, and then we are entitled to declare $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and sample from it, with no encoder and no $x$ in sight. Every sample lands where some training input's blob lives, so the decoder is never asked to decode a void. The model ties the two stages together through the marginal likelihood, which integrates over the latent:

$$ p(x^{(n)};\, W_f, W_g) \;=\; \int_z\, p(x^{(n)} \mid z;\, W_g)\; p(z \mid x^{(n)};\, W_f)\; dz. $$

For the diagonal Gaussians a VAE uses, the KL penalty has a clean closed form per latent dimension:

$$ \mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2)) \,\big\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, I)\big) = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\big(\mu_k^2 + \sigma_k^2 - 1 - \ln \sigma_k^2\big), $$

which is zero exactly when $\mu = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$, and grows as codes drift from the origin ($\mu_k^2$) or as blobs get overconfident ($\sigma_k \to 0$ blows up $-\ln \sigma_k^2$). Now feel the two losses fight. Reconstruction wants blobs far apart and narrow, each input keeping its own private, precise code. The KL wants every blob centred on the origin with unit width, all inputs sharing one big Gaussian. Training is the negotiation, and the balance point decides what kind of machine you get:

Fig 6.1, the reconstruction-KL negotiation on the glyph world, with every number computed live: the average closed-form KL, the pixel-space reconstruction error of decoding the code means $\mu$, and the fraction of 220 fresh $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ samples that decode onto the data manifold. At low β reconstructions are crisp and generations are garbage; at high β every prior sample decodes plausibly and reconstructions blur as families overlap. The dashed rings are the target prior.

Check your understanding

Your classmate says: "the KL term is just a regularizer to stop overfitting, like weight decay." What is the better story?

Weight decay shrinks parameters; the KL shapes the latent distribution itself, toward a specific target chosen because we intend to sample from it later. Drop weight decay and a classifier still classifies; drop the KL and a VAE stops being a generator, which Fig 6.1's low-β end shows directly.
Right, and say the trade-off out loud: more KL weight buys a smoother, prior-matched, sampleable latent (better generation) at the price of reconstruction accuracy, and less buys the reverse. A VAE without the KL term is just a noisy deterministic autoencoder: a compressor, not a generator.
The KL term typically fights the reconstruction gradient rather than smoothing it. Its purpose is structural, aligning the latent with the prior you will sample from, not an optimization convenience.

The reparameterization trick

One engineering obstacle remains, and it sits right in the middle of the forward pass. Training needs backpropagation from the reconstruction loss back to the encoder's outputs $\mu$ and $\sigma$. But between them and the decoder there is a sampling step, draw $z$ from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and a random draw is not a differentiable function: there is no gradient of "roll the dice" with respect to the dice's parameters. The fix is a change of variables so tidy it feels like a card trick. Write the sample as

$$ \tilde{z} = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon, \qquad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1). $$

Same distribution, different plumbing: scale a standard Gaussian by $\sigma$, shift it by $\mu$. All the randomness now lives in $\epsilon$, an input that no parameter influences, and given $\epsilon$ the latent is plain arithmetic in $\mu$ and $\sigma$: differentiable, backprop-friendly. The gradient flows through the deterministic path and simply does not need one through the noise. At test time, generation reuses the same pipe: sample $\epsilon$, decode.

Worked by hand: one reparameterized sample and its KL

Say the encoder returns $\mu = [0.5, -1.0]$ and $\sigma = [0.8, 0.3]$, and we draw $\epsilon = [1.2, -0.5]$. The sample is elementwise:

$$ \tilde{z} = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 + 0.8 \times 1.2 \\ -1.0 + 0.3 \times (-0.5) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.46 \\ -1.15 \end{bmatrix}. $$

And this input's KL penalty, from the closed form, dimension by dimension:

$$ \mathrm{KL} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(0.5^2 + 0.8^2 - 1 - \ln 0.8^2\big) + \tfrac{1}{2}\big(1.0^2 + 0.3^2 - 1 - \ln 0.3^2\big) = 0.168 + 1.249 = 1.417 \text{ nats}. $$

Read the two terms: dimension 1 sits near the prior and pays little; dimension 2, with its far mean and narrow spread, pays nine times more. The gradient of this number through $\mu$ and $\sigma$ is what nudges every blob toward the origin, and it exists precisely because $\tilde{z}$ was written as arithmetic rather than as a dice roll.

Fig 6.2, the reparameterization trick as geometry. The purple point is $\mu$, the orange ellipses are the 1σ and 2σ contours of $q$, the ghost cloud is 260 real reparameterized draws, and the green arrow is $\sigma \odot \epsilon$ carrying you to the red sample $z$. Slide ε with fixed $\mu, \sigma$: the sample orbits the blob. Slide $\mu$ or $\sigma$ with fixed ε: the same noise lands somewhere new, which is exactly the dependence the gradient differentiates. The KL readout updates from the closed form.

Check your understanding

Why write $z = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$ instead of just sampling $z \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ directly inside the network?

They are the same distribution: scaling a standard normal by σ and shifting by μ is the definition of $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. The trick changes the computational graph, not the statistics.
The cost of the two is essentially identical, one Gaussian draw either way. What differs is differentiability, not speed.
Right. "Roll the dice with parameters μ, σ" is a dead end for backprop; "roll a fixed dice, then scale and shift the outcome" gives the same z with a clean gradient path through the scale and the shift. That one line is what makes end-to-end VAE training possible.

The code

The two ideas compress into two small functions. This is the schematic NumPy version (a real implementation on images would use convolutional encoder layers producing $\mu$ and $\sigma$, and transposed convolutions in the decoder; the loss is the same): 

def reparameterize(mu, sigma):
    # eps ~ N(0, 1): all the randomness, isolated from the parameters
    eps = np.random.randn(*np.shape(mu))
    return mu + sigma * eps          # z: plain arithmetic in mu and sigma

def vae_loss(x, x_hat, mu, sigma):
    # 1) reconstruction: keep the output close to the input (MSE; BCE also common)
    recon = np.mean((x - x_hat) ** 2)
    # 2) regularization: KL( N(mu, diag(sigma^2)) || N(0, I) ), closed form
    kl = 0.5 * np.sum(mu**2 + sigma**2 - 1 - np.log(sigma**2))
    return recon + kl                # train end-to-end on the sum

Check your understanding: complete the code

A teammate's vae_loss reads kl = 0.5 * np.sum(mu**2 + sigma**2 - 1 - ???). Which completion is correct, and what does that term do?

Right. With it, the per-dimension term $\tfrac{1}{2}(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \ln\sigma^2)$ is exactly zero at μ = 0, σ = 1 and positive everywhere else. The $-\ln\sigma^2$ piece is the anti-collapse force: without it, the loss would happily shrink every blob to a point and quietly reinvent the deterministic autoencoder, gaps and all.
The mean penalty is already there, the mu**2 term. The missing piece must involve σ, and it must diverge as σ → 0 to keep blobs from collapsing; log of the variance is what the closed form actually derives.
The exact form matters: the closed-form KL is $\tfrac{1}{2}(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \ln\sigma^2)$, not a hand-picked penalty. Get it wrong and the minimum is no longer at σ = 1, so trained blobs would not match the prior you intend to sample from.

Generation: the payoff

Now collect the winnings. Train the VAE, then unplug the encoder entirely. What remains is: sample $\epsilon$, decode. Because the KL term spent all of training herding codes into $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, a fresh Gaussian draw is indistinguishable from a real input's code, and the decoder turns it into a convincing new sample. The same smoothness buys interpolation: the straight line between two codes stays on the manifold, so it decodes into a gradual morph rather than the spliced garbage of Fig 4.1.

Right: each press of Generate draws nine fresh $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ by Box-Muller and decodes them; green borders mark samples that landed on the data manifold. The encoder is nowhere in this figure. That is the point.

Check: why not generate from p(z | x)? It is right there.

The trained encoder gives a perfectly good distribution $p(z \mid x)$. Why insist on sampling from the unconditional $p(z) = \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ instead?

Answer. Because of what the conditioning does. To sample $p(z \mid x)$ you must supply an $x$, a real data point, and the code you draw sits inside that input's little blob. Decode it and you get that input back with minor variation: a paraphrase of a training example, not a new sample. That defeats the purpose (we wanted data that did not exist) and it leaks: if the training data is sensitive, "generate" would emit lightly disguised copies of real records, which is a privacy problem the source flags explicitly. Generation needs a $z$ unconditioned on any real input. The entire KL apparatus exists to make that possible: it trains $p(z \mid x;\, W_f)$ toward the fixed $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ for every $x$, so that after training, the unconditional prior is a faithful description of where codes live, and sampling it requires no input at all.

7. Summary and method card

One objective carried the whole lesson: every method here judges its representation by reconstruction, $\min \sum_i \lVert x^{(i)} - \tilde{x}^{(i)} \rVert^2$ with $\tilde{x}^{(i)} = g_\theta(f(x^{(i)}))$. What changed, step by step, was who $f$ and $g_\theta$ are allowed to be:

The family, side by side
PCAEncoder $U_{1:K}^\top$, decoder $U_{1:K}$: a fixed orthogonal projection from an eigendecomposition. Closed form, convex, interpretable; flat subspaces only.
Linear AEEncoder $W_f x$, decoder $W_g z$, both learned, no activations. At its optimum, recovers the PCA subspace: $W_g W_f = U_K U_K^\top$, with a non-unique factorization.
Nonlinear AEEncoder $f(x)$, decoder $g_\theta(z)$, MLPs with activations. Learns curved K-dimensional manifolds PCA cannot; non-convex, less interpretable, still a point encoder.
VAEEncoder $q(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \operatorname{diag}(\sigma^2))$, decoder $p_\theta(x \mid z)$. Trained on the ELBO: reconstruction plus KL to $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, sampled via $\mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$. A smooth, sampleable latent: a generative model.
Method card · Autoencoders & VAEs
AssumptionData lie near a low-dimensional, possibly curved manifold, and a good code is one you can reconstruct from. For generation: the latent should additionally be smooth and sampleable.
Model classLearned encoder/decoder networks through a bottleneck; deterministic (AE, with sparse/denoising/convolutional variants) or distributional (VAE).
Learning / selectionMinimize reconstruction loss by backpropagation; for VAEs add the KL term toward $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and use the reparameterization trick $\tilde{z} = \mu + \sigma \odot \epsilon$ to differentiate through the sampling step.
PredictionCompress: $z = f(x)$. Reconstruct: $\tilde{x} = g_\theta(z)$. Generate (VAE): $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, then decode.
StrengthsNonlinear autoencoders capture curved structure PCA cannot; VAEs give a smooth, sampleable latent space and can generate new data; variants handle denoising and sparse coding with the same template.
LimitationsNon-convex training with no closed-form optimum; codes less interpretable than PCA's; VAEs face a reconstruction-vs-regularization trade-off (too much KL blurs reconstructions, too little breaks generation).
Next stepBroader generative modeling: GANs and diffusion models build directly on this decode-from-noise idea.

Where this sits in the course story: this lesson closes the dimensionality-reduction thread that PCA opened, and it closes it by changing what dimensionality reduction is for. PCA compressed to summarize. The autoencoder learned the compression. The VAE made the compressed space itself into an object worth owning: a smooth, complete map of the data manifold, where every coordinate decodes to something real. Once you have that, generation stops being magic; it is a table lookup in a space built for the purpose. The generative models beyond this course, GANs, diffusion, all inherit that move.

Quiz 1

In one sentence each: what do the encoder, the bottleneck, and the decoder contribute to an autoencoder?

Denoising is one variant, not the core; and the bottleneck constrains the activations' dimensionality, not the weights. Think compression first.
Right. Squeeze, forbid copying, rebuild, measure. Every variant in the lesson edits one clause of that sentence.
Quiz 2

A VAE and a deterministic autoencoder have identical architectures except for the latent layer. What two changes turn the AE into the VAE, and what does each buy?

Right, and the division of labour is worth restating: the distribution answers proximity mismatch and missing uncertainty, the KL answers the disjoint latent. Both are needed; blobs without KL drift apart, KL without blobs has nothing to herd.
Neither the latent width nor the reconstruction flavour (MSE vs BCE) is the distinction; both appear in both models. The distinction is distribution-per-input plus prior-matching.
The encoder is essential throughout training (it produces μ and σ); it is only at generation time that you can leave it unplugged.
Quiz 3 (transfer): choosing between PCA and a nonlinear autoencoder

You have a new sensor dataset, 200 dimensions, modest sample count, and a manager who wants "the dimensionality reduced". Argue when you would reach for a nonlinear autoencoder over PCA, and what you would check first.

Answer. Check the shape of the problem before choosing the tool. First run PCA, because it is nearly free: one eigendecomposition, no tuning, and its reconstruction-error curve $\sum_{i>K}\lambda_i$ tells you how much structure is linear. If a small K already reconstructs well, stop; PCA wins on every other axis (convexity, determinism, interpretable variance-ranked coordinates, robustness at small sample sizes). Reach for the nonlinear autoencoder when the evidence says the manifold curves: PCA needs a suspiciously large K, or the residuals show systematic structure, or domain knowledge says the degrees of freedom are things like angles and phases, which trace curves, not lines (Fig 2.1 is the cartoon of this). Then be honest about the costs you are accepting: non-convex training that needs data and tuning, codes with no canonical meaning (the factorization is not even unique in the linear case), and a validation-set reconstruction comparison against the PCA baseline to prove the added machinery earned its keep. The autoencoder is the upgrade you buy when the data demands it, not the default.

Quiz 4 (transfer): a colleague's shortcut generator

A colleague trains a standard (deterministic) autoencoder on customer records, then proposes generating synthetic customers by sampling random latent vectors and decoding them, "like a VAE but simpler". Predict what happens, and explain what the VAE's extra machinery would have bought.

Answer. Predict garbage, for the exact reason Fig 4.1 makes clickable. A deterministic encoder places codes only where training data lands, so the latent space is islands and voids; a random sampling distribution knows nothing about the islands, so most draws land in voids where the decoder never trained, and decode to blends and splices that match no real customer. Occasionally a draw lands near an island and looks plausible, which makes the failure worse: the output quality is a lottery. There is also no principled sampling distribution to use: with no prior enforced during training, the code cloud has arbitrary location, scale, and shape, so even fitting a Gaussian to the codes afterward leaves the voids inside the cloud untouched. The VAE's machinery answers each piece: encode distributions, not points, so codes claim neighbourhoods and overlap; add the KL term so all that probability mass is herded into $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, making the sampling distribution and the code distribution the same object by construction; and train the decoder on reparameterized samples $\mu + \sigma\odot\epsilon$, so it has literally practised decoding the kind of z the prior will produce. One caution transfers back: on sensitive records, sampling near a specific training code can still paraphrase a real customer, so the privacy argument for avoiding $p(z \mid x)$-style generation applies to any generator trained on personal data.

Quiz 5 (transfer): the GMM as a different route to generation

Assignment work in this course also fits Gaussian Mixture Models. A GMM can generate new points and can score any point's log-likelihood. Contrast the GMM route with the VAE route: how does each generate, and what does the GMM's log-likelihood buy that a plain VAE decode does not?

Answer. Both are generative models, but they put the model in different places. A GMM models the data density directly in input space: $p(x) = \sum_k \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$. Generation is two dice rolls: pick a component by its weight, then draw from that Gaussian. No encoder, no decoder, no latent geometry to engineer; the price is that a mixture of full-dimensional Gaussians struggles when the data hugs a thin curved manifold in a high-dimensional space, exactly the regime autoencoders were built for. The VAE instead models a simple density in latent space and learns a decoder to carry it onto the manifold: sample $z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$, decode. The extra thing the GMM hands you for free is an explicit, computable $\log p(x)$ for any query point, which makes it a natural anomaly detector: a record whose log-likelihood sits far below the training distribution's typical values is flagged as not-from-this-distribution. A vanilla VAE gives no such single number cheaply (its likelihood is the intractable integral over z from section 6; reconstruction error is a usable but cruder proxy). Rule of thumb: want density and anomaly scores on moderate-dimensional data, GMM; want realistic samples and a structured latent for high-dimensional, manifold-shaped data, VAE.

That closes the thread. PCA compressed with fixed linear maps and left us one question: what if the maps could learn? The autoencoder answered it, the bottleneck kept the answer honest, sparse and denoising variants put the template to work, and then the latent space's gaps exposed the limit of encoding points at all. Encoding distributions and pinning them to a prior turned the compressor into a generator: sample noise, decode data. The dot product that started this course measured similarity between what exists; the decoder that ends it manufactures things that never did. Compression, pushed far enough, became imagination.
