Clustering & Density Estimation

PCA compressed data and autoencoders learned to reconstruct it, but both lectures dodged the most human question you can ask of an unlabeled dataset: what groups is it made of? Nobody hands you labels for that. This lecture builds the machinery that finds groups on its own. K-means does it with the simplest possible loop, guess some cluster centres, assign every point to its nearest centre, move each centre to the middle of its points, repeat. Then we soften the assignments, ask what probability model that loop secretly believes in, and arrive at Gaussian mixtures fitted by expectation maximization: the same alternation, but now it learns shapes, weights, and a full density you can sample from. One loop, seen twice, and the second view is one of the most-used algorithms in all of statistics.

0. The hook: structure without labels

Here is a scatter of points. No labels, no targets, no teacher. And yet you have already clustered it: your eye picked out the clumps before you finished reading this sentence. The data plainly has groups, and it plainly has a bumpy density, dense here, empty there. The question of this lecture is whether an algorithm can recover what your eye just did, and then go one step further than your eye can: write down the density as a formula and generate brand-new points from it.

Everything below builds that figure from scratch. First the simplest version, K-means, where a cluster is just a centre point and membership is all or nothing. 1. Unsupervised learning: three ways to summarize

Recall the deal supervised learning offered: inputs and targets together, $\{(x^{(i)}, t^{(i)})\}$, a clear goal, and instant feedback because every prediction can be checked against a target. Unsupervised learning is what remains when the targets are taken away. We are given only inputs $\{x^{(i)}\}$, labels unknown, the goal is less explicit, and nothing ever tells us whether our output was correct. That sounds hopeless until you notice how much structure survives without labels: we can still reduce dimensions, find hidden causes, model the density of the data, and find groups. Why bother? Three practical payoffs keep coming back: detect outliers (points the structure cannot explain), compress the dataset (replace many numbers with few), and facilitate other learning downstream (good structure makes supervised tasks easier).

The field organizes into three classes, and it helps to see them as three answers to "summarize each example with what?":

  • Dimensionality reduction: summarize each case with a few continuous variables. That was PCA and the autoencoder lectures.
  • Clustering: summarize each case with a prototypical example, the cluster it belongs to. This lecture.
  • Density estimation: summarize the whole dataset with a distribution over the data space. Also this lecture, and the two turn out to be the same machinery.

Fig 1.1, one dataset, three summaries, all computed live. Reduce projects every point onto its first principal direction (the PCA move from Lecture 18). Cluster colours each point by its nearest of three centroids found by a real K-means run. Density shades the plane by a fitted mixture of Gaussians, bright where data is likely. Same points every time; what changes is the kind of summary we ask for.

Vocabulary before we start, because the words do real work. Clustering means grouping $N$ examples into $K$ related groups. A centroid $m_k$ is the prototype of cluster $k$, its mean. A responsibility $r_k^{(n)}$ measures how much cluster $k$ is assigned point $n$: hard 0-or-1 in K-means, a number in $[0, 1]$ later. The remaining words, mixture model, latent variable, EM, get defined when they earn their entrance.

2. K-means: assign, update, repeat

The loop

Set up the problem concretely. We have data $\{x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(N)}\}$ living in Euclidean space, $x^{(n)} \in \mathbb{R}^d$, and we believe it belongs to $K$ clusters. A cluster, for K-means, is nothing but its centroid, so the model is $K$ points $m_1, \dots, m_K \in \mathbb{R}^d$. Initialize them randomly. Then alternate two steps until the assignments stop changing:

Assignment step. Give every point to its nearest centroid. Writing the responsibilities as 1-of-K vectors,

$$ \hat{k}(n) = \arg\min_k d(m_k, x^{(n)}), \qquad r_k^{(n)} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } k = \hat{k}(n) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases} $$

where $d$ is a distance function; with the usual L2 choice the winner is $\hat{k}(n) = \arg\min_k \lVert m_k - x^{(n)} \rVert^2$.

Update step. Move every centroid to the centre of gravity of the points it just won:

$$ m_k = \frac{\sum_n r_k^{(n)} x^{(n)}}{\sum_n r_k^{(n)}}. $$

Because the responsibilities are 0 or 1, that fraction is just "the mean of my points": the numerator adds up the assigned points, the denominator counts them. That is the entire algorithm. The hyperparameters are the number of clusters $K$, the initial centroids, and the distance function, and all three will come back to bite us shortly. But first, drive the loop yourself:

Fig 2.1, the K-means loop, this lesson's first signature figure. Drag the crosses to seed the centroids anywhere, then alternate the two steps: Assign colours every point by its nearest centroid and tints the plane by the same rule, and Update slides each centroid to the mean of its points, leaving a trail. The inertia readout is the real objective $J$, recomputed after every step; watch it fall and never rise until the assignments freeze and the run declares convergence. Re-seed and a different starting position can end somewhere different.

Worked by hand: one full iteration on four points

Four points, two clusters: $x^{(1)} = (1, 1)$, $x^{(2)} = (2, 1)$, $x^{(3)} = (4, 3)$, $x^{(4)} = (5, 4)$, with initial centroids $m_1 = (0, 0)$ and $m_2 = (4, 4)$.

Assignment step. Squared distances to $(m_1, m_2)$:

x₁: (2, 18) → cluster 1   x₂: (5, 13) → cluster 1
x₃: (25, 1) → cluster 2   x₄: (41, 1) → cluster 2

So $r^{(1)} = r^{(2)} = (1, 0)$ and $r^{(3)} = r^{(4)} = (0, 1)$, and the objective is $J = 2 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 9$.

Update step. Each centroid moves to the mean of its points:

m₁ = ((1,1) + (2,1)) / 2 = (1.5, 1)
m₂ = ((4,3) + (5,4)) / 2 = (4.5, 3.5)

Recompute: $J = 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.5$, down from 9. Run the assignment step again and every point keeps its cluster, so the algorithm has converged in one iteration. Fig 2.4 executes these exact numbers in code.

What is it optimizing?

The loop looks like a heuristic, but it is doing textbook optimization. K-means minimizes the total within-cluster distance, also called the inertia or total variation:

$$ \min_{\{m\}, \{r\}}\; J(\{m\}, \{r\}) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{k=1}^{K} r_k^{(n)}\, d(m_k, x^{(n)}) \qquad \text{s.t. } \sum_k r_k^{(n)} = 1\ \forall n,\quad r_k^{(n)} \in \{0, 1\}. $$

Two groups of variables, and the loop is block coordinate descent on them: the assignment step holds the centroids fixed and picks the responsibilities that minimize $J$ (nearest centroid is by definition the best assignment), and the update step holds the responsibilities fixed and picks the centroids that minimize $J$ (the mean of a cluster's points minimizes their summed squared distance to one point; if we used plain distances instead of squared ones the minimizer would be the median). Each half-step can only lower $J$ or leave it alone, so $J$ never increases, and since there are finitely many assignments the loop must eventually stop changing. When it stops, we are at a local minimum at least. That is the falling staircase you watched in Fig 2.1's readout.

Check: why does the mean minimize the summed squared distance?

The update step claims that for a fixed set of points $x^{(1)}, \dots, x^{(n)}$, the single point $m$ minimizing $\sum_i \lVert x^{(i)} - m \rVert^2$ is their mean. Why?

Answer. Differentiate and set to zero: $\nabla_m \sum_i \lVert x^{(i)} - m \rVert^2 = -2 \sum_i (x^{(i)} - m) = 0$ gives $m = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x^{(i)}$, and the objective is a convex bowl in $m$, so this stationary point is the global minimum. The name "K-means" is this fact wearing a hat. The companion fact is worth storing next to it: the mean minimizes the sum of squared distances, while the median minimizes the sum of distances. Swap the distance function in $J$ and the correct update step changes with it, which is one reason the distance function is listed among the hyperparameters.

K-means never merges clusters on its own; each point picks exactly one nearest centroid and each centroid averages only its own points. A cluster can end up empty, but two centroids never fuse into one.

Where it gets used

Two applications from the lecture make the algorithm feel less abstract. Image segmentation: treat every pixel as a point in colour space, $(R, G, B)$, and cluster; pixels in the same cluster form a segment. If you want the segments to be spatially coherent, superpixels, extend each pixel's feature vector with its coordinates, $(R, G, B, x, y)$, so that "similar" now means similar colour and nearby. Vector quantization: compression by clustering. Run K-means on the pixels, then store each pixel as just the index of its centroid plus one shared table of $K$ centroid colours. Smaller $K$ compresses harder at lower quality; the reconstruction replaces every pixel with its cluster's prototype, which is exactly the "summarize each case with a prototypical example" promise from section 1 made literal.

The two headaches: initialization and K

Headache one: $J$ is non-convex in the joint variables, so block coordinate descent can park in a local minimum. Two centroids can end up splitting one true clump between them while another clump is served by a single stretched centroid, and the loop is perfectly converged and perfectly wrong. The standard remedies are cheap: add small noise and re-run, run multiple random initializations and keep the best $J$, or use split-and-merge moves, merge two nearby clusters and split a large one, to hop out of the trap. See the failure for yourself:

Fig 2.2, local minima are real. Every press of "Random restart" seeds three centroids at fresh random positions and runs K-means to convergence, live. The bars log the final inertia of each run: some restarts find the natural three-clump answer (the low bars, marked with the star), others converge with two centroids sharing one clump (the tall bars), and both are fixed points of the loop. Multiple restarts plus keep-the-best is the standard defence.

Headache two: the loop cannot choose $K$, because $J$ cheats. Add a centroid and the best achievable $J$ can only fall; push all the way to $K = N$ and every point becomes its own centroid, driving $J$ to its global minimum of exactly 0, which summarizes nothing. So we cannot pick $K$ by minimizing $J$. The practical recipe: run K-means for a range of $K$, plot inertia against $K$, and pick the elbow, the value where the curve stops falling steeply and starts crawling. Past the elbow, extra centroids are subdividing real clumps rather than discovering new ones, and the payoff per centroid collapses:

Fig 2.3, the elbow method on a dataset with three true clumps. For every $K$ from 1 to 8 the curve shows the best inertia over several real K-means restarts, computed live. Click any point on the curve to see the corresponding clustering on the right: the drop from 2 to 3 is enormous, the drop from 3 to 4 is bookkeeping, and that bend at $K = 3$ is the elbow. Pushing toward $K = N$ would drive the curve to exactly zero and tell you nothing.

Check your understanding

Why can we not choose $K$ by simply minimizing the inertia $J$ over $K$ as well?

$J$ is already non-convex at fixed $K$; that is the local-minima headache, not the choosing-K headache. The problem with varying $K$ is different in kind.
The elbow method does compute $J$ for a whole range of $K$, and it is affordable. Cost is not the obstacle.
Right. More centroids can only help the objective, so $J$ is a rigged judge: it always votes for bigger $K$. That is why we look for the elbow, the point of diminishing returns, instead of the minimum. Keep this in your pocket: the same pathology returns for mixture models in section 7.

The code

One step of the loop is a few lines. Assignments come from an argmin over distances, updates from a masked mean: 

import numpy as np

def kmeans_step(X, centroids):
    # assignment step: index of the nearest centroid for each point
    d2 = ((X[:, None, :] - centroids[None, :, :]) ** 2).sum(axis=2)
    assign = d2.argmin(axis=1)              # (N,)  hard responsibilities
    # update step: each centroid moves to the mean of its points
    K = centroids.shape[0]
    new_centroids = centroids.copy()
    for k in range(K):
        if (assign == k).any():
            new_centroids[k] = X[assign == k].mean(axis=0)
    J = d2[np.arange(len(X)), assign].sum() # inertia after assignment
    return assign, new_centroids, J

Do not take my word that it reproduces the worked example. Run it:

Check: why can the inertia never increase during the loop?

Fig 2.1's readout only ever went down or stayed flat. What guarantees that, exactly?

Answer. Both half-steps are exact minimizations of the same function with half the variables frozen. In the assignment step the centroids are fixed and each point independently picks the centroid minimizing its term of $J$; switching a point to a nearer centroid can only shrink its contribution, and points that were already best-assigned keep their value. In the update step the assignments are fixed and each centroid moves to the mean of its cluster, which is the unique minimizer of that cluster's summed squared distance. So every step satisfies $J_{\text{after}} \le J_{\text{before}}$. A bounded-below quantity that never increases and has finitely many assignment configurations to visit must stop changing: convergence, though only to a local minimum. Note the fine print: the guarantee is tied to matching the update to the distance. Mean with squared L2, median with L1.

3. Soft K-means: assignments by degree

Look again at what the assignment step throws away. A point sitting exactly between two centroids is 50/50 by any reasonable account, and K-means rounds it to 100/0. All the information in "how close was the vote" is destroyed before the update step ever sees it. Soft K-means keeps it: instead of hard 0/1 assignments, give each point a graded responsibility to every cluster, 0.7 here and 0.3 there, so the update step can use more of what the data actually says. Only the assignment step changes:

$$ r_k^{(n)} = \frac{\exp\!\big[-\beta\, d(m_k, x^{(n)})\big]}{\sum_j \exp\!\big[-\beta\, d(m_j, x^{(n)})\big]}. $$

You have met this function before. It is the softmax from the logistic-regression lecture, applied to negative distances: near centroids get scores close to the top, far centroids get exponentially little, and the whole thing normalizes to 1 over clusters. The new hyperparameter $\beta$ is called the stiffness, and it plays exactly the role an inverse temperature plays in a softmax: it sets how decisive the assignment is. The update step does not change at all, it is the same weighted mean $m_k = \frac{\sum_n r_k^{(n)} x^{(n)}}{\sum_n r_k^{(n)}}$, except the weights are now fractions, so every point tugs every centroid a little, in proportion to its responsibility.

Fig 3.1, the stiffness dial, this lesson's softmax made visible. Every point is painted with the live blend of the three cluster colours weighted by its responsibilities, so a 50/30/20 point is visibly a mix of orange, green, and purple. Slide $\beta$ down and the whole plane washes toward an even blend; slide it up and the colours snap crisp, hard K-means recovered as the limit. Click any point to read its actual responsibility numbers as bars, and press Iterate to run real soft update steps at the current stiffness.

Worked by hand: one soft responsibility

Take the worked example's point $x^{(2)} = (2, 1)$ with the converged centroids $m_1 = (1.5, 1)$, $m_2 = (4.5, 3.5)$, and stiffness $\beta = 0.2$. Squared distances: $d_1 = 0.25$, $d_2 = 12.5$.

exp(-0.2 · 0.25) = 0.95123   exp(-0.2 · 12.5) = 0.08208
r₁ = 0.95123 / (0.95123 + 0.08208) = 0.9206   r₂ = 0.0794

A 92/8 split: mostly cluster 1, but cluster 2 still gets a real tug from this point in the update step. Crank $\beta$ to 2 and the same arithmetic gives $r_1 = 0.999998$, hard assignment in all but name. That is the $\beta \to \infty$ limit happening numerically.

Why prefer this? Three benefits, all bought by the same smoothness: soft assignments are more robust to noise and outliers (a stray point no longer commandeers a centroid, it just leans on all of them a little), less sensitive to a poor choice of $K$ (superfluous centroids share territory instead of carving it up arbitrarily), and the cost surface being optimized is smoother, which makes the optimization better behaved. And in the limit $\beta \to \infty$ the softmax saturates to an argmax and soft K-means becomes hard K-means exactly, so nothing was lost, K-means is now the stiffest member of a family.

But softening exposes questions the algorithm cannot answer about itself. How should $\beta$ be set? Does the neat convergence guarantee survive the softening? And what about clusters that are elongated, or wider in one direction than another, when our distance function treats every direction and every cluster identically? Soft K-means has no principled reply, because it was built by tweaking a procedure rather than by stating a model. Those open questions are precisely what pushes us to the generative reformulation in the next section, where every one of them gets an answer for free. For completeness, the clustering toolbox holds more than these two: hierarchical clustering builds a tree of clusters and suits problems with genuine hierarchy or an unknown cluster count, and DBSCAN finds arbitrarily shaped clusters, tolerates noise, and scales well. This lecture stays on the K-means-to-mixtures road.

4. A generative view of clustering

Step back and sort what we have. Hard clustering gives non-overlapping clusters: a point belongs to a cluster or it does not, full stop. Soft clustering lets a point belong to several clusters by degree. Section 3 produced soft degrees by tweaking a formula, but there is a probabilistically grounded way to get them: a mixture model, in which each cluster is itself a little generative model of data, a Gaussian for continuous features or a multinomial for counts, and the degrees of membership fall out as honest probabilities. The algorithm that learns such models, EM, works by a kind of disciplined imagination: pretend the data really was produced by the model, then adjust the parameters to maximize the probability of what we observed.

To see why a special algorithm is needed at all, take the simplest possible case: 1-D data, $K = 2$ Gaussian clusters. Suppose first that a benevolent oracle told us each point's source, $t^{(n)} \in \{1, 2\}$. Then fitting is a solved problem from the Gaussian Bayes lecture, plain MLE per group:

$$ \hat{\mu}_k = \frac{\sum_n \mathbb{I}[t^{(n)} = k]\, x^{(n)}}{\sum_n \mathbb{I}[t^{(n)} = k]}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}_k^2 = \frac{\sum_n \mathbb{I}[t^{(n)} = k]\,\big(x^{(n)} - \hat{\mu}_k\big)^2}{\sum_n \mathbb{I}[t^{(n)} = k]}. $$

Now suppose instead the oracle told us the parameters $(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$ but hid the sources. Also solved: infer each point's source with the Bayes posterior,

$$ p(C = k \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid C = k)\, p(C = k)}{p(x)}, $$

the very rule that powered the Gaussian Bayes classifier. So: labels would give us parameters, and parameters would give us labels. We have neither. This is the chicken-and-egg at the heart of clustering, and it should feel familiar, because K-means has the same two-sided structure: assignments need centroids, centroids need assignments. EM resolves it the same way K-means did, by refusing to wait for the oracle: start with random Gaussians, infer soft sources from the current parameters (by posterior), refit the parameters from the current sources, and repeat.

Left: "if you knew the labels", press Fit and each colour group gets its Gaussian fitted by the closed-form MLE, mean and variance per group, drawn as a curve. Right: "if you knew the Gaussians", press Colour and every grey point is shaded by its Bayes posterior under the two given curves. Before we can run that loop properly we need to write down the model it is fitting, and that means being precise about mixtures of Gaussians.

5. Gaussian mixture models

Recall the multivariate Gaussian from the Gaussian Bayes lecture:

$$ \mathcal{N}(x;\, \mu, \Sigma) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d/2}\, |\Sigma|^{1/2}} \exp\!\Big[-\tfrac{1}{2}(x - \mu)^\top \Sigma^{-1} (x - \mu)\Big], $$

one bump, centred at $\mu$, with shape and tilt set by the covariance $\Sigma$. One bump cannot describe clumpy data. So take a weighted sum of bumps, a mixture of Gaussians (MoG):

$$ p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x;\, \mu_k, \Sigma_k), \qquad \sum_k \pi_k = 1, \quad 0 \le \pi_k \le 1. $$

The weights $\pi_k$ are the mixing coefficients: how much of the data each component accounts for. Why must they sum to 1 and stay in $[0, 1]$? Because $p(x)$ has to be a proper probability density, and a convex combination of densities is the only weighted sum that integrates to 1. Feel what the knobs do before we fit anything:

Fig 5.1, the mixture density builder. In 1-D, drag the sliders for $\pi_1$, the means, and the spreads: the dashed curves are the weighted components $\pi_k \mathcal{N}$ and the solid curve is their sum, recomputed live, with $\pi_2 = 1 - \pi_1$ enforced so the mixture always integrates to 1. Two bumps merge into one broad hill as the means approach, exactly how mixtures build bumpy densities out of simple parts. Switch to 2-D and drag the component means themselves; the shading is the live mixture density and the ellipses are the components' 1-sigma and 2-sigma contours.

A GMM, then, is this density with all parameters collected as $\Theta = \{\pi_k, \mu_k, \Sigma_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$, learned from data. The payoff for the extra machinery is expressiveness: with enough components a mixture can approximate essentially any density, and this holds even for diagonal-covariance components, GMMs are universal density approximators. So write down the maximum-likelihood objective and fit it, like we fit everything else in this course. The log-likelihood of the data is

$$ \ell(\Theta) = \sum_{n=1}^{N} \ln\!\left( \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}\!\big(x^{(n)};\, \mu_k, \Sigma_k\big) \right). $$

And here direct optimization jams, for three reasons worth naming. First, that sum inside the log: for a single Gaussian the log reaches the exponential and everything collapses into quadratics with a closed-form maximum, but a log of a sum simplifies into nothing, and setting gradients to zero yields equations with no closed-form solution. Second, the constraint $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$ must be respected by any update. Third, identifiability: relabel the components, swap the names of cluster 1 and cluster 2, and the density is unchanged, so solutions come in $K!$ identical copies and the landscape is riddled with symmetric optima. We need a better handle on the problem, and the handle is a variable that does not exist in the dataset.

Check your understanding

A colleague proposes the model $p(x) = 2\,\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_1, \Sigma_1) + 3\,\mathcal{N}(x; \mu_2, \Sigma_2)$, arguing that the fitting procedure can absorb any scaling. What is wrong?

Positivity is necessary but not sufficient. Integrate the proposal over all of space: each Gaussian contributes its weight, so the total mass is $2 + 3 = 5$, not 1.
Unequal mixing weights are fine and common; a 0.9/0.1 mixture is a perfectly good density. The constraint is on the sum, not on equality.
Right. A convex combination of densities is the only weighted sum that stays a density, which is exactly why the mixing coefficients are constrained, and why the M-step's $\pi_k = N_k / N$ automatically lands on the constraint: effective counts over the total always sum to 1.

6. Latent variables and EM

The missing variable

Ask where the mixture density comes from, as a story about how each point was generated. First, nature rolls a $K$-sided die weighted by the mixing coefficients and picks a component, $z \sim \text{Categorical}(\pi)$. Then, nature draws the point from that component's Gaussian. The variable $z$ is a latent variable: it exists in the story, one per point, but it is unobserved, the dataset only records $x$. Two identities you already trust, marginalization $p(x) = \sum_z p(x, z)$ and factorization $p(x, z) = p(x \mid z)\, p(z)$, recover the mixture exactly:

$$ p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} p(x, z = k) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \underbrace{p(z = k)}_{\pi_k}\, \underbrace{p(x \mid z = k)}_{\mathcal{N}(x;\, \mu_k, \Sigma_k)}. $$

So the awkward sum-inside-a-log is what a clean two-stage story looks like after its first stage has been erased. Watch the story run forward:

Fig 6.1, the latent variable made visible. Each press of "Sample a point" runs the generative story live: a categorical draw over the $\pi_k$ bar picks the component $z$ (watch it light up), then a draw from that component's Gaussian places the point, coloured by its $z$. Sample a hundred and the mixture density materializes out of dots. Then flip "Observe only x", the colours vanish, and you are looking at exactly what a real dataset gives us: the points, with every $z$ erased. Clustering is the art of guessing the colours back.

Why does inventing $z$ help? Because if the oracle handed us every $z^{(n)}$, maximum likelihood would crack open into the closed forms we already own from the Gaussian Bayes lecture: fit each $\mu_k$ and $\Sigma_k$ from the points labelled $k$, and set $\pi_k = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n \mathbb{I}[z^{(n)} = k]$, the fraction of points in component $k$, just like a class prior. We do not have the $z^{(n)}$. But section 4 taught us what to do when we lack labels and lack parameters: alternate guessing one from the other. Done with probabilities, that alternation is the expectation-maximization algorithm.

EM for a Gaussian mixture

Initialize $\mu_k, \Sigma_k, \pi_k$ somehow (random, or from a K-means run). Then iterate two steps.

E-step. With parameters frozen, compute each point's soft membership: the posterior probability that component $k$ generated point $n$, called the responsibility,

$$ \gamma_k^{(n)} = p\big(z^{(n)} = k \mid x^{(n)}\big) = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}\!\big(x^{(n)};\, \mu_k, \Sigma_k\big)} {\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}\!\big(x^{(n)};\, \mu_j, \Sigma_j\big)}. $$

Look at the shape of that formula: a set of nonnegative scores, one per component, normalized by their sum. It is Bayes' rule, and it is also functionally a softmax over components, the same move soft K-means made, except the scores now come from an actual probability model, prior times likelihood, instead of an invented $e^{-\beta d}$.

M-step. With responsibilities frozen, re-estimate the parameters. Define the effective count $N_k = \sum_n \gamma_k^{(n)}$, the number of points component $k$ is responsible for, fractional points included. Then

$$ \mu_k = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_n \gamma_k^{(n)} x^{(n)}, \qquad \Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_n \gamma_k^{(n)} \big(x^{(n)} - \mu_k\big)\big(x^{(n)} - \mu_k\big)^\top, \qquad \pi_k = \frac{N_k}{N}. $$

Recognize all three. They are the Gaussian Bayes closed-form MLEs with the hard indicator $\mathbb{I}[z^{(n)} = k]$ replaced by the soft $\gamma_k^{(n)}$. The new $\mu_k$ is the centre of gravity of the data the component is responsible for, exactly K-means' update weighted by posterior responsibility instead of hard membership. And notice the honest caveat: this is not a true closed form, because the $\gamma_k^{(n)}$ on the right-hand side were computed from the previous parameters in the E-step. Each M-step is exact only given the current responsibilities, which is why the two steps must iterate rather than solve the problem in one shot. Finally, after each iteration, evaluate the log-likelihood $\ell(\Theta)$ and stop when it changes only marginally. Now watch the whole thing run:

Worked by hand: one E-step and one M-step in 1-D

Data $\{0, 1, 4, 5\}$, two components, current guesses $\pi_1 = \pi_2 = 0.5$, $\mu_1 = 0$, $\mu_2 = 4$, $\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = 1$.

E-step for $x = 1$: the densities are $\mathcal{N}(1; 0, 1) = 0.2420$ and $\mathcal{N}(1; 4, 1) = 0.0044$, so

γ₁ = (0.5 · 0.2420) / (0.5 · 0.2420 + 0.5 · 0.0044) = 0.982

Repeating for all four points gives component 1 the responsibilities $(0.9997,\ 0.982,\ 0.0003,\ 0.0000)$.

M-step for component 1: effective count $N_1 = 0.9997 + 0.982 + 0.0003 + 0.0000 = 1.982$, and

μ₁ = (0.9997·0 + 0.982·1 + 0.0003·4 + 0·5) / 1.982 = 0.496
σ₁² = 0.252   π₁ = 1.982 / 4 = 0.4955

The mean moved from the guess 0 toward the true centre of the left pair $\{0, 1\}$, the variance shrank to match that pair's spread, and the mixing weight settled to roughly half, all from one iteration. Note where the numbers came from: the 0.982 in the numerator was manufactured by the E-step, which is exactly why the M-step is not a standalone closed form.

One EM step in code, faithful to the formulas: 

def gmm_em_step(X, pis, mus, Sigmas):
    N, K = len(X), len(pis)
    # E-step: responsibilities, one softmax-like row per point
    G = np.zeros((N, K))
    for k in range(K):
        G[:, k] = pis[k] * mvn_pdf(X, mus[k], Sigmas[k])
    G = G / G.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)      # rows sum to 1
    # M-step: weighted Gaussian MLEs (Gaussian Bayes forms, soft counts)
    Nk = G.sum(axis=0)                        # effective counts
    for k in range(K):
        mus[k] = (G[:, k:k+1] * X).sum(axis=0) / Nk[k]
        D = X - mus[k]
        Sigmas[k] = (G[:, k:k+1] * D).T @ D / Nk[k]
    pis = Nk / N
    ll = np.log(sum(p * mvn_pdf(X, m, S)      # log-likelihood, to monitor
         for p, m, S in zip(pis, mus, Sigmas))).sum()
    return pis, mus, Sigmas, G, ll

In gmm_em_step, suppose the E-step had filled G[:, k] with the unnormalized scores pis[k] * mvn_pdf(X, mus[k], Sigmas[k]). Which line turns the scores into valid responsibilities?

Column normalization would make each component's responsibilities sum to 1 across the dataset, which is not the constraint. The E-step is a posterior over components for each point, so the denominator in $\gamma_k^{(n)}$ sums over $j$, the components, not over $n$.
Right. Each row of G is one point's $\pi_k \mathcal{N}(x^{(n)}; \mu_k, \Sigma_k)$ scores, and dividing by the row sum is exactly the denominator $\sum_j \pi_j \mathcal{N}(x^{(n)}; \mu_j, \Sigma_j)$ of Bayes' rule. Rows sum to 1: one posterior per point.
That is the K-means assignment step in disguise: an argmax. EM's whole advantage is keeping the memberships soft so every component feels a graded pull from every point.

Check: in what sense is the M-step "not a true closed form"?

The M-step formulas look exactly as closed-form as the Gaussian Bayes MLEs they mimic. What is the catch?

Answer. A genuine closed form maps the raw data straight to the maximum-likelihood parameters, once, done, like $\hat{\mu} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_n x^{(n)}$ for a single Gaussian. The M-step's right-hand sides contain $\gamma_k^{(n)}$, and those responsibilities are not data: they were computed in the E-step from the previous iteration's parameters. So the M-step is only exact conditional on the current soft assignments; change the parameters and the responsibilities that justified them are stale, which is why the E-step must run again. The pair of steps is a fixed-point iteration on the likelihood, each E-step and M-step improving $\ell(\Theta)$ or leaving it unchanged, and the climbing curve in Fig 6.2 is that guarantee measured live. The K-means analogy is exact: the update step there was also a perfect solve, but only for the current assignments.

7. MoG vs K-means

Two loops, one alternating skeleton. Time to set them side by side and be precise about what changed. What they share: both are sensitive to the starting point and converge only to a local optimum, so multiple restarts help both; and neither can discover $K$, K-means because inertia falls to 0 at $K = N$, and MoG because the likelihood grows without bound as $K$ grows (in the limit, park a component with shrinking variance on every data point and the density spikes explode). Where they differ: K-means stops when the assignments stop changing, a discrete event, while MoG stops when the change in the data probability $p(\{x^{(n)}\})$ is small, a continuous one. And most importantly, K-means measures every cluster with the same fixed distance, while MoG effectively measures with a Mahalanobis distance $(x - \mu_k)^\top \Sigma_k^{-1} (x - \mu_k)$ induced by each component's covariance, a per-cluster, direction-aware ruler that is itself re-learned at every M-step. That is the answer to soft K-means' open question about elongated and unequal-width clusters: let each cluster carry its own metric.

Fig 7.1, the same stretched, tilted clusters handed to both algorithms, run live to convergence. K-means (left) is stuck drawing spherical territories with one fixed ruler, so it slices diagonally through the elongated clumps. The GMM (right) learns each component's covariance, its ellipses tilt to match the data, and the soft colouring follows the true structure. Now flip the constraint toggle: with $\Sigma_k = I$ frozen and mixing weights held uniform, the GMM's E-step reduces to a softmax over plain squared distances and its clustering collapses onto soft K-means, same boundaries, same failure. The covariances were the entire advantage.

The collapse in that toggle is a theorem, not a coincidence. Fix every covariance to the identity, $\Sigma_k = I$, and hold the mixing weights uniform: then $\pi_k \mathcal{N}(x; \mu_k, I)$ is a constant times $\exp[-\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert x - \mu_k \rVert^2]$, the E-step responsibility becomes a softmax over squared distances with stiffness $\beta = \tfrac{1}{2}$, and the M-step becomes the responsibility-weighted mean. That is soft K-means, line for line. Soft K-means was a mixture model all along, one that had quietly assumed round, equal-size, equally likely clusters; MoG is what it looks like when those assumptions are made explicit and then relaxed.

One more thing MoG bought us that no K-means variant can match: it is a density. The fitted $p(x)$ can be evaluated and sampled. Sampling is the generative story run forward, draw $z \sim \text{Categorical}(\pi)$, then draw $x \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_z, \Sigma_z)$, which is how Fig 0.1 conjured new points, and how Assignment 4 has you generate new face images from a GMM fitted in PCA space. Evaluation runs the other way: a point's log-likelihood $\ln p(x)$ under the fitted mixture scores how well the model explains it, and points with unusually low log-likelihood are anomalies. Clustering, generation, and anomaly detection, one model, three services.

8. Summary and method card

One skeleton carried the whole lecture: guess the assignments from the parameters, refit the parameters from the assignments, repeat. K-means is that skeleton at its most austere, hard assignments, centroids only, block coordinate descent on inertia, convergence guaranteed because both half-steps only ever lower $J$. Soft K-means replaced the argmax with a softmax and exposed the questions a procedure cannot answer about itself. The generative view answered them: model each cluster as a Gaussian, weight them with mixing coefficients, and the awkward log-of-a-sum likelihood untangles the moment you name the latent variable $z$. EM is the skeleton with probabilities installed, E-step posteriors playing the role of assignments, M-step weighted Gaussian MLEs playing the role of updates, log-likelihood climbing monotonically in place of inertia falling. And the prize for the upgrade is a genuine density: sample it, score it, ship it.

Method card · Clustering: K-Means & Gaussian Mixtures
AssumptionThe data contains $K$ latent groups. K-means assumes Euclidean, roughly spherical clusters; a GMM assumes one Gaussian component per group.
Model classK-means: $K$ centroids with hard assignments. GMM: $K$ Gaussian components with mixing weights $\pi_k$ and soft responsibilities.
Learning / selectionAlternate assignment and update steps: block coordinate descent on within-cluster squared distance for K-means, EM on the log-likelihood for GMMs. Treat $K$ and the initialization as hyperparameters (elbow curves, restarts).
PredictionK-means: nearest centroid. GMM: posterior responsibilities $\gamma_k$ for membership, $p(x)$ for density, ancestral sampling for generation.
StrengthsSimple, interpretable, scalable. GMMs additionally represent uncertainty, overlapping clusters, unequal scales, and correlated features, and provide a usable density.
LimitationsSensitive to feature scaling, initialization, and the choice of $K$; both converge to local optima. K-means struggles with irregular cluster shapes; GMMs assume Gaussianity and risk singular covariances when a component collapses onto few points.
Next stepFrom discovering latent structure in static data to learning sequential decisions: bandits and Markov decision processes, next lecture.

Where this sits in the course story: the unsupervised thread is now complete. PCA found directions, autoencoders found codes, and this lecture found groups and densities, each one a different answer to "summarize the data without labels". What none of these methods do is act: they describe the world but never touch it. The final stretch of the course changes that. Reinforcement learning is what happens when the model's outputs become decisions, the decisions have consequences, and the only feedback is a reward signal arriving late. Quiz 1

In one sentence each, what do the E-step and the M-step of EM for a GMM compute?

EM never computes a gradient; that is what makes it attractive. Both steps are exact solves of subproblems, inference of memberships and weighted MLE, with the likelihood monitored on the side.
Right. Infer soft memberships from parameters, refit parameters from soft memberships, and the log-likelihood never decreases along the way.
Hard assignments would make it K-means. The E in EM is an expectation precisely because memberships stay soft: every component keeps a fractional claim on every point.
Quiz 2

An inertia-vs-$K$ curve reads $J = 410, 195, 80, 71, 64, 59$ for $K = 1 \dots 6$. Which $K$ does the elbow method suggest, and why not simply take $K = 6$, which has the lowest $J$?

Right. The elbow is where the marginal centroid stops discovering clumps and starts subdividing them; here that is unmistakably $K = 3$. Chasing minimum $J$ ends at the degenerate $K = N$, one centroid per point, $J = 0$, and nothing learned.
Then $K = N$ would be best of all, with $J$ exactly 0 and every point its own cluster. Inertia comparisons are only meaningful at fixed $K$.
There is no tie to break: going from 1 to 2 and 2 to 3 buys enormous reductions, which is the signature of real structure being found. Simplicity arguments start at the elbow, not before it.
Quiz 3 (transfer): where K-means fails and a GMM succeeds

A biologist hands you gene-expression data whose two cell populations form long, tilted, partially overlapping streaks in feature space. K-means with $K = 2$ returns a split that cuts both streaks in half crosswise. Why does it fail here, and what specifically does a GMM change?

Answer. K-means scores every candidate assignment with one fixed, isotropic ruler: plain squared Euclidean distance. Its decision boundary between two centroids is therefore always the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining them, and its implied cluster shape is a sphere. Long tilted streaks violate that shape badly: points at the far end of streak A are Euclidean-closer to streak B's centroid than to their own, so the bisector slices across both streaks, exactly what the biologist saw. A GMM replaces the shared ruler with a learned, per-cluster Mahalanobis distance $(x - \mu_k)^\top \Sigma_k^{-1}(x - \mu_k)$: each component's covariance stretches along its streak, so distance is measured cheaply along the streak direction and expensively across it, and the fitted ellipses align with the populations, Fig 7.1's left-versus-right panel exactly. The overlap is handled too: points in the shared region get honest intermediate responsibilities instead of an arbitrary hard split. The cost is more parameters per cluster (a full covariance) and the Gaussianity assumption; if the streaks were curved bananas rather than straight ellipses, both methods would struggle and something like DBSCAN would deserve a look.

Quiz 4 (transfer): the M-step is an old friend

Write the connection precisely: how do the EM M-step formulas relate to the closed-form estimates from the Gaussian Bayes lecture, and what plays the role of the class labels?

Answer. The Gaussian Bayes classifier was trained on labelled data by per-class MLE: $\hat{\mu}_c$ is the mean of class $c$'s points, $\hat{\Sigma}_c$ their covariance, and the prior $\hat{p}(c)$ the fraction of points in class $c$. Each is a sum over the data weighted by the hard indicator $\mathbb{I}[t^{(n)} = c]$, divided by the count $\sum_n \mathbb{I}[t^{(n)} = c]$. The M-step is the same three formulas with every indicator replaced by the responsibility $\gamma_k^{(n)}$ and the count by the effective count $N_k = \sum_n \gamma_k^{(n)}$: labels have become posterior probabilities of labels. So the role of the class labels is played by the E-step's soft guesses at the latent $z^{(n)}$, and if the responsibilities ever became one-hot, the M-step would reduce to the Gaussian Bayes estimator verbatim. That is the deep symmetry: a GMM is a Gaussian Bayes model whose labels went missing, and EM is how you train it anyway, substituting expected labels for observed ones. It also explains the caveat from section 6: because the "labels" are manufactured from the previous parameters, the M-step is exact only per-iteration, not a one-shot closed form.

Quiz 5 (transfer): why neither method can discover K

Both K-means and the GMM require $K$ as an input. Explain, using each method's own objective, why neither can select $K$ by optimizing that objective, and what practitioners do instead.

Answer. Both objectives are rigged in the same direction: more components never hurt the training criterion. For K-means, adding a centroid weakly decreases the optimal inertia, and at $K = N$ every point owns a private centroid and $J$ reaches its global minimum of exactly 0, a "perfect" score for a clustering that says nothing. For the GMM, the training log-likelihood grows without bound as $K$ grows: extra components can shadow existing ones for free, and worse, a component can centre itself on a single data point and shrink its variance toward zero, sending its density spike, and the likelihood, to infinity. So optimizing the objective over $K$ selects degenerate models in both worlds; the objectives measure fit, and fit alone always votes for more capacity, the same overfitting logic this course met with polynomial degree back in the regularization lecture. In practice: for K-means, sweep $K$, plot the inertia curve, and take the elbow where returns collapse (Fig 2.3). For mixtures, the analogous moves are elbow-style inspection of held-out likelihood or penalized criteria that charge for parameters. Either way, $K$ is a hyperparameter, chosen outside the training objective, exactly like every other capacity knob in the course.

That is clustering and density estimation. One alternating loop, dressed twice: centroids and hard assignments when you want speed and simplicity, Gaussians and posteriors when you want shapes, uncertainty, and a density you can sample. The chicken-and-egg that looked like a paradox in section 4 turned out to be an algorithm design, break the circle with a guess and let two exact half-solves take turns improving it. Hold onto that instinct of alternating between inferring what you cannot see and refitting what you can.
