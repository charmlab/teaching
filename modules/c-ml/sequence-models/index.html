Sequence Models

The language models we built last lecture share one embarrassing flaw: they read through a fixed window, so anything that happened more than a couple of words ago simply does not exist for them. This lecture builds memory. A recurrent network carries a hidden state forward through time, one update rule reused at every step, and that single idea unlocks sequences of any length. It also creates the deepest training problem in this course, gradients that vanish or explode across time, and the fix, gated cells like the LSTM, is one of the great architectural ideas in machine learning. Every concept here has an animation and an interactive figure, and the whole story ends one step short of attention.

0. The hook: the model that forgot the subject

Start from where the language-modelling lecture left us. A language model factorizes the probability of a sentence with the chain rule,

$$ p(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(w_t \mid w_{<t}), $$

and both of our working models, the counted n-gram and the neural trigram, made the same approximation to survive: condition on a fixed window of the most recent words instead of the whole history. A trigram sees exactly two words back. That bought us tractable tables and a tractable neural network, and the price was quietly enormous. Any relevant information that falls outside the window is simply gone.

Feel how fast that fails. Take the lecture's sentence: "Amir went to class and then he returned home." To continue the sentence sensibly after "he", a model has to know who "he" is, and the answer, Amir, sits six words back. Slide the window and watch the subject fall off the edge:

Fig 0.1, the fixed-window failure. The model predicts the word after "he" while seeing only the last $k$ words (orange). At the trigram setting, $k = 2$, the subject "Amir" (purple) is invisible, and no amount of training fixes what the model literally cannot see. Only at $k \ge 7$ does the subject come back into view.

You could keep growing the window, but the language-modelling lecture already showed why that road ends: n-gram tables blow up exponentially in the window length, and even the neural trigram's input layer grows with every extra context word while still imposing a hard cutoff. Language does not respect cutoffs. The subject of a sentence, the topic of a paragraph, the name introduced a page ago: useful context can sit arbitrarily far back. So the plan for this lecture is different in kind, not degree. Instead of a wider snapshot, we give the model memory: a running summary that is carried forward and updated word by word, so that in principle nothing ever has to fall off the edge.

1. The RNN: a network with memory

A recurrent neural network (RNN) reads a sequence one step at a time and maintains a hidden state $h_t$, a vector that acts as a learned running summary of everything the network has read so far. At each step, the new summary is computed from the old summary and the new input:

$$ h_t = f(h_{t-1},\, x_t). $$

Read that equation the way you would read a habit. The network does not get to see the whole sequence at once; it gets the one thing it wrote down about the past, $h_{t-1}$, plus the current input $x_t$, and it must produce the next note-to-self $h_t$. Everything the model will ever know about word one, by the time it reaches word fifty, has to survive inside that vector.

The second half of the definition matters just as much: the same parameters are reused at every time step. One update function $f$, one set of weights, applied at $t = 1$, at $t = 2$, at $t = 1000$. This is weight sharing across time, and it should feel familiar: the CNN lecture shared one filter across all image positions because a pattern is worth detecting anywhere. Here we share one update rule across all time steps because "how to fold a new word into a summary" should not depend on whether the word is the third or the three-hundredth. Sharing buys us two things at once: the parameter count is independent of sequence length, so one network handles sequences of any length, and every position's data teaches the same weights.

Because the network is a loop, a cell feeding its own output back into itself, we can also draw it unrolled: copy the cell once per time step and pass the hidden state along the chain. The loop and the chain are the same network, drawn twice. The unrolled drawing is the one to keep in your head, because it turns a recurrent network into an ordinary feedforward computation graph, which is exactly how we will train it in section 3.

Fig 1.1, the signature picture of the lesson. The folded view is the network as defined: one cell, one self-loop. Press Unroll and the loop opens into a chain across time; then step through the inputs and watch $h_t$ computed live from $h_{t-1}$ and $x_t$ with a real $\tanh$ update. Every arrow between cells carries the same weights $W_h$, printed once because there is only one copy.

To make "the hidden state is memory" concrete, strip the idea to its smallest possible example, straight from the lecture. Take a scalar hidden state, a scalar input, no activation function, and one shared weight $w$:

$$ h_{t+1} = w \cdot x_{t+1} + w \cdot h_t. $$

Set $w = 1$ and unroll it by hand: $h_1 = x_1$, then $h_2 = x_2 + h_1 = x_1 + x_2$, then $h_3 = x_1 + x_2 + x_3$. The hidden state is computing a running sum of the input. Nothing about "the first number" was stored explicitly anywhere; its influence simply lives on inside $h$, which is the whole trick. Feed it a sequence yourself:

Fig 1.2, the running-summary demo. Step through a number sequence and watch $h_t$ accumulate under $h_{t+1} = w x_{t+1} + w h_t$; at $w = 1$ the readout confirms $h_t$ equals the running sum exactly. Then nudge $w$ off 1 and notice old inputs fading ($w < 1$) or swelling ($w > 1$): remember that behaviour, section 3 is built on it.

The real cell is the same idea with a nonlinearity and learned weight matrices. The vanilla RNN update we will carry through this lecture is

$$ h_t = \sigma\!\left(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b\right), $$

where $W_h$ mixes the old summary, $W_x$ injects the new input, and $\sigma$ is a squashing activation. Let us run it by hand once, scalar case, so the numbers are yours. Take $W_h = 0.5$, $W_x = 1.0$, $b = 0$, $h_0 = 0$, with $\sigma$ the logistic function, and feed $x_1 = 1.0$, $x_2 = 0.5$:

Worked by hand: two steps of the vanilla RNN

z₁ = 0.5·0 + 1.0·1.0     = 1.0000   →  h₁ = σ(1.0000) = 0.7311
z₂ = 0.5·0.7311 + 1.0·0.5 = 0.8655   →  h₂ = σ(0.8655) = 0.7038

Notice the mechanics: $x_1$ never appears in the second line, yet it shaped $z_2$ through the $0.5 \cdot 0.7311$ term. The first input's influence arrived at step 2 only by surviving inside $h_1$, already scaled down by $W_h = 0.5$. File that factor away; it is about to become the villain of section 3.

In code, the update is four lines, and the loop over a sequence is two more: 

import math

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-z))

def rnn_step(h, x, Wh, Wx, b):
    z = Wh * h + Wx * x + b       # mix old summary with new input
    return sigmoid(z)             # squash into the next summary

h = 0.0                           # empty memory
for x in [1.0, 0.5, -0.3, 0.9]:
    h = rnn_step(h, x, Wh=0.5, Wx=1.0, b=0.0)

What does the hidden state $h_t$ represent, and why does the RNN reuse the same weights at every time step?

The hidden state is a fixed-size vector, so it cannot store the raw sequence; it compresses the past into a summary. And sharing is not a memory optimization, it is what makes one rule apply to sequences of any length.
Right on both counts. $h_t$ is the model's note-to-self about the past, and one shared $f$ means "how to update a summary" is learned once, from every position in every sequence, and then applies at any length. Same logic as sharing a CNN filter across image positions.
The prediction is computed from $h_t$ (next section), but the state itself is a summary of the history, not a distribution. And token probabilities are certainly not all equal.

2. An RNN as a language model

Now plug the recurrent cell into the job from last lecture. As a language model, an RNN computes, at every step,

$$ \text{current token} + \text{memory} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{next-token probabilities}. $$

The plumbing is exactly the pipeline we already own. Tokens go in as IDs, one-hot vectors, or embeddings; the cell folds each one into the hidden state; and a linear layer plus softmax turns $h_t$ into a distribution over the vocabulary, trained with cross-entropy on the true next token. The only new part is that the context is no longer a fixed window: it is whatever the hidden state managed to remember, which in principle reaches back to the first word.

Training reuses the trick from last lecture, teacher forcing: at every step, feed the true previous token from the corpus, not whatever the model would have guessed, so each step is a clean supervised problem. Generation is the same loop with the wire bent back: sample or pick a token from the model's output distribution and feed that in as the next input. Same cell, different diet, and the difference matters, because during generation the model's own mistakes become its future context. Watch both modes on a genuinely trained network:

Fig 2.1, the RNN language-model stepper. A small RNN is actually trained in your browser on a toy corpus when this figure loads, so every probability is real. Step through a sentence: the hidden state (heat strip) carries forward, and the bars show the softmax over the vocabulary at each step. In teacher forcing the true token (green) is fed next regardless of the model's favourite; in free generation the model's own pick (orange) is fed back, and the sentence becomes whatever it becomes.

One design question remains: what should a "token" be? Last lecture used words, and words come with two costs. A realistic vocabulary has tens of thousands of entries, so the softmax and the embedding table are large and training and prediction get expensive; and any word not in the vocabulary at training time is simply unrepresentable. Character-level modelling fixes both at a stroke: the vocabulary shrinks to roughly a hundred characters, and any word whatsoever can be spelled. The catch is the exchange rate. A ten-word sentence becomes fifty-odd characters, so the dependency between "Amir" and "he" that sat six tokens apart at word level now sits dozens of steps apart, and long-term memory stops being a luxury and becomes the whole game. Character-level RNNs live and die by how far their hidden state can carry information, and they struggle as sequences get long. Which raises the question this lecture actually turns on: how far can a vanilla RNN carry information?

Check your understanding

During training, an RNN language model is fed the true previous token rather than its own prediction. Why?

The model produces a distribution at every step from the very first update; we could sample from it mid-training if we wanted. We choose not to.
Weights are not corrupted by inputs; they change only by gradient updates. The real reason is about making the training signal clean, not about protecting the weights.
Right. With the true history as context, "predict the next token" is ordinary supervised learning at every position. Generation then changes the diet: the model conditions on its own outputs, which is exactly why generated text can drift in ways training text never did.

3. The bottleneck: backprop through time and unstable gradients

How do we train the weights of a recurrent cell? With the algorithm we already trust. Unroll the network across time, as in Fig 1.1, and the result is a plain feedforward computation graph: the same kind of graph the backpropagation lecture taught us to differentiate node by node. Running backprop on the unrolled network has its own name, backpropagation through time (BPTT), but there is nothing new in it. Same chain rule, same local derivatives multiplied along paths, with one twist: because the weights are shared across time, every unrolled copy of the cell contributes gradient to the same $W_h$.

The twist is not the problem. The problem is the shape of the graph. Suppose the loss at a late step $T$ depends on something that happened at an early step $t$, the "Amir" that explains the "he". The gradient connecting them must travel backward through every intermediate hidden state, and the chain rule makes that a product:

$$ \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} = \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_{T-1}}\, \frac{\partial h_{T-1}}{\partial h_{T-2}} \cdots \frac{\partial h_{t+1}}{\partial h_t}. $$

For the vanilla cell $h_t = \sigma(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b)$, each factor is a Jacobian matrix, so a long-range gradient is a product of many Jacobians across time. To see what repeated multiplication does, drop to the scalar case, where each factor is just a number:

$$ \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} \approx \prod_{k=t+1}^{T} \sigma'(z_k)\, w. $$

If each backward step multiplies by roughly the same value $a$, then after $T$ steps the gradient has been scaled by $a^T$, and exponentials are merciless in both directions:

Worked: the two numbers that define this lecture

|a| < 1:  0.9⁵⁰ = 0.00515…  ≈ 0.005   (the gradient has vanished)
|a| > 1:  1.1⁵⁰ = 117.39…   ≈ 117     (the gradient has exploded)

A per-step factor just 10% below 1 wipes out the signal across 50 steps; a factor 10% above 1 amplifies it more than a hundredfold. Nothing in between is stable except $a$ pinned exactly near 1, which ordinary training does not arrange.

These are the vanishing gradient and exploding gradient problems, and it is worth translating both out of calculus. A vanished gradient means early inputs get essentially no credit or blame for late predictions: the learning signal from "he" never reaches "Amir", so long-range dependencies are precisely the thing the network cannot learn. An exploded gradient means the update step becomes huge and optimization goes unstable, loss spiking instead of falling. Both are fatal for language, because language depends on distant words. And the saturating activations we like for squashing make vanishing worse: wherever $\sigma$ flattens out, $\sigma'(z_k)$ is close to zero, shrinking the product further. Gradient clipping: the practical patch

Exploding gradients have a blunt, effective, widely used fix. If the gradient's norm exceeds a threshold $\eta$, rescale the whole vector back to that norm:

$$ \lVert g \rVert > \eta \;\Rightarrow\; g \leftarrow \eta\, \frac{g}{\lVert g \rVert}. $$

This is gradient clipping, and the geometry is the point: the clipped gradient keeps its direction exactly and changes only its magnitude. The optimizer still moves the same way, just not catastrophically far. Worked once by hand: take $g = (3, 4)$, so $\lVert g \rVert = 5$, with threshold $\eta = 2$. Then $g \leftarrow 2 \cdot (3, 4)/5 = (1.2, 1.6)$, whose norm is exactly 2 and whose direction is unchanged. In code: 

def clip_grad(g, eta):
    norm = math.sqrt(sum(gi * gi for gi in g))
    if norm > eta:                          # only intervene when too long
        g = [gi * eta / norm for gi in g]   # same direction, norm = eta
    return g

Fig 3.3, the clipping demo. Drag the gradient vector; whenever it leaves the threshold circle, the clipped version (orange) is computed by the rescale above: identical direction, norm pinned to $\eta$. Drag it back inside the circle and clipping does nothing at all. The readout confirms the angle never changes.

Be precise about what was just bought. Clipping caps how far a single update can move, so it tames exploding gradients. It does nothing for vanishing gradients: a signal that arrived at $10^{-8}$ is still $10^{-8}$ after clipping, because clipping only ever shrinks. You cannot rescue information that never arrived. Fixing vanishing needs a better memory architecture, not a better optimizer patch, and that is section 4.

4. Gated memory: LSTMs and GRUs

Step back and ask what the vanishing gradient is really telling us. In a vanilla RNN, written compactly as $h_t = \sigma(W[h_{t-1}, x_t] + b)$, every piece of remembered information is forced through the same squash-and-mix update at every single step. Long-range information survives only by luck, by the product of fifty derivatives happening to stay near 1. The diagnosis is architectural, so the cure should be too. And there is a second observation hiding in plain sight: not every past word deserves equal treatment. Some inputs should be remembered for a long time (the subject of the sentence), some should be discarded almost immediately (a filler word), and some parts of memory matter for the current prediction while others do not. A good memory is selective.

A gate is the mechanism that makes selectivity learnable: a value between 0 and 1, computed from the current input and state, that multiplies a piece of information to control how much of it passes. Gate at 1, the information flows untouched; gate at 0, it is erased; in between, it is attenuated. The LSTM (long short-term memory) cell is built from exactly three such gates wrapped around one new object, the cell state $C_t$: a protected memory lane, a conveyor belt that runs straight through time and lets information persist. The gates control all traffic on and off the belt:

  • the forget gate decides what past information to discard from the cell state,
  • the input gate decides what new information to store into it,
  • the output gate decides what part of the memory to expose through $h_t$ for the current prediction.

The key idea is controlled memory flow. The model no longer learns only the input-to-output relation at each step; it also learns when to remember and when to forget. Keep the forget gate open and the belt carries a value across dozens of steps essentially untouched, no repeated squashing, which is exactly the persistence a vanilla RNN could not offer. Drive the gates yourself; this is worth ten minutes of play:

Fig 4.1, the LSTM gate explorer. Left: one cell, live. The conveyor belt across the top carries $C_{t-1}$ through the forget gate's multiplier, picks up the input gate's contribution, and the output gate decides how much of $\tanh(C_t)$ escapes as $h_t$; every number recomputes as you slide. Right: persistence. A value stored once decays as $f^{\,t}$ under forget gate $f$, so at $f = 1$ it survives forever, and the presets show remembering, forgetting, and the sneaky one: memory kept but hidden ($o = 0$).

In schematic code, one LSTM step reads like the diagram (this is for structure, not for running; every framework ships a tuned implementation): 

def lstm_step(h, C, x):
    hx = concat(h, x)                     # gates read the state and the input
    f = sigmoid(Wf @ hx + bf)             # forget gate: keep how much old memory?
    i = sigmoid(Wi @ hx + bi)             # input gate: store how much new info?
    C_cand = tanh(Wc @ hx + bc)           # candidate: what new info to write
    C = f * C + i * C_cand                # the conveyor belt update
    o = sigmoid(Wo @ hx + bo)             # output gate: expose how much memory?
    h = o * tanh(C)                       # what the rest of the network sees
    return h, C

Look at the belt line, C = f * C + i * C_cand. When $f \approx 1$ and $i \approx 0$, it reduces to $C_t \approx C_{t-1}$: information is copied forward, not squashed forward, and that near-identity path is what lets memory (and the learning signal that travels along it) survive many steps. The GRU (gated recurrent unit) is a simpler gated variant built on the same principle with fewer gates and no separate cell state; the idea to retain is gated, controllable memory, not the exact plumbing.

Two pieces of history worth knowing, both from the lecture. First, LSTMs were proposed by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber in 1997 and then sat almost unused for about a decade before becoming central to the deep-learning era, powering speech recognition, machine translation, and sequence prediction; being right early is not the same as being adopted. (Schmidhuber is this lecture's scientist.) Second, the fine print: an LSTM still processes its input one step at a time. Gates fixed the memory problem, not the sequential one. Step 500 cannot be computed before step 499, which makes training slow to parallelize, and that surviving bottleneck is one of the reasons attention and transformers are about to matter.

Check: memory that persists but is not exposed

In Fig 4.1, set the output gate to 0 and leave the forget gate at 1. The hidden state is $h_t = 0$, so downstream layers see nothing. Has the cell forgotten?

Answer. No, and this is exactly why the LSTM separates $C_t$ from $h_t$. The cell state still carries its value along the belt ($C_t = f \cdot C_{t-1} = C_{t-1}$ when $f = 1$ and nothing new is written); the output gate only controls what is revealed this step, $h_t = o \cdot \tanh(C_t) = 0 \cdot \tanh(C_t)$. Reopen the gate ten steps later and the remembered value is still there to expose. Remembering and reporting are different decisions, and the LSTM lets the model learn them separately.

5. Chaining cells: bidirectional, stacked, seq2seq

A recurrent cell, vanilla or gated, is a building block, and blocks combine. Three combinations carry most of the field's classic systems.

Bidirectional. A forward RNN can only condition on the past, but sometimes the disambiguating context comes later: "Though he later went home, Amir went to..." is completed more confidently if the model can also read what follows. A bidirectional RNN runs a second cell over the sequence in reverse (the flipped direction) and combines both hidden states, so a position's representation sees the whole sentence. The obvious caveat: you need the future to be available, which is fine for analyzing complete text and impossible for generating it left to right.

Stacked. Cells also stack in depth: the first layer's hidden states become the second layer's input sequence. As with every deep architecture in this course, extra layers buy better feature extraction, and here the budget is small: usually just two or three layers, paid for in compute, since each layer is another full pass along the sequence.

Fig 5.1, what a position can see. Click any token: with a forward-only pass its representation depends on the prefix alone, tokens out of reach fade out, and the counter says so. Toggle the backward pass and the whole sentence comes into reach; toggle a second layer to stack cells in depth. One switch each, one line of what it buys.

Seq2seq. The combination that mattered most historically solves a problem we have quietly dodged: input and output lengths differ. A translation's source and target sentences do not line up token for token, so "predict the next token of the same stream" is not enough. The encoder-decoder (seq2seq) architecture splits the job: an encoder RNN reads the input sequence into hidden states $h_1, \dots, h_T$, and a decoder RNN generates the output sequence, token by token, the same way our language model generated text. The handoff between them is the catch. In the basic design, the decoder receives exactly one thing: the encoder's final hidden state,

$$ h_1, \dots, h_T \;\longrightarrow\; h_T \;\longrightarrow\; \text{decoder}. $$

One fixed-size vector, asked to summarize the entire input, however long. This is the seq2seq bottleneck, and you should feel how strange it is: we built recurrent memory precisely so that context would not be crushed into a fixed window, and here at the architecture's crowning moment we crush the whole input into a fixed vector anyway. Check: why is the final state a bottleneck, and what would help?

State the seq2seq bottleneck in one sentence, and suggest what the decoder would need to relieve it.

Answer. The bottleneck: a basic seq2seq model forces the entire input sequence, no matter its length, through the single fixed-size vector $h_T$, so a 5-word and a 50-word input get the same memory budget and long inputs are inevitably summarized lossily. What would help: give the decoder access to all the encoder states $h_1, \dots, h_T$, not just the last one, and let it choose which of them matter for the token it is currently producing. That "choose which past states matter right now" mechanism is attention, and it is the subject of the next lecture. Notice the shift in philosophy: instead of preserving everything in one vector, retrieve what you need when you need it.

6. Why attention comes next

Tally the ledger of this lecture. RNNs process one step at a time and carry information forward through a hidden state: memory beyond any fixed window, which is what we came for. LSTMs and GRUs add gates, so the memory becomes selective and can persist across long spans. But two costs never went away. Sequential processing is slow, because step $t$ waits on step $t-1$ no matter how many processors you own. And long-range dependencies, even gated, remain hard: information still has to survive the journey through every intermediate state, whether squashed by a vanilla update or ferried by a conveyor belt.

Attention starts from a different question. Not "how do I preserve everything that might matter?" but "which previous tokens are relevant right now?" If the model, at each step, could look back directly at any earlier state and pull in what it needs, then nothing has to survive a journey at all: the dependency between "he" and "Amir" becomes one direct lookup instead of six fragile hops. That reframes sequence modelling from memory preservation to memory retrieval. A 2017 paper took the idea to its logical extreme, dropped the recurrence entirely, and announced the result in its title: "Attention Is All You Need". The architecture it introduced, the Transformer, replaces recurrence with attention and is the subject of the next lecture, and of essentially every large language model you have ever used.

7. Summary and method card

One sentence per act. Fixed windows lose distant context, so we gave the model a hidden state, a learned running summary updated by one weight-shared rule at every step. As a language model, the RNN turns "current token plus memory" into next-token probabilities, trained with teacher forcing, run free for generation. Training by backprop through time revealed the flaw: long-range gradients are products like $a^T$, which vanish ($0.9^{50} \approx 0.005$) or explode ($1.1^{50} \approx 117$); clipping caps the explosions and cannot touch the vanishing. LSTMs answer architecturally with a cell state and forget, input, and output gates, learning when to remember and when to forget. Cells chain into bidirectional, stacked, and encoder-decoder designs, and the basic seq2seq handoff squeezes the whole input through one final state. The big idea of the lecture, stated once more: sequence models need mechanisms for memory. Recurrence stores context over time, gates make the storage selective, and attention, next lecture, replaces preservation with retrieval.

AssumptionLanguage depends on context, and useful context may appear far before the current word.
Model classNeural sequence models: RNNs with a hidden state updated by shared weights; gated cells (LSTMs, GRUs); encoder-decoder chains; attention and transformers next.
Learning / selectionTrain by predicting the next token or output sequence with cross-entropy (teacher forcing); evaluate with held-out likelihood, perplexity, or downstream tasks.
PredictionFold each token into the state, $h_t = f(h_{t-1}, x_t)$; read next-token probabilities from $h_t$ with softmax; decode step by step for generation.
StrengthsCaptures sequential context beyond fixed n-grams; one shared rule handles any length; gates enable long, selective memory; attention will add direct access to relevant tokens and parallel architectures.
LimitationsRNNs are sequential (slow to parallelize) and suffer vanishing/exploding gradients; the basic seq2seq handoff bottlenecks at one vector; transformers are powerful but expensive and data-hungry.
Key knobsCell type (vanilla, LSTM, GRU), hidden size, depth (stacking), directionality, clipping threshold $\eta$, token granularity (word vs character).

Where this sits in the course story: backpropagation gave us the training algorithm, CNNs showed that architecture should encode the structure of the data, and language modelling turned text into next-token prediction. This lecture built the architecture for sequences and found its limit honestly. Next lecture: transformers, BERT and GPT-style models, and the modern LLM pipeline built on top of them.

8. End-of-lesson quiz

Two quick checks, then three transfer questions that ask you to design and connect, not recall. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Quiz 1

A vanilla RNN and an LSTM both fail to learn a dependency spanning 80 tokens. You add gradient clipping to both. What improves?

Clipping caps overly long gradients; it cannot amplify tiny ones. An 80-step vanished signal stays vanished. The product $a^{80}$ is the problem, and clipping never changes $a$.
LSTMs are trained by the same BPTT and can absolutely explode; clipping applies to both. What clipping cannot do, for either, is fix vanishing.
Right. Clipping is an optimizer patch for explosions: direction kept, magnitude capped. Vanishing is an architecture problem, which is exactly why section 4 followed section 3.
Quiz 2

Halfway through generating a sentence, an RNN language model produces an unlikely word. What happens next, and why does this not occur during training?

The whole point of recurrence is that predictions do not depend on the current token alone: the hidden state carries the entire generated prefix, mistake included.
Right. Generation feeds the model's own output back in, so errors compound through the hidden state. Teacher forcing exists precisely to keep training conditioned on real text. Fig 2.1's two modes are this answer, animated.
Plain step-by-step generation never backtracks; it commits and continues. (Search strategies that keep alternatives, like beam search, were last lecture's decoding story.)
Quiz 3 (transfer): design a task where bidirectional wins

Design a task where a bidirectional RNN clearly beats a forward-only one, and explain why the same trick is unavailable to a text generator.

Answer. Any task where the label for a position depends on words that come after it, and where the full text is available up front. A clean example: tagging each word in a completed sentence as a person, place, or neither. In "Jordan sank the winning shot", the evidence that "Jordan" is a person arrives only with "sank the winning shot"; a forward-only model must commit at "Jordan" with no disambiguating context, while a bidirectional model's backward pass hands that future context to the first position. (Same structure as the lecture's "Though he later went home, Amir went to..." example.) The trick is unavailable to a generator because generation is causal: at the moment the model must choose the next word, the future half of the sequence does not exist yet, so there is nothing for a backward pass to read. Bidirectionality is for analyzing complete sequences, not producing them left to right.

Quiz 4 (transfer): the character-level trade

Character-level modelling shrinks the vocabulary from tens of thousands of words to about a hundred characters and can spell any word. Explain what it pays for those wins, in terms of the machinery of this lecture.

Answer. It pays in memory span, the one resource this lecture showed to be fragile. Every word becomes several tokens, so the same sentence is several times longer as a sequence, and every dependency stretches accordingly: "Amir ... he", six words apart, might be thirty characters apart. Longer spans mean more hops for information to survive in the hidden state and, during training, more factors in the BPTT product $\prod \sigma'(z_k) w \approx a^T$, pushing gradients further toward vanishing (Fig 3.1: compare $T = 6$ against $T = 30$ at any $a < 1$). The model also has to spend capacity learning what words are, since spelling regularities now live inside the sequence itself. So the trade is: a small softmax and no unseen-word problem, in exchange for making long-term memory even more essential and long sequences harder. That is why gated cells matter most exactly where character-level models operate.

Quiz 5 (transfer): BPTT is not a new algorithm

A classmate says backpropagation through time must be a fundamentally different algorithm from the backpropagation of Lecture 10, since it handles loops. Set them straight, and name the one genuinely new bookkeeping detail.

Answer. There is no new algorithm. Unroll the RNN across time (Fig 1.1) and the loop disappears: what remains is an ordinary feedforward computation graph, exactly the object Lecture 10 differentiated. BPTT is plain backprop applied to that unrolled graph, the same chain rule, the same error signals flowing backward node by node, each multiplied by a local derivative. The genuinely new bookkeeping is weight sharing across time: the unrolled graph contains $T$ copies of the cell, but they are copies of one parameter set, so the gradient for $W_h$ is the sum of the contributions from every time step, just as a CNN filter's gradient sums over every image position it touched. The trouble BPTT runs into, long products of local derivatives shrinking or blowing up (Fig 3.2), is not a flaw in the algorithm either; it is a property of the graph's long-chain shape. Backprop faithfully computes a gradient that happens to be tiny or huge.

That is the sequence-model story: memory beyond the window. A hidden state carries a learned summary forward under one shared update rule; training it means backprop on the unrolled graph, where long products make gradients vanish or explode; clipping contains the explosions; gates and a cell state make memory selective enough to last; and chained cells give us bidirectional readers, stacked feature extractors, and encoder-decoder translators, whose single-vector handoff is the last bottleneck standing. The next lecture stops trying to preserve the past and starts retrieving it: attention, and the transformer.
