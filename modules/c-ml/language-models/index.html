Language Models

Every lecture so far took inputs that were already numbers: pixels, measurements, features. Language arrives as words, and words come with a property no image has: an inherent order. "A smart person" and "a person smart" contain the same three words, and only one of them sounds right. This lecture turns that intuition into a machine. The whole subject compresses into one move: treat language as a prediction problem, given the previous words, predict the next one. Counting gets us surprisingly far, then hits a wall called data sparsity, and climbing over that wall forces us to invent tokens, embeddings, and neural language models, the exact machinery underneath every modern chatbot.

0. The hook: which sentence sounds right?

Read these two word sequences: "a smart person" and "a person smart". You did not deliberate. Some circuit in your head instantly scored the first one higher, and you could do it because you have consumed a lifetime of English and know which words tend to follow which. Now hand the same two sequences to a machine. Nothing we have built in this course can express "sounds right". Classifiers separate classes, regressors fit curves, CNNs find patterns in grids. None of them has an opinion about word order.

Here is the reframe the whole lecture stands on. "Sounds right" is a probability statement: among all the word sequences English speakers produce, "a smart person" occurs often and "a person smart" almost never. So build a model that assigns every sequence a probability, and the fluency judgement becomes arithmetic: score both sequences, prefer the higher one. A model that does this is called a language model, and below is a tiny real one doing exactly that judgement, trained by counting word pairs in an eight-sentence corpus:

Fig 0.1, the sequence scorer. A bigram model counts word pairs in the small corpus shown, then scores both candidate sequences as a product of per-word conditional probabilities, exactly the chain rule of section 1. Every factor is computed live from the counts; the never-seen pair "person smart" gets a tiny smoothed probability and drags its whole product down by a factor of about fifty.

Keep the shape of that figure in mind: a sequence's score is a product, one factor per word, and each factor asks "given what came before, how likely is this next word?" That question, predict the next word, turns out to be the master key. Score sentences, generate text, autocomplete a search box: all of it reduces to next-word prediction done well.

1. Language as probability

Four terms before anything else, because everything in this lecture is built from them:

Corpus
A collection of text documents. Our training data.
Token
A basic unit of text: a word, a piece of a word, or a character. For now, read "token" as "word"; section 3 complicates this on purpose.
Vocabulary $\mathcal{V}$
The set of unique tokens, of size $|\mathcal{V}|$.
Language model
A model that assigns probabilities to token sequences.

So how do we assign a probability to a whole sequence $s = (w_1, \dots, w_T)$? The chain rule of conditional probability factors any joint distribution into a product of conditionals, one per position:

$$ p(s) = p(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}) $$

Read it as a story: the probability of a sentence is the probability of its first word, times the probability of the second word given the first, times the probability of the third given the first two, and so on to the end. For "a smart person" that is $p(\text{a}) \cdot p(\text{smart} \mid \text{a}) \cdot p(\text{person} \mid \text{a smart})$, which is exactly what Fig 0.1 multiplied out. Two things make this factorization the right one for language. It is exact, no approximation yet. And every factor is a next-word prediction: the entire modelling problem has become "estimate $p(\text{next word} \mid \text{words so far})$".

Now the catch. The conditioning context $w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}$ grows with $t$: word fifty conditions on forty-nine previous words, and virtually no two long histories in any corpus are identical, so we could never estimate a separate distribution for each one. The classical fix is to truncate the memory. The nth-order Markov assumption says the next word depends only on the last $n-1$ words:

$$ p(w_1, \dots, w_T) \approx \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(w_t \mid w_{t-(n-1)}, \dots, w_{t-1}) $$

This is an approximation, and a lossy one: with $n = 3$, the model deciding the tenth word remembers only words eight and nine. But it buys us something decisive: the context is now a fixed, short window, and there are few enough short windows that we can actually estimate a distribution for each. A model built this way is called an n-gram model, where an n-gram is just a contiguous sequence of $n$ tokens ("a smart" is a bigram, "a smart person" is a trigram).

2. The n-gram model: counting as learning

The Markov assumption left us needing $p(w_t \mid \text{context})$ for every short context. Where do those numbers come from? From the oldest estimator there is: counting. Conditional probability says $P(B \mid A) = P(A, B) / P(A)$, so estimate both the numerator and denominator by their corpus frequencies:

$$ p(w_t \mid \text{context}) \approx \frac{\operatorname{count}(\text{context},\, w_t)}{\operatorname{count}(\text{context},\, \cdot)} $$

That is the entire learning algorithm. Conceptually the model is a giant table: one row per context (for a trigram model, one row per word pair, roughly $|\mathcal{V}|^2$ rows), one column per possible next word ($|\mathcal{V}|$ columns), and each row, once divided by its total, is a probability distribution over the next word. One bookkeeping detail: we mark the end of every sentence with a special </s> token, so "what follows the last word" has a well-defined answer, and pad the start with <s> so the first word has a context too. Those special tokens count as vocabulary entries like any other.

Worked by hand: bigrams from the Sam corpus

Train a bigram model on three sentences: "I am Sam", "Sam I am", "I do not like green eggs and ham". The word "I" appears as a context 3 times, followed by "am" twice and "do" once. The word "am" appears as a context twice, followed by "Sam" once and by the end-of-sentence marker once:

$$ p(\text{am} \mid \text{I}) = \tfrac{2}{3}, \qquad p(\text{do} \mid \text{I}) = \tfrac{1}{3}, \qquad p(\text{Sam} \mid \text{am}) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad p(\text{I} \mid \text{am}) = 0 $$

Note the zero: "am I" never occurs in the corpus, so the model declares it impossible. File that away; it becomes the villain of this section.

Small denominators make loud, unreliable claims, which is data sparsity in miniature.

Generation: run the table in reverse

A table of next-word distributions does not just score text, it can write text. Start from a context, look up its row, sample a next word from that distribution, append it, shift the context window forward, and repeat until the model emits the end-of-sentence token. Higher $n$ means more context per decision and locally more coherent output; it also means each specific context was seen fewer times, so the model has fewer continuations to choose from and starts parroting whole phrases from the corpus:

Fig 2.2, the n-gram text generator. Each step shows the current context (highlighted), the live next-word distribution sampled from the counting table, and the sampled word (black) getting appended. At $n=1$ the output is word salad, at $n=2$ word pairs make local sense, and at $n=3$ whole phrases from the corpus reappear because most trigram rows contain a single continuation.

The wall: data sparsity

So why not keep raising $n$? Ten words of context would surely predict better than two. Count what that costs. The table has one cell per possible n-gram, about $|\mathcal{V}|^n$ cells, which is exponential in $n$. A modest 10,000-word vocabulary gives $10^{8}$ bigram cells, $10^{12}$ trigram cells, $10^{16}$ 4-gram cells. Meanwhile the corpus stayed the same size, so the counts spread thinner and thinner: most long contexts occur rarely or never, and $\operatorname{count}(\text{context}, \cdot) \approx 0$ for almost every row. That hurts twice. A tiny denominator makes the estimate noisy, like the overconfident $p(\text{not} \mid \text{do}) = 1$ above. And a zero numerator assigns probability exactly 0 to perfectly plausible next words, the way our model declared "am I" impossible after three sentences. Longer contexts are more informative in principle and less estimable in practice, and that tension is the engine driving the rest of this lecture.

The counting machinery is a few lines of Python. ngram_counts builds the table, p_next normalizes one row into a distribution: 

def ngram_counts(corpus, n):
    counts = {}                                # context tuple -> {next word: count}
    for sentence in corpus:
        words = ["<s>"] * (n - 1) + sentence.split() + ["</s>"]
        for i in range(n - 1, len(words)):
            ctx, w = tuple(words[i - n + 1 : i]), words[i]
            row = counts.setdefault(ctx, {})
            row[w] = row.get(w, 0) + 1
    return counts

def p_next(counts, ctx):                       # one row -> a distribution
    row = counts.get(tuple(ctx), {})
    total = sum(row.values())
    return {w: c / total for w, c in row.items()}

3. Turning text into numbers

Everything so far manipulated words as opaque symbols, which counting can get away with. To go further, and in particular to put language into a neural network, we need an honest answer to a question we skipped: how does raw text become numbers a model can compute with? The pipeline every modern system uses has four steps:

$$ \text{characters} \;\to\; \text{tokens} \;\to\; \text{token IDs} \;\to\; \text{vectors} \;\to\; \text{learned language model} $$

Step one: tokenization is a choice

Tokenization splits raw text into the units the model will treat as atomic. It sounds trivial, split on spaces, until the details arrive: contractions ("don't"), names, URLs, emojis, and every word the corpus never contained. And there is no single correct answer, because the split is a modelling decision. Word-level tokenization is simple and easy to inspect, but needs special rules for all the cases above and drowns in unseen words. Named-entity tokenization keeps multi-word names like "University of Waterloo" together as single units. Subword tokenization, the choice of modern LLMs, splits rare words into reusable pieces ("unbelievably" into something like "un believ ably") so that even a word never seen in training is built from pieces that were. A related classical trick, lemmatization, maps surface forms to a dictionary form ("spending" to "spend", "better" to "good"); it strips superficial variation, which helps some classical pipelines and destroys distinctions others need, so it is task-dependent. Same string, three different token sequences:

Fig 3.1, the tokenizer playground. Type any text and toggle between word-level, subword, and named-entity tokenization: the chips are the tokens, the small number under each is its integer ID in that tokenizer's vocabulary. The same string gives different token counts and different vocabularies under each scheme, which is the point: tokenization is a choice, not a fact of the text.

Step two: token IDs, and why they lie

After tokenization, each distinct token type gets a unique integer ID in $[1, |\mathcal{V}|]$, because models index tables with integers, not strings. Here is the trap: those integers look like numbers but are only labels. A bigger ID does not mean a more important word, and a smaller gap between IDs does not mean more similar meaning. The fact that $|251 - 255| < |251 - 999|$ tells you nothing about the words behind those IDs; alphabetical neighbours are not semantic neighbours. Feed raw IDs into a model that adds and multiplies its inputs and you have silently asserted that word 500 is "halfway between" word 1 and word 999, an ordering nobody intended:

Fig 3.2, IDs versus one-hot. Left view: three words placed at their IDs on a number line; the ID distances (computed live) put "pizza" next to "pillow" and far from "burger", a nonsense geometry. Right view: the same words as one-hot vectors, shown as length-$|\mathcal{V}|$ strips with a single 1; every pairwise dot product is 0, so the false ordering is gone, and so is every notion of similarity.

The standard first fix is one-hot encoding: represent token $w$ as a vector of length $|\mathcal{V}|$ that is all zeros except a single 1 at position $w$,

$$ o_{w,j} = \begin{cases} 1 & j = w \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$

One-hot vectors treat identity as purely categorical, which repairs the false ordering: no word is "bigger" than another, and no word is spuriously close to another. But look at the price. The vectors are enormous ($|\mathcal{V}|$ dimensions for a single word) and almost entirely zeros, and every pair of distinct words is exactly orthogonal: $o_{\text{summer}}^\top o_{\text{autumn}} = 0$, the same as $o_{\text{summer}}^\top o_{\text{tractor}} = 0$. "Summer" and "autumn" should have something in common, and one-hot encoding is structurally incapable of saying so. We fixed the lie and installed a blindness.

4. Word embeddings

We want vectors where similar words are geometrically close. But what makes two words "similar", and how would a machine ever measure it without understanding meaning? The answer is one of the great ideas of computational linguistics, the distributional hypothesis (Firth, 1957): "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Words that occur in similar contexts have related meanings. You learn what "sushi" is partly because it shows up in the same sentence-shaped slots as "pizza": after "ate", before "tastes fresh", near "ordered". Meaning leaves a statistical fingerprint, and the fingerprint is countable.

So count it. Give each word $w$ a context-count vector $c_w$ with one entry per vocabulary word, holding how often that word appears near $w$ in the corpus. Similar words now get vectors pointing in similar directions, and we measure that with cosine similarity, the same measure the k-NN lecture used:

$$ \cos\theta = \frac{c_{w_1}^\top c_{w_2}}{\lVert c_{w_1} \rVert\, \lVert c_{w_2} \rVert} $$

This works, genuinely: "pizza" and "burger" score high because they share neighbours, "pizza" and "guitar" score low. But the vectors are still $|\mathcal{V}|$-dimensional and mostly zeros, meaningful but huge. The final step is compression: a dense word embedding maps each word to $e_w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ with $d \ll |\mathcal{V}|$, keeping the semantic geometry ($e_{\text{pizza}} \approx e_{\text{burger}}$) while shrinking thousands of dimensions to a few hundred. The coordinates of $e_w$ are learned features, not named context words; no individual dimension means anything by itself, only the geometry does. Watch the whole compression happen on a real co-occurrence matrix:

Fig 4.1, from context counts to a dense embedding. Pick a word: its full context-count row over the 31-word vocabulary (computed live from the mini-corpus) shrinks to a $d$-dimensional dense vector via a low-rank factorization of the co-occurrence matrix, computed in your browser. The cosine table shows that with enough dimensions the similar pairs stay similar and the dissimilar pairs stay dissimilar; slide $d$ down to 1 or 2 and watch the geometry collapse, every word forced to look like every other.

Where do dense embeddings come from at scale? The figure above used the linear-algebra route: stack all the context counts into a co-occurrence matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times |\mathcal{V}|}$ and find a low-rank approximation $X \approx R\tilde{R}^\top$, so each count is approximated by a dot product of two short vectors, $x_{ij} \approx r_i^\top \tilde{r}_j$, and each row of $R$ becomes a word vector. The famous embedding methods are refinements of exactly this idea; the details are optional depth:

For the interested reader: GloVe and Word2Vec

GloVe fits the logarithm of the co-occurrence counts rather than the raw counts, with a weighting function that mutes unreliable rare pairs, minimizing

$$ J(R, \tilde{R}) = \sum_{i,j} f(x_{ij}) \left( \log x_{ij} - r_i^\top \tilde{r}_j \right)^2, \qquad f(x) = \begin{cases} (x/100)^{3/4} & x < 100 \\ 1 & x \ge 100 \end{cases} $$

The log tames the hugely frequent pairs (otherwise "the" would dominate every fit) and $f$ down-weights counts too small to trust, the same small-denominator worry as in section 2.

Word2Vec reaches the same destination through a prediction task instead of a matrix factorization: CBOW trains a model to predict a target word from its surrounding context words, and skip-gram predicts the context words from the target. Either way, words appearing in similar contexts are pushed toward similar vectors, because they must make similar predictions. Both methods bank on the same hypothesis: meaning is reflected in word-context statistics.

Here is the payoff that made word embeddings famous. Because the geometry encodes meaning, directions in embedding space become relationships, and analogies turn into vector arithmetic:

$$ e(\text{Paris}) - e(\text{France}) \;\approx\; e(\text{Rome}) - e(\text{Italy}) $$

The vector from a country to its capital is roughly the same arrow no matter which country you start from, so "Paris is to France as ? is to Italy" is answerable by adding arrows. Explore the geometry:

5. Neural language models

Time to collect the debt from section 2. The n-gram model died of data sparsity because it treated every context as an unrelated row in a table: what it learned about "the dog" taught it nothing about "the cat". But we now own vectors where cat and dog are close. So replace the lookup table with a learned function: map the context words to their embeddings, and let a neural network turn those embeddings into a distribution over the next word,

$$ \text{context embeddings} \;\longmapsto\; p_\theta(\cdot \mid \text{context}) $$

Similar contexts now produce similar inputs to the network, and therefore similar predictions, automatically. The embeddings live in an embedding table $E \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times d}$, one row per vocabulary token; the "embedding layer" you meet in every deep-learning framework does nothing more mysterious than return row $w$: $e_w = E[w] \in \mathbb{R}^d$. And crucially, $E$ is a parameter, learned by backpropagation along with everything else, so the network invents whatever word geometry helps it predict next words.

The simplest full design is the neural trigram model, same two-word context as a count trigram, different machinery. Four steps:

  1. Look up the two context words: $e_{w_{t-2}} = E[w_{t-2}]$, $e_{w_{t-1}} = E[w_{t-1}]$.
  2. Concatenate them: $c_t = [\,e_{w_{t-2}}^\top,\ e_{w_{t-1}}^\top\,]^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{2d}$.
  3. One linear layer produces a score per vocabulary word: $z_t = W c_t + b$, with $W \in \mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}| \times 2d}$.
  4. Softmax turns scores into the next-word distribution: $p_\theta(w_t \mid w_{t-2}, w_{t-1}) = \operatorname{softmax}(z_t)$.

Steps 3 and 4 should look familiar: this is exactly the multi-class logistic regression head from Lecture 6, with $|\mathcal{V}|$ classes (one per word) and the concatenated embeddings as the feature vector. The parameter count, and why it matters

Count what this model costs. The embedding table is $|\mathcal{V}| \times d$, the linear layer is $|\mathcal{V}| \times 2d$ (ignore the bias), so

$$ \underbrace{d|\mathcal{V}|}_{E} \;+\; \underbrace{2d|\mathcal{V}|}_{W} \;=\; 3d|\mathcal{V}| $$

parameters, linear in both the vocabulary size and the embedding dimension. The count trigram needed a table of roughly $|\mathcal{V}|^3$ cells, exponential in the context length. Concretely, with $|\mathcal{V}| = 10{,}000$ and $d = 100$: the neural trigram has $3 \cdot 100 \cdot 10{,}000 = 3 \times 10^6$ parameters, while the count table has $10{,}000^3 = 10^{12}$ cells. Three million numbers you can train against a trillion cells you could never fill.

And the win is bigger than storage. The neural model generalizes through shared embeddings. Suppose "the cat is eating" never occurs in training but "the dog is eating" does. The count model looks up the row for context "the cat", finds no "is" ever followed it, and answers exactly zero. The neural model computes with $e_{\text{cat}}$, and since cat and dog appear in similar contexts elsewhere in the corpus, training pushed $e_{\text{cat}} \approx e_{\text{dog}}$, so the cat context produces nearly the dog context's input and inherits a healthy probability for "is". Knowledge about dogs transfers to cats through the geometry. Watch both models answer the same query:

Fig 5.2, the generalization demo. The training corpus contains "the dog is ..." sentences but never "the cat is". Left: the count trigram's row for context "the cat", where $p(\text{is})$ is exactly 0. Right: the trained neural model's distribution for the same context, where "is" gets real probability because the learned embeddings put cat near dog (their live cosine similarity is shown). Flip the context to "the dog" and the two models roughly agree, because that context has data.

One honest caveat: a single linear layer on concatenated embeddings can only capture additive interactions between the two context words. Deeper models insert hidden layers, $h^{(\ell)} = \sigma(W^{(\ell)} c + b^{(\ell)})$ with $z_t = W^{(L)} h^{(L)} + b^{(L)}$, buying nonlinear interactions between the context embeddings, the same depth-for-expressiveness trade the neural networks lectures priced out.

6. Training and decoding

Training: teacher forcing

Where does the training signal come from? This is the quietly beautiful part: language modelling is supervised learning where the text labels itself. Every position in every sentence is an example: input, the words so far $w_{<t}$; label, the actual next word $w_t$. No annotation, no human labelling, just text. During training, the model always conditions on the true previous words from the corpus, a regime called teacher forcing, and is rewarded for putting high probability on the true next word. Two special tokens do the bookkeeping: <BOS> pads the left edge so a trigram model always has two context words, even at position one, and <EOS> gives the model a way to end a sentence.

Treating sentences as independent, we maximize the likelihood $\prod_i p(s^{(i)})$, with each sentence factored by the chain rule as $p(s) = \prod_t p(w_t \mid w_{<t})$. Maximizing log-likelihood is exactly minimizing cross-entropy, the same loss as logistic regression, summed over positions:

$$ -\log p(s) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(w_t \mid w_{<t}) = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{w=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|} t_{tw} \log y_{tw} $$

where $t_{tw}$ is the one-hot target at position $t$ and $y_{tw}$ the predicted probability. Every position is a $|\mathcal{V}|$-way classification problem, and the loss is the log-loss you already know.

Generation: the model is on its own

Generation breaks the symmetry. At generation time there is no true history, so the model conditions on its own previous outputs:

$$ \hat{w}_t = \operatorname{Decode}\!\left( p_\theta(\cdot \mid \hat{w}_{<t}) \right) $$

That $\operatorname{Decode}$ is a genuine choice, and it is worth being precise about what kind of choice: a decoding rule is not a different model. The same trained $p_\theta$ produces wildly different text depending on how you turn each distribution into a word:

  • Greedy: $\hat{w}_t = \arg\max_v\, p_\theta(v \mid \hat{w}_{<t})$. Deterministic, safe, and prone to getting stuck repeating locally plausible phrases.
  • Sampling with temperature: divide the logits by $\tau$ before the softmax, $$ p_\tau(v) = \frac{\exp(z_v / \tau)}{\sum_u \exp(z_u / \tau)} $$ Small $\tau$ sharpens the distribution toward the argmax; large $\tau$ flattens it toward uniform. Temperature is a dial between "predictable" and "chaotic".
  • Top-k: sample, but only from the $k$ most probable tokens, renormalized. Cuts off the long tail of unlikely words.
  • Top-p (nucleus): sample from the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability reaches $p$. The cutoff adapts: a confident distribution keeps few tokens, an uncertain one keeps many.
  • Beam search: keep $B$ partial sentences alive and grow the ones with the best total $\sum_t \log p_\theta(w_t \mid w_{<t})$. Strong for translation and summarization, where one high-probability answer is wanted; for open-ended generation it drifts generic and repetitive.
Worked by hand: softmax, then temperature

Say the model scores three candidate next words with logits $z = (2, 1, 0)$. The plain softmax ($\tau = 1$) exponentiates and normalizes: $e^2 = 7.389$, $e^1 = 2.718$, $e^0 = 1$, sum $11.107$, so

$$ p = (0.665,\ 0.245,\ 0.090) $$

Cool it to $\tau = 0.5$: the logits double to $(4, 2, 0)$ and the top word takes over, $p_{0.5} = (0.867,\ 0.117,\ 0.016)$. Heat it to $\tau = 2$: the logits halve to $(1, 0.5, 0)$ and the distribution flattens, $p_{2} = (0.506,\ 0.307,\ 0.186)$. Same model, same scores, three different personalities.

def softmax(z):
    ez = [math.exp(v - max(z)) for v in z]     # subtract max: numerical safety
    return [v / sum(ez) for v in ez]

def sample_with_temperature(z, tau=1.0):
    p = softmax([v / tau for v in z])
    r = random.random()                        # inverse-CDF sampling
    for w, pw in enumerate(p):
        r -= pw
        if r <= 0:
            return w

The temperature slider reshapes the bars through $p_\tau$ (recomputed live), top-k and top-p grey out the tokens their rule discards, and Sample draws real samples from whatever distribution you have built; greedy always returns the same word. Push $\tau$ up and watch "purple" and "algebra" start winning samples.

Check your understanding

You lower the sampling temperature from $\tau = 1$ toward $\tau \to 0$. What happens to generation?

Right. Dividing logits by a small $\tau$ stretches the gaps between scores, and the exponential turns bigger gaps into lopsided probabilities. At $\tau \to 0$ all mass lands on the top token: greedy decoding, recovered as a limit.
Backwards: that is raising the temperature. Check the worked example: $\tau = 0.5$ pushed the top word from 0.665 to 0.867.
Softmax is not scale-invariant: $\operatorname{softmax}(z/\tau) \ne \operatorname{softmax}(z)$ unless the logits are all equal. The exponential makes rescaling change the shape, which is the entire point of the dial.

7. Evaluating language models

Two language models disagree; which is better? The extrinsic answer plugs each model into a real downstream system, translation, speech recognition, summarization, and measures which helps more. That is the measurement you actually care about, and it is expensive, slow, and specific to one application. The intrinsic answer scores held-out text directly: a good language model should be less surprised by real text it has never seen, meaning it assigns that text higher probability.

The standard intrinsic metric is perplexity. Take the held-out tokens $w_1, \dots, w_M$, compute the average negative log2-probability the model assigns each one (the average "bits of surprise"), then exponentiate back:

$$ \bar{\ell} = -\frac{1}{M} \sum_{t=1}^{M} \log_2 p_\theta(w_t \mid w_{<t}), \qquad \operatorname{PPL} = 2^{\bar{\ell}} $$

Lower is better. The exponentiation is what makes the number interpretable: a perplexity of 10 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it were choosing uniformly among 10 plausible next words. A useless model that guesses uniformly over the whole vocabulary lands at perplexity exactly $|\mathcal{V}|$; every bit of learned structure ratchets that number down.

Worked by hand: perplexity of four tokens

A model assigns a held-out four-token sequence the step-by-step probabilities $\tfrac{1}{2},\ \tfrac{1}{4},\ \tfrac{1}{4},\ \tfrac{1}{8}$. The surprises are $-\log_2$ of each: $1, 2, 2, 3$ bits. Average: $\bar\ell = 8/4 = 2$ bits. Perplexity: $2^2 = 4$. The model behaves as if it were choosing among about four plausible words at each step.

def perplexity(model, text):
    words = ["<s>"] + text.split() + ["</s>"]
    bits = 0.0
    for i in range(1, len(words)):
        p = model(words[i], context=words[:i])   # p(w_i | w_<i)
        bits += -math.log2(p)
    return 2 ** (bits / (len(words) - 1))

Fig 7.1, the perplexity meter. A bigram model trained live on the picnic corpus reads a held-out sentence token by token; each row shows $p(w_t \mid w_{t-1})$ and its bits of surprise, accumulating into the average and the final $\operatorname{PPL} = 2^{\bar\ell}$. Compare against the uniform model, whose perplexity is exactly $|\mathcal{V}|$, then switch to the scrambled sentence: same words, destroyed order, and the perplexity explodes, proof the model learned word order and not just word frequency.

Perplexity is a clean, fast, comparable number, and you should distrust it exactly as much as it deserves. It measures one-step prediction under teacher forcing on in-distribution text. It does not measure factual correctness (a fluent lie can be high-probability), long-range coherence (one step at a time never checks whether paragraph five contradicts paragraph one), downstream usefulness, or the quality of text under your actual decoder, which section 6 showed can transform the same model's output completely. Perplexity is a good thermometer and a bad final exam.

Check your understanding

On a held-out sequence of 5 tokens, a model assigns per-token probabilities $\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{4}, \tfrac{1}{16}$. What is its perplexity?

Perplexity averages the log-probabilities over all positions; the mode of the probabilities is not involved. Convert each to bits, average, then exponentiate.
Right. $-\log_2$ of each probability gives 1, 1, 2, 2, 4; the mean is 2 bits; $2^2 = 4$. On average the model is as uncertain as a uniform choice among 4 words, even though one step was a 16-way surprise.
The 10 is the total bits; perplexity uses the per-token average (2 bits), then exponentiates: $2^2 = 4$. Without the average, longer texts would look automatically worse.

8. Summary and method card

The lecture in one paragraph. Language is sequential, so model it as next-word prediction: the chain rule makes that exact, the Markov assumption makes it tractable. Counting n-grams works until the table ($|\mathcal{V}|^n$ cells) outgrows any corpus, and the zeros and noisy ratios of data sparsity take over. Fixing that required fixing the representation: tokens to IDs (labels, not measurements), IDs to one-hot vectors (categorical, but blind to similarity), one-hot to embeddings (dense vectors whose geometry is meaning, courtesy of the distributional hypothesis). With embeddings in hand, the neural language model replaces the exponential table with a $3d|\mathcal{V}|$-parameter function that generalizes to unseen contexts through shared geometry. Training is supervised learning on free labels via teacher forcing and cross-entropy; generation is a separate decoding choice, from greedy to temperature to top-p; and perplexity gives a useful, limited measure of how surprised the model is by held-out text.

Method card · Language Models
AssumptionA word's probability depends on its context, and words appearing in similar contexts have related meanings (the distributional hypothesis).
Model classSequence models that represent tokens with embeddings and predict the next word with a neural classifier, $p_\theta(w_t \mid \text{context}) = \operatorname{softmax}(W c_t + b)$.
Learning / selectionPreprocess text to tokens and IDs; learn the embedding table and network weights by maximizing sequence likelihood, equivalently minimizing cross-entropy, with teacher forcing.
PredictionScore sequences by the chain rule; generate by repeatedly decoding from $p_\theta(\cdot \mid \hat w_{<t})$ with a chosen rule (greedy, temperature, top-k/top-p, beam).
StrengthsHandles sparsity far better than count tables ($3d|\mathcal{V}|$ vs $|\mathcal{V}|^n$), learns semantic word vectors as a by-product, and reframes language as scalable next-word prediction.
LimitationsFixed-context models still miss long-range dependencies and word order beyond the window; perplexity is only a proxy for quality; output depends heavily on the decoding rule.
Key hyperparametersContext length $n$, embedding dimension $d$, network depth, tokenization scheme, and at generation time the decoding rule ($\tau$, $k$, $p$, $B$).

Where this sits in the course story: the CNN lectures showed that respecting an input's structure (locality, weight sharing) beats brute force, and this lecture did the same for language, whose structure is sequential order and distributional meaning. The fixed context window is the one assumption still creaking: a trigram model, neural or not, cannot remember word eight when predicting word eighty. Next lecture removes that ceiling with recurrent networks that carry a memory across the whole sequence, the road that leads through LSTMs to attention and transformers, where next-word prediction at scale becomes the systems everyone now talks to.

9. End-of-lesson quiz

Two quick checks, then three transfer questions that ask you to design and explain, not recall. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Quiz 1

A neural trigram model and a count trigram model are trained on the same corpus with $|\mathcal{V}| = 20{,}000$ and $d = 50$. Which statement is correct?

Swapped: the exponential blow-up belongs to the counting table, the trim $3d|\mathcal{V}|$ to the neural model. That asymmetry is the whole argument for going neural.
Conditioning on the same context does not mean paying the same price: the table needs a cell per possible context-word combination, the network needs only embeddings plus one weight matrix.
Right, both halves. $3d|\mathcal{V}|$ is linear in vocabulary size while $|\mathcal{V}|^3$ is astronomical, and the deeper win is behavioural: shared embeddings let the neural model transfer what it knows to unseen contexts instead of answering 0 (Fig 5.2).
Quiz 2

Model A has perplexity 12 on held-out text; Model B has perplexity 45. What can you validly conclude?

Perplexity is a teacher-forced, one-step measure; two models with different perplexities can flip in generation quality once a decoder and error accumulation enter. It suggests, it does not guarantee.
Right, and stated with exactly the right caution. Perplexity is a real signal, lower means less surprised by real text, but it is silent about long-range coherence, factuality, and behaviour under your chosen decoding rule.
Perplexity never checks facts: fluent, statistically typical text scores well whether or not it is true. Probability mass is not truth.
Quiz 3 (transfer): design a decoding strategy

You are building a brainstorming assistant that must produce varied suggestions (ten different taglines, not one tagline ten times) that are still coherent (no word salad). Design a decoding strategy and justify each choice against the alternatives.

Answer (one good design). Rule out greedy and beam search first: both are deterministic-ish maximizers, so they collapse diversity, and beam search in open-ended generation drifts generic on top of that. Pure high-temperature sampling buys diversity but pays in coherence, because the flattened tail lets genuinely bad tokens through (Fig 6.1: crank $\tau$ and watch "algebra" win samples). The clean solution is top-p (nucleus) sampling with a moderate temperature, say $\tau \approx 0.9$ and $p \approx 0.9$: the nucleus cutoff deletes the incoherent tail entirely, while sampling within the nucleus keeps runs different from each other. Top-p beats top-k here because the candidate set adapts: when the model is confident the nucleus is small and the output stays sharp, and when many continuations are genuinely plausible, exactly the situation brainstorming wants, the nucleus widens on its own. Then generate the ten taglines as ten independent samples.

Quiz 4 (transfer): great perplexity, terrible generation

A teammate reports: "our model's perplexity dropped from 40 to 9, but the text it generates still turns repetitive and derails after a few sentences." Explain how both observations can be true at once.

Answer. Perplexity and generation test the model under different conditions. Perplexity is measured with teacher forcing: at every step the model is handed the true history from real held-out text and asked for one next-word distribution. Generation feeds the model its own outputs as history, so a small early oddity produces a context slightly unlike anything in training, which produces a slightly worse next prediction, and errors compound step by step; the perplexity number never sees this feedback loop. On top of that, perplexity is one-step-ahead, so long-range coherence, staying on topic across sentences, not contradicting itself, simply is not measured. And the decoding rule intervenes: a greedy decoder can lock a well-calibrated model into repetitive loops that sampling would escape. Better one-step prediction on real text (40 to 9) is compatible with all of these failure modes remaining.

Quiz 5 (transfer): the softmax head is an old friend

Show precisely how the neural trigram's output layer is multi-class logistic regression from Lecture 6. What plays the role of the input features, the classes, and the loss?

Answer. Multi-class logistic regression computes one linear score per class from a feature vector, $z = Wx + b$, applies a softmax, and trains with cross-entropy against a one-hot target. The neural trigram's head is literally this with a renaming: the feature vector $x$ is the concatenated context embedding $c_t \in \mathbb{R}^{2d}$, the classes are the $|\mathcal{V}|$ vocabulary words (row $v$ of $W$ is class $v$'s weight vector), and the loss $-\sum_w t_{tw} \log y_{tw}$ is the same cross-entropy with the true next word as the one-hot label. The only genuinely new ingredient is where the features come from: instead of being handed measurements, the model learns its features, because the embedding table behind $c_t$ is trained by the same gradient. Classifier head from Lecture 6, learned representation from Lecture 9, applied to text.

That is language modelling from first principles: order makes language special, the chain rule turns order into next-word prediction, counting solves it until sparsity wins, and embeddings plus a softmax classifier solve what counting could not, with decoding and perplexity rounding out the toolkit. Every chatbot you have used is this lecture's machinery with a longer memory and more parameters. The longer memory is the next two lectures: recurrent networks, then attention and transformers.
