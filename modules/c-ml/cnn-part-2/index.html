0. The hook: an idea is not enough

Here is a fact that should bother you. By 1998, Yann LeCun's LeNet-5 was a complete, working convolutional network: convolution, pooling, fully connected layers, trained end to end by backpropagation, reading handwritten digits on real envelopes for the US Postal Service. Every architectural idea from Part I already existed, deployed, in production. And then, for over a decade, almost nothing. Computer vision kept running on hand-crafted features, and neural networks were a niche taste. The breakthrough did not come from a new equation. It came in 2012, when a CNN called AlexNet won the ImageNet challenge by a margin nobody could argue with, and it won because three things had finally scaled up at the same time:

🗂️
Data
LeNet trained on 60,000 tiny digit scans. AlexNet trained on 1.2 million labelled photographs across 1,000 categories. No dataset, no deep model worth training.
⚡
Compute
Training AlexNet took 5 to 6 days on two gaming GPUs. On the CPUs of the LeNet era the same run was simply out of reach. Hardware built for video games turned out to be built for convolution.
🏗️
Architectures
Depth went from 5 weight layers to 8, then 22, then past 100, and each jump needed a new structural idea to stay trainable: bottlenecks, factorized filters, skip connections.

The big idea of this lecture, worth writing at the top of your notes: the success of deep learning did not come from any single ingredient. It came from scaling architecture, data, compute, optimization, and validation together. Take any one away and the whole thing stalls, which is exactly what the twenty quiet years demonstrate. The plan: first we settle the riddle Part I left open, then we take the three ingredients in turn, and then we cover the practical miscellany, transfer learning, what the filters actually learn, where these models are brittle, and what vision tasks beyond classification look like.

1. The 1×1 convolution, riddle answered

Part I ended on a riddle: does a $1 \times 1$ convolution ever make sense? A kernel that sees one pixel cannot detect an edge, a corner, or any spatial pattern at all. It looks useless. The resolution is the third dimension. A kernel's depth always equals its input's depth, so on a stack of $M$ feature maps a "$1 \times 1$" kernel is really $1 \times 1 \times M$: at every location it computes a weighted sum straight down the channel stack,

$$ z_{\text{out}}[i,j] \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{M} w_m \, z_m[i,j] \;+\; b $$

one learned linear combination of all $M$ channels, applied at every pixel. That single observation buys three distinct things, and all three matter in practice:

  • Dimensionality reduction. Use $I < M$ filters and the volume goes from $W \times H \times M$ to $W \times H \times I$: fewer channels, same spatial size, and every convolution downstream gets cheaper because its kernels are now $K \times K \times I$ deep instead of $K \times K \times M$.
  • Cross-channel mixing. The output is a learned combination of existing detectors, so it can capture correlations across channels: "edge here AND red here" becomes one new feature.
  • Extra capacity. Follow it with a nonlinearity and you have added a genuine layer of processing, per pixel, at almost no spatial cost. Interspersed with ReLUs, 1×1 convolutions deepen the network cheaply.

And the direction is not fixed: use more $1 \times 1$ filters than input channels and the depth goes up instead of down. GoogLeNet, coming in section 4, leans on the reduction direction so hard it has a name for it, the bottleneck: squeeze the channels with a cheap $1 \times 1$, do the expensive $3 \times 3$ or $5 \times 5$ work in the narrow space, and the layer's cost collapses. Worked: what a 1×1 layer does to the volume

Take a $28 \times 28 \times 64$ volume through $I = 16$ filters of size $1 \times 1$. The sizing formula from Part I barely notices: $\hat W = (28 - 1 + 0)/1 + 1 = 28$. Output volume: $28 \times 28 \times 16$, same spatial grid, a quarter of the channels.

params = K²·I·J + I = 1·16·64 + 16 = 1,040
a 5×5 conv on the full 64 channels into 16 maps: 25·16·64 + 16 = 25,616

The 1×1 layer costs 4% of the 5×5 layer's parameters, and afterwards that 5×5 layer only needs kernels 16 deep instead of 64 deep. Squeeze first, then do the expensive spatial work: that is the bottleneck trick.

Check your understanding

A $1 \times 1$ convolution with $I$ filters is applied to a $14 \times 14 \times 48$ volume. Which quantity can it NOT change?

Backwards: changing the channel count is the one thing it is best at. Output depth equals the number of filters, so $I = 8$ compresses to 8 channels and $I = 96$ expands to 96.
Each output channel is a learned weighted combination of all 48 input channels, plus a bias, usually followed by a ReLU. That is real feature-building, not copying: Fig 1.1 shows the mixture arithmetic.
Right. With $K = 1$, $P = 0$, $S = 1$ the sizing formula gives $\hat W = W$ always. A 1×1 convolution operates purely along the channel dimension: depth is its whole business, space is untouched.
Check: give three reasons to use a 1×1 convolution

Say them out loud before expanding. The lecture wants three distinct benefits.

Answer. One, dimensionality reduction: with fewer filters than input channels it shrinks the depth of the volume without touching the spatial dimensions, which cuts the compute of every layer that follows (and it can equally increase the depth if you use more filters). Two, cross-channel combination: each output is a learned linear combination of all input channels at that pixel, so it captures correlations between existing feature maps and builds new features out of them. Three, added capacity: interleaved with nonlinearities it acts like an extra (cheap) layer of processing, adding depth and expressive power. This is exactly the recipe GoogLeNet uses for its bottlenecks.

2. Ingredient 1: large curated datasets

Start with the least glamorous ingredient, because it decides everything downstream: garbage in, garbage out. A model can only learn the structure its dataset contains, so curation is not clerical work, it is a set of design decisions, each with consequences:

  • Which categories? How many classes, and how fine-grained: is "dog" one class, or is every breed its own?
  • How many images? Sample count is the fuel for parameter count; too little and big models memorize.
  • How much resolution? Input dimensions set the compute bill for every experiment ever run on the data.
  • How to annotate accurately? Labels are the targets; noisy labels put a ceiling on every model, and semi-supervised tricks only stretch, not replace, human labelling.
  • How are images sourced? Ethics, privacy, and cost all live here, and biases in sourcing become biases in the model.
  • How to preprocess? Normalize and standardize so the optimizer sees well-conditioned inputs, the same lesson as always.

Three landmark datasets show how these choices evolved, and how violently the scale jumped. MNIST: handwritten digits from scanned zip codes, 10 classes, 60K training and 10K test images, grayscale $28 \times 28$, digits centred and size-normalized, about 60 MB. CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100: labelled subsets of the 80-million-tiny-images collection, 10 or 100 classes, 60K training and 6K test images, colour, about 160 MB. Then the discontinuity. ImageNet (2009): 1,000 classes, 1.2 million training images, 150K test images, about 50 GB. Its categories come from WordNet synsets, about 22,000 available, with the 1,000 most common chosen for the challenge, and they are deliberately fine-grained, many dog breeds among them. The images were pulled from image search and hand-labelled by Mechanical Turk workers, which is itself a lesson: at this scale, labelling is an industrial process. Feel the jumps:

Fig 2.1, the dataset ladder. Cards for MNIST, CIFAR-10/100, and ImageNet with the numbers from the lecture; pick an attribute and the log-scale bars re-sort so you can feel the jump, roughly three orders of magnitude from MNIST to ImageNet in disk size. The thumbnails are synthetic sketches in each dataset's style (procedurally drawn, not real dataset photos).

ImageNet mattered because it came with a scoreboard: the ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (ILSVRC), an annual competition that made progress public, measurable, and brutally comparable. Two of its tasks matter for this lecture. Classification asks what is in the image: one label out of 1,000. Object detection asks what and where: every object, each with a bounding box. Keep that distinction; section 7 stretches it further, all the way to labelling every pixel.

3. Ingredient 2: compute

The second ingredient is the one that snuck up on everybody. After 2012, neural networks began to outperform everything else in the ILSVRC standings, and a pattern emerged immediately: the organizations at the top of the leaderboard were the ones with the most compute and the recruited academic talent to use it. The competitive advantage was no longer a clever feature descriptor. It was GPUs, data, and the budget to run big experiments.

Which brings in the business lesson the lecture likes: when there is a gold rush, sell shovels. NVIDIA, co-founded and led by Jensen Huang since 1993, spent two decades building GPUs for 3D graphics, hardware whose whole job is doing millions of small multiply-accumulate operations in parallel. Convolution, as you now know from Part I, is exactly millions of small multiply-accumulate operations. When the 2012 boom hit, NVIDIA was uniquely positioned, and it became the dominant supplier of AI compute, with over 80% market share. The miners changed every year; the shovel seller kept winning.

But here is the part that matters for how you think, not just for trivia night: more GPU power enabled deeper models, but only when data, compute, optimization, and validation scaled together. This is the same "scale the ingredients together" claim from the hook, and later scaling-laws work on neural language models made it quantitative: loss improves predictably as you grow model and data in balance, and stalls when one outruns the others. A giant model on a small dataset memorizes. A giant dataset under a tiny model plateaus. Plenty of both without the compute to finish training helps nobody. Play with the balance:

Fig 3.1, the scale-together playground. 4. Ingredient 3: deeper and deeper architectures

Third ingredient: the models themselves. The story runs through four architectures, and each one is remembered for a structural idea, not for a parameter setting. Walk the timeline first, then we will do the arithmetic that explains the oddest fact in it: the 2014 network is deeper than the 2012 network and fifteen times smaller.

LeNet: the proof it could work at all

LeNet (LeCun and colleagues, developed from 1988 to 1998, LeNet-1 through LeNet-5) recognized handwritten digits and characters so the US Postal Service could sort envelopes by zip code automatically. The recipe is the one Part I built: convolution for feature extraction, average pooling (they called it subsampling) for downsampling, fully connected layers for the decision, originally with tanh activations where a modern network would use ReLU. It worked, it shipped, and it was the existence proof that end-to-end learned vision was possible. Now put Part I's sizing formula to work and fill in LeNet-5's whole layer table by hand:

Total: $156 + 2{,}416 + 48{,}120 + 10{,}164 + 850 = 61{,}706$ weights, which is the lecture's "about 60,000 parameters" for LeNet-5. Notice C3: a 5×5 convolution on a 5×5 input is a convolution that only fits once, so its output is $1 \times 1 \times 120$, effectively a fully connected layer wearing a convolution costume. Hold that thought for GoogLeNet.

AlexNet: the 2012 detonation

AlexNet (2012) is architecturally a scaled-up LeNet, 8 weight layers, ReLU instead of tanh, about 60 million parameters, but its ILSVRC result changed the field: 16.4% top-5 error, where top-5 means the model gets five guesses and is scored correct if the true label is among them. The gap to every non-neural competitor was so large that the argument ended more or less overnight; this single win set off the modern deep-learning boom. The engineering detail worth remembering: 60M parameters did not fit on one GPU of the day, so the network was literally split into two parallel branches running on two GTX 580 3GB gaming cards, communicating only at certain layers, and training still took 5 to 6 days. The architecture was bent around the hardware, which tells you how close to the edge of feasible this was.

GoogLeNet: deeper and 15× smaller

GoogLeNet (2014) made the point that depth and size are different axes: 22 weight layers, nearly three times AlexNet's depth, with about 4 million parameters against AlexNet's 60 million, and 6.6% test error. Where did 93% of the parameters go? Three deliberate moves. First, it removed the giant fully connected layers at the end and went fully convolutional; in AlexNet-style designs those dense layers held the bulk of all weights, exactly like the FC-versus-conv arithmetic from Part I predicts. Second, it factorized big convolutions into stacks of smaller ones, several small kernels reaching the same receptive field for fewer weights. Third, the star of section 1: $1 \times 1$ bottlenecks squeezing channel counts before every expensive convolution. Depth up, parameters down, accuracy up. The lesson generalizes: parameters are a design choice, not a tax you must pay for depth.

ResNet: past 100 layers, past the benchmark

ResNet (He and colleagues, 2015) broke the depth barrier outright: over 100 layers, made trainable by skip connections, which we will demystify in a moment. It was so strong that the community's measuring stick had to change, from top-5 accuracy to top-1, and error analysis found that many of the remaining "mistakes" traced to wrong ground-truth labels rather than wrong predictions. CNNs had surpassed human performance on ImageNet, and the community largely retired the benchmark and moved on to harder problems. Parameters, connections, FLOPS: memory versus speed

The comparison table counts parameters, but parameters answer only one question: how much memory does the model need? The question a deployment engineer asks is different: how fast is it? The quantity that answers that is the number of connections, every multiply, add, nonlinearity, and pooling operation the forward pass executes, measured in FLOPS (floating-point operations). For a conv layer the two quantities split cleanly because of weight sharing:

$$ \text{params} = (K^2 J + 1)\,M, \qquad \text{connections} = \hat W \hat H \, M \,(K^2 J + 1) $$

Same bracket, but connections multiply it by the whole output grid, because the shared kernel is re-executed at every one of the $\hat W \hat H$ positions. Parameters do not grow with image size; work does. So FLOPS set the computational complexity and therefore the achievable frames per second, while parameters set the memory footprint, and a network can be small-but-slow or big-but-fast depending on where its weights sit. Check it live:

Fig 4.2, parameters versus connections for one conv layer. Sliders for input size, kernel, filters, and stride; both counts are computed from the formulas above and drawn as log-scale bars. Grow the input and watch connections climb while parameters sit still; then compare a conv layer against a dense layer, where the two counts are the same thing.

The same arithmetic, as code you can rerun. This layer_report is Part I's sizing formula plus the two counting formulas, and the version below actually executes: 

def layer_report(W, H, J, K, M, P, S):
    Wo = (W - K + 2*P) // S + 1        # output width  (Part I formula)
    Ho = (H - K + 2*P) // S + 1        # output height
    per_filter = K*K*J + 1             # weights per filter, incl. bias
    params      = per_filter * M       # memory
    connections = Wo * Ho * M * per_filter   # work per forward pass
    print(f"output:      {Wo} x {Ho} x {M}")
    print(f"params:      {params:,}")
    print(f"connections: {connections:,}  (~{2*connections:,} FLOPS)")

Right. Each shared weight can be re-executed thousands of times per forward pass, so the operation count (connections, measured in FLOPS) is what sets frames per second; parameters only tell you what fits in memory. Fig 4.2 lets you build exactly this A-versus-B pair.

Skip connections: how ResNet stays trainable

One structural idea is still unexplained: how does anything with 100+ layers train at all? Backpropagation multiplies gradients layer by layer on the way down, and Part I of the neural-network story already showed what repeated multiplication does to a signal: shrink it a little per layer and after 100 layers essentially nothing arrives. The fix is almost insultingly simple. A skip connection adds the block's input straight to its output,

$$ y = F(x) + x $$

so the block learns only the residual $F$, the correction on top of identity. Two consequences. Forward: even if a block's transformation is nearly useless, the signal passes through intact, so depth cannot strangle the activations. Backward: the derivative of $F(x) + x$ contains a clean "$+\,1$" path, so the gradient always has a highway back to early layers, bypassing the chain of shrinking factors instead of dying in it. Watch the difference:

Fig 4.4, the skip-connection demo. A stack of identical blocks, each passing on a fraction of its incoming signal; slide the depth and toggle skips. Without skips the signal reaching the deepest layers decays geometrically (each bar a fixed fraction of the last); with skips the identity path keeps it alive at any depth. A conceptual model of the effect, one number per layer, but the decay it shows is the real reason plain 100-layer stacks refuse to train.

5. Transfer learning

Everything so far assumed ImageNet-scale data. You will almost never have it. A hospital hands you 2,000 labelled scans, a factory 500 photos of defective parts, and training a 60M-parameter network from scratch on that is a memorization exercise, section 3 already told you why. The practical answer, and the single most used trick in applied deep learning, is transfer learning: do not start from random weights, start from a model someone already trained on lots of general data.

The recipe has three moves. Pre-train: take a large model trained on a big general dataset, in vision almost always an ImageNet classifier; you download this, you do not train it. Freeze: lock most of its weights so they no longer update; those early and middle layers already detect edges, textures, and parts, and section 6 will show they are generic. Fine-tune: chop off the classification head, replace it with a freshly initialized head sized for your classes, and train only the unfrozen tail on your small dataset. The frozen backbone becomes a feature extractor; your data only has to teach the last step. Two dials control how aggressively to do this, and the lecture's guidance is:

  • Fewer new examples: freeze more. Every unfrozen layer is capacity your tiny dataset must fill; with very little data, train the new head only.
  • More dissimilar data: fine-tune more. Microscopy slides or radar maps share less with ImageNet photos than pet pictures do, so deeper layers' photo-specific features need adjusting, unfreeze further down.
  • Use a smaller learning rate than training from scratch. Fine-tuning happens late in training in disguise: the weights already sit near a good optimum, and recall the shape of loss surfaces around optima, big steps near a minimum overshoot and destroy the pre-trained knowledge you came for.

Fig 5.1, the transfer-learning workbench. A pre-trained backbone (five stages plus head) you can freeze or unfreeze stage by stage; the two sliders, how much new data and how similar it is to ImageNet, drive the recommendation panel, which applies the lecture's rules: freeze more when data is scarce, fine-tune deeper when data is dissimilar, and always fine-tune with a smaller learning rate than from-scratch training. The trainable-parameter readout updates live from the stage sizes.

In code, the whole trick is a few lines. Illustrative torchvision-style sketch, the standard shape of every transfer pipeline (this one is for reading, not for the in-page runner): 

# illustrative sketch: pre-trained backbone, frozen, new head
model = torchvision.models.resnet18(weights="IMAGENET1K_V1")  # pre-trained

for p in model.parameters():          # freeze everything
    p.requires_grad = False

model.fc = nn.Linear(512, num_my_classes)   # new head, randomly initialized

optimizer = torch.optim.SGD(
    model.fc.parameters(),            # only the head trains
    lr=0.001,                         # smaller than from-scratch (e.g. 0.1)
)
Check your understanding

You have 800 labelled images of manufacturing defects, visually quite unlike ImageNet photos. Which fine-tuning setup follows the lecture's rules?

800 images against millions of parameters from random initialization is guaranteed memorization. Even for dissimilar data the early layers' edges and textures transfer; you fine-tune deeper, you do not discard the backbone.
Right, and notice you are balancing the two rules against each other: little data says freeze more, dissimilar data says fine-tune more, so you keep the generic early layers frozen, adapt the task-specific later ones, and protect the pre-trained weights with a small learning rate.
The head maps to ImageNet's 1,000 classes; it cannot output "defect type 3". At minimum you must replace and train a new head, and with dissimilar data you should unfreeze some later layers too.

6. What CNNs learn, and interpretability

Transfer learning works because of what the layers learn, and we can look. Visualize the filters of a trained CNN and representation learning becomes visible: early layers learn simple, generic patterns, oriented edges, colour contrasts, textures, essentially the detectors Part I had you design by hand, except learned. Later layers combine them into task-specific parts and objects: eyes, wheels, feathers, whole faces. This is exactly the receptive-field story from Part I made flesh, small fields see edges, large fields see things, and it explains the freezing heuristic from section 5: early layers transfer anywhere, later layers are married to their training task.

Fig 6.1, what filters look like, layer by layer. Procedurally generated pattern swatches in the style of trained-CNN visualizations: oriented edges and colour gradients for early layers, textures and motifs for the middle, part-like and object-like structure for late layers (synthetic sketches, not screenshots of a real network). Click a swatch to see the kind of image patch that would excite it most.

So we can see the filters; can we explain the decisions? Here the lecture turns honest and uncomfortable. Interpretability is hard for a structural reason: there is no ground truth for an explanation. When training a classifier, every image comes with the right answer; when explaining one, nobody hands you the "correct explanation" to compare against, which makes explainability essentially an unsupervised problem. Consequences follow quickly. Explanations can simply be wrong, plausible-looking heatmaps pointing at irrelevant pixels. Worse, some attribution methods are fragile in a way that should alarm you: randomize the network's weights, destroying everything it learned, and the "explanation" sometimes barely changes, which raises the question of what, exactly, it was explaining. And there are often trade-offs between performance and interpretability, the most accurate model is rarely the most transparent one. The bar we actually want is higher than accuracy: predictions that are fair across groups, privacy-preserving, and right for the right reasons.

The sharpest demonstration that accuracy is not understanding is the adversarial example. Take an image the network classifies correctly and confidently. Add a perturbation so small it is invisible to you, but chosen carefully, pointed exactly along the classifier's weak direction, and the network now confidently declares the panda a vulture, the classic example from the lecture. The scene did not change; the pixels moved a hair; the prediction flipped. Do it yourself:

Fig 6.2, the adversarial slider. A small linear classifier (real weights, real scores, built in this page) correctly labels a synthetic stand-in "panda". Slide the perturbation strength: the perturbation, shown magnified because at true scale it is nearly invisible, is aimed at the classifier's weakest direction, and the predicted label flips to "vulture" while the image looks unchanged. Check: why is explainability described as an unsupervised problem?

Use the supervised/unsupervised distinction from the start of the course.

Answer. Supervised learning has targets: for every input we know the desired output, so we can measure error and optimize against it. For explanations there is no such target. Nobody can hand you the true explanation of a network's decision to train against or to grade against, so there is no loss function that says "this explanation is 87% correct". That is the defining condition of an unsupervised problem: structure must be found without labelled ground truth. It is why explanation methods can disagree with each other, why some pass sanity checks (like the weight-randomization test) and others fail, and why we cannot simply "train a better explainer" the way we train a better classifier: we would not know what better means. Trust in explanations therefore has to come from indirect evidence, sanity checks, and human judgment, not from a validation accuracy.

7. Applications: from labels to pixels

Everything in this lecture so far said "classification", one image in, one label out, ImageNet-style: object classification answers what. The other tasks of practical vision are extensions of that question. Object detection answers what and where: find every object and put a bounding box around it, the second ILSVRC task from section 2. And pushing "where" to its logical limit, semantic segmentation answers the question for every single pixel: dense per-pixel classification, where the output is not a label but a full-resolution map assigning each pixel to a class. It is the natural extension of classification and a stepping stone toward scene understanding and visual question answering, systems that must reason about what is where.

The architectures for these tasks are not built from nothing. Modern segmentation models use CNNs as their backbone, and a pre-trained ImageNet classifier is one of the most common building blocks, section 5's transfer recipe applied at the level of whole systems: the classifier's feature hierarchy does the seeing, and a task-specific head turns features into boxes or masks. One scene, two questions:

A self-driving car needs to know which pixels of the camera image are road, which are sidewalk, and which are pedestrians. Which task is this, and what is the output's shape?

"This image contains road" does not say where the road is, and driving is entirely about where. One label per image throws away the spatial answer.

8. Summary and method card

The single sentence to keep: deep vision succeeded when architecture, data, compute, optimization, and validation scaled together, and no one of them was the hero. LeNet had the architecture in 1998 and went nowhere for lack of the rest; ImageNet supplied the data and the scoreboard; GPUs supplied the compute; and a lineage of structural ideas, ReLU-era scaling in AlexNet, fully convolutional design with 1×1 bottlenecks in GoogLeNet, skip connections in ResNet, kept depth trainable until the models passed human accuracy and the benchmark itself retired. Around that core sit the working skills: count parameters when you care about memory and connections when you care about speed; steal a pre-trained backbone and fine-tune it gently instead of training from scratch; expect early layers to be generic and later layers task-specific; and hold your trust loosely, because explanations have no ground truth and an invisible perturbation can flip a confident answer.

9. End-of-lesson quiz

Two quick checks, then three transfer questions that ask you to design and argue, not recall. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Quiz 1

Rank what changed between LeNet-5 (1998) and AlexNet (2012) using the lecture's comparison table: parameters went from ~60K to 60M, training examples from 60K to 1.2M, image size from 32×32 to 256×256×3. What was the enabling change that made training the bigger model possible at all?

The algorithm was the same backpropagation from 1998 (and earlier). What changed was the scale at which it could be run, plus architecture details like ReLU.
Right. A 1,000× parameter jump and a 60× pixel jump multiply into a compute bill only parallel hardware could pay; AlexNet was literally architected around two 3GB gaming cards. Data (ImageNet) made the model worth training, GPUs made it trainable, and neither alone would have sufficed.
Initialization tricks help optimization at the margin, but they do not turn an infeasible compute budget into a feasible one. The binding constraint was hardware.
Quiz 2

A vendor pitches you a "97% accurate" defect classifier and offers a saliency heatmap with each prediction as proof it is trustworthy. Which caution from this lecture applies?

A heatmap is an output of an explanation method, not a certificate. The lecture's sanity-check result cuts deep here: some attribution methods produce nearly the same heatmap for a randomized, know-nothing network.
The number is not the issue; the epistemics are. Even a 99.9% model can be right for wrong reasons (dataset quirks, spurious cues), and the heatmap cannot prove otherwise because no ground-truth explanation exists to validate it against.
Right. Explainability is an unsupervised problem: there is no target explanation to grade against, explanations can be incorrect, and fragile attribution methods fail basic sanity checks. Demand held-out evaluation under deployment conditions, stress tests, and error analysis, and treat the heatmap as a debugging aid, not proof.
Quiz 3 (transfer): design a dataset curation plan

Your team must build an image dataset for classifying 40 species of local birds from trail-camera photos. Propose a curation plan (walk the lecture's six considerations) and name its two biggest risks.

Answer (one good version). Categories: the 40 species, plus an "other/no bird" class so the model has somewhere to put everything else; decide up front whether visually near-identical species get merged or kept fine-grained (fine-grained is harder but more useful, the ImageNet lesson). Samples: aim for hundreds to thousands per class, and balance them; trail cameras will overrepresent common species, so rare ones need targeted collection or augmentation. Resolution: enough to separate species by plumage detail, but fixed and modest, since it multiplies every future compute bill. Annotation: expert-verified labels, or crowd labels with an adjudication step for lookalike species; label noise caps every model trained later. Sourcing: trail cameras photograph whatever walks by, so scrub images capturing people, respect land permissions, and record capture metadata to audit coverage. Preprocessing: normalize sizes and exposure; document everything so training and deployment match. The two biggest risks: label noise on fine-grained lookalikes (garbage in, garbage out) and sourcing bias, e.g. one camera site, season, or time of day dominating, so the model learns backgrounds and lighting instead of birds, accurate in validation and wrong in the field. Both are curation failures no architecture can repair. And given 40 classes and modest data, the training plan should start from a pre-trained backbone, not from scratch.

Quiz 4 (transfer): fine-tune or train from scratch?

For each scenario, choose fine-tuning or training from scratch, and justify with the lecture's rules: (a) 300,000 labelled product photos, task and imagery close to ImageNet; (b) 1,500 labelled ultrasound images; (c) 50 million labelled images of a completely new sensor type (thermal), with a large compute budget.

Answer. (a) Fine-tune, and you may not need much of it: the data is similar to ImageNet, so freeze most of the backbone, train the head (and perhaps the last stage), small learning rate. With 300K examples you could unfreeze more, but starting pre-trained costs nothing and converges faster. (b) Fine-tune, no contest: 1,500 images cannot train a deep network from scratch. The data is dissimilar (ultrasound vs photos), which pushes toward fine-tuning more layers, but the tiny sample size pushes toward freezing more; resolve the tension by freezing the early generic layers, fine-tuning the later ones and the new head, with a small learning rate and heavy augmentation. (c) This is the one case where from-scratch is defensible: data is plentiful, compute is available, and thermal imagery shares little low-level statistics with RGB photos, so the pre-trained early filters are least helpful here. Even so, common practice would still try ImageNet initialization as a baseline, because it rarely hurts and often speeds convergence. The general rule: from-scratch training needs the full ingredient stack from section 0, big data AND big compute; whenever either is missing, transfer.

Quiz 5 (transfer): why adversarial examples worry deployment engineers

Explain, to a product manager, why adversarial examples are a serious concern for deployed vision systems and not just an academic curiosity. Use what Fig 6.2 demonstrated.

Answer. Three points. First, the failure is invisible: the perturbation that flips "panda" to "vulture" is too small for a human to see, so no reviewer looking at the input would ever flag it, and the system fails while appearing to work on perfectly normal-looking data. Second, the failure is constructible: it is not a rare accident but something an adversary can compute on purpose, aiming the perturbation along the model's weak directions exactly as the slider in Fig 6.2 does. For a spam filter, a content moderator, or a vehicle's sign detector, that means an attacker with knowledge of the model can manufacture inputs that defeat it reliably. Third, it reveals what the model actually learned: decision boundaries that pass within a hair's breadth of correctly classified points, in directions no human perceives, which means high test accuracy was never evidence of human-like understanding, echoing the interpretability lesson that accurate is not the same as right for the right reasons. Deployment implications: threat-model your inputs (can an adversary craft them?), monitor confidence and distribution shift, consider robustness testing before shipping, and never treat validation accuracy as a security guarantee.

That is the practice of convolutional networks: an architecture from Part I, multiplied by curated data, parallel compute, and two decades of structural ideas, then made usable at human scale through transfer learning, and made trustworthy only with care, because the models are brittle in ways accuracy does not show.
