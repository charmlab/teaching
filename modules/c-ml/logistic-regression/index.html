Logistic Regression

Last lecture we built classifiers that draw a line and make a call. This lecture the call gets a confidence. Logistic regression keeps the linear score, squashes it through a sigmoid into a probability, and trains with a loss that actually punishes confident mistakes. Getting there takes four honest attempts, and the three failures teach more than the success. Every step has an interactive figure, every equation gets real numbers, and by the end you will have trained the model yourself, by hand and in code.

0. A verdict is not a confidence

The classifiers of last lecture end in a threshold. Compute a linear score, check its sign, announce a class. That machine has two problems, and they are the two problems this whole lecture exists to fix.

First, it cannot tell you how sure it is. A patient whose score is 0.01 and a patient whose score is 47 get the same output: positive. One of those calls is a coin flip and the other is near certainty, and the model has no way to say which is which. Real decisions need that difference. A spam filter that is 51% sure should behave differently from one that is 99.9% sure.

Second, it is hard to train. The threshold is a step function: flat, then a jump, then flat again. Move the weights a little and the output usually does not change at all, so the loss does not change either, so the gradient is zero, so gradient descent, the one optimizer we trust, has nothing to work with. Feel both problems before any math:

Fig 0.1, the problem in one picture. Drag the query along the axis: the hard threshold gives the same blunt verdict whether the query sits on the boundary or a mile from it. Toggle the probabilistic view and the model finally says how sure it is.

What we want is a model that outputs a probability: a number in $[0, 1]$ that means "this is my degree of belief that the class is 1". The central idea of the lecture, and it is worth saying once in plain words: frame classification as learning probabilities, then decide from them. The model that does this is logistic regression, and the cleanest way to understand it is to watch it get invented, one failed attempt at a time.

1. Four tries at a trainable classifier

Set up the problem. Inputs $x$, binary targets $t \in \{0, 1\}$, and the linear score we already trust from the last two lectures: $z = w^\top x$ (with the bias absorbed into $w$ by the usual constant feature). That score has a name I will use from now on: logit. The logit is the raw linear score before anything is done to it. The question of the lecture is what to do with it, and the answer takes four tries.

Try #1: the hard threshold

$$ z = w^\top x, \qquad y = \mathbb{I}[z \ge 0], \qquad L_{0\text{-}1} = \mathbb{I}[y \neq t] $$

Predict class 1 when the logit is non-negative, and count a loss of 1 for every mistake. The zero/one loss is exactly what we care about, the number of errors, and it is exactly what we cannot optimize. As a function of the weights it is flat with jumps: nudging $w$ almost never flips a prediction, so the loss sits still, and where it does flip, the loss jumps discontinuously. The gradient is zero almost everywhere and undefined at the jumps. It kills gradients. Smooth optimization is dead on arrival.

Try #2: least squares on the score

$$ z = w^\top x, \qquad L_{\text{sq}} = \tfrac{1}{2}(z - t)^2 $$

Borrow last lecture's machinery: regress the score directly onto the binary targets with squared loss, then threshold $z$ to decide. Now everything is differentiable and we can train. But we are optimizing the wrong thing. Squared loss wants $z$ to equal the target, not to be on the right side of it. A point that is "too correct", a class-1 example whose score is 5 instead of 1, still incurs loss, so the fit drags the line back toward the pack. One outlier deep in its own correct territory can tilt the whole boundary the wrong way and sacrifice points that were fine.

Try #3: sigmoid plus squared loss

$$ z = w^\top x, \qquad y = \sigma(z), \qquad L_{\text{sq}} = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - t)^2 $$

Fix Try #2's complaint by bounding the output. The sigmoid $\sigma$ (section 2 gives it the full treatment) squashes any real score into $[0,1]$, so "too correct" stops being penalized: once $y$ is near the target it cannot overshoot it. Smooth, bounded, trains the bounded output directly. And yet it fails, more quietly this time. Chain rule on the loss:

$$ \frac{\partial L_{\text{sq}}}{\partial w} = (y - t)\, \underbrace{y(1 - y)}_{\text{can be tiny}}\, x $$

That middle factor $y(1-y)$ is the sigmoid's slope, and it collapses toward zero whenever $y$ is near 0 or 1. Here is the disaster: a confident mistake, say $y \approx 1$ when $t = 0$, is precisely where we need the loudest correction, and it is precisely where $y(1-y) \approx 0$ mutes the gradient. The loss saturates. The model is maximally wrong and the learning signal is almost nothing.

Try #4: sigmoid plus cross-entropy

Keep the model, $y = \sigma(z)$, and change the loss. The cross-entropy loss (also called the log loss) is

$$ L_{CE}(y, t) = -t \log y - (1 - t)\log(1 - y) $$

Read it one case at a time. If $t = 1$, the loss is $-\log y$: near zero when $y$ is near 1, and blowing up toward infinity as $y \to 0$. If $t = 0$, the loss is $-\log(1-y)$: quiet while $y \approx 0$, explosive as $y \to 1$. Cross-entropy reacts sharply to confident mistakes, exactly where squared loss went numb. That asymmetry fixes the learning signal, and section 4 shows it does so with almost suspicious elegance. This combination, a linear logit, a sigmoid, and cross-entropy, is logistic regression.

Worked by hand: cross-entropy's asymmetry, real numbers

Confident and correct. $t = 1$, model says $y = 0.98$:

$$ L_{CE} = -\log(0.98) \approx 0.020 $$

Confident and wrong. $t = 1$, model says $y = 0.02$:

$$ L_{CE} = -\log(0.02) \approx 3.912 $$

Same confidence, opposite verdicts, and the wrong one costs about 194 times more. Cross-entropy shouts.

2. The sigmoid

The function doing the squashing deserves its own section. The sigmoid, also called the logistic function, is

$$ z = w^\top x, \qquad y = \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} \in [0, 1] $$

Trace its behaviour from the formula. As $z \to -\infty$, the $e^{-z}$ in the denominator explodes and $y \to 0$. At $z = 0$, the denominator is exactly 2, so $y = 0.5$: the model is on the fence precisely when the score is on the boundary. As $z \to +\infty$, $e^{-z}$ vanishes and $y \to 1$. An S-shaped ramp from 0 to 1, centred where the score is zero. In the vocabulary you will use for the rest of the course: $z$ is the logit, and $\sigma$ is the activation function, the nonlinearity applied to a linear score.

Worked by hand: the sigmoid at five scores

$\sigma(0) = \frac{1}{1+1} = 0.5$.  $\sigma(2) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-2}} = \frac{1}{1.135} \approx 0.881$.  $\sigma(-2) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{2}} = \frac{1}{8.389} \approx 0.119$.  $\sigma(4) \approx 0.982$.  $\sigma(-4) \approx 0.018$.

Two things to notice and keep. Symmetry: $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$, which you can verify in every pair above. And compression: moving the score from 2 to 4 moves the output only from 0.881 to 0.982. Out on the tails, big changes in $z$ barely move $y$, which is the same flatness that sabotaged Try #3.

In one dimension the model is $y = \sigma(w_0 + w_1 x)$, and the two weights have clean jobs. The bias $w_0$ shifts the curve left and right: the centre sits where $w_0 + w_1 x = 0$, at $x = -w_0 / w_1$. The weight $w_1$ controls steepness: crank it and the ramp sharpens. Push $w_1 \to \infty$ and the sigmoid approaches a hard step, which means Try #1 is hiding inside this model as a limiting case. Logistic regression did not throw the threshold away, it made the threshold soft and therefore trainable. Prove all three claims with the sliders:

Fig 2.1, the sigmoid shaper. Slide $w_0$ to shift the curve, $w_1$ to sharpen or flip it. The marked crossing is where $y = 0.5$, the decision boundary, at $x = -w_0/w_1$. Press the step-limit button and watch $w_1$ climb until the sigmoid is indistinguishable from Try #1's hard threshold.

In code, the sigmoid is one line, and it is the first of four functions this lesson builds:

def sigmoid(z):
    return 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))   # works elementwise on arrays too
Check: what exactly does the step-function limit recover, and what does it cost?

As $w_1 \to \infty$ with the boundary $-w_0/w_1$ held fixed, describe what the model becomes and what is gained or lost.

Answer. The ramp's width shrinks toward zero, so $\sigma(w_0 + w_1 x)$ tends to 0 for $x$ below the boundary and 1 above it: exactly Try #1's hard-threshold classifier at the same boundary. What is lost is everything this lecture wanted. The outputs stop being graded confidences and become blunt verdicts, and the gradient dies: $y(1-y)$ is near zero for every point not sitting on the boundary, so training stalls. Steepness is confidence, and infinite steepness is untrainable overconfidence. You can watch the gradient readout in Fig 2.1 collapse as $w_1$ grows.

3. The probabilistic interpretation

So far the sigmoid is a squashing trick. Here is the reading that turns it into a model. Treat the output as the model's probability that the class is 1:

$$ p(t = 1 \mid x; w) = y = \sigma(w^\top x), \qquad p(t = 0 \mid x; w) = 1 - y $$

The two cases fold into one expression that will do real work in section 4. For $t \in \{0, 1\}$:

$$ p(t \mid x; w) = y^{t} (1 - y)^{1 - t} $$

Check it against both cases, because compact formulas earn trust through arithmetic. If $t = 1$: the expression is $y^1 (1-y)^0 = y$. If $t = 0$: it is $y^0 (1-y)^1 = 1 - y$. The exponents act as a switch, selecting whichever probability matches the observed label.

Under this reading, an output of $y = 0.8$ means "80% probability of the positive class". That sentence carries a promise: the number should behave like a real probability, so that among all the examples where the model says 0.8, about 80% actually turn out positive. An output with that property is called a calibrated probability, and it is what separates a probability from a score that merely lives in $[0, 1]$.

The decision boundary is still a line

Where does the model stop preferring one class over the other? Where it is split evenly: $p = 0.5$. The sigmoid hits 0.5 exactly when its input is zero, so the boundary is

$$ w^\top x + w_0 = 0 $$

which is the same linear boundary we have had since last lecture. Nothing about the geometry changed. What changed is everything around the line: the model now grades the whole plane, confident far from the boundary, uncertain near it, instead of painting two flat regions. A linear boundary, now with probabilities attached.

4. Training: likelihood, cross-entropy, gradient descent

The model assigns a probability to every labelled example: probability $y^{(i)}$ if $t^{(i)} = 1$, and $1 - y^{(i)}$ if $t^{(i)} = 0$. So there is a natural definition of a good weight vector: one under which the training data we actually saw is probable. Multiply the per-example probabilities together (the compact form from section 3 doing its job) to get the likelihood of the training data:

$$ \ell(w) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} \left( y^{(i)} \right)^{t^{(i)}} \left( 1 - y^{(i)} \right)^{1 - t^{(i)}} $$

Maximize that and you have trained the model. But products of hundreds of numbers in $[0,1]$ are numerically miserable and analytically awkward. The standard trick: the logarithm is monotonic, so $f$ and $\log f$ have the same maximizer. Take the log, and the product becomes a sum. Then flip the sign and divide by $N$, because our habit is minimizing averages. What drops out is exactly the loss Try #4 pulled from a hat, now derived rather than guessed, the empirical cross-entropy:

$$ J_{CE}(w) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[ -t^{(i)} \log y^{(i)} - (1 - t^{(i)}) \log\!\left(1 - y^{(i)}\right) \right], \qquad y^{(i)} = \sigma\!\left(w^\top x^{(i)}\right) $$

Maximizing likelihood and minimizing cross-entropy are the same act. There is no closed-form solution this time (the sigmoid saw to that), so we train the way everything after linear regression trains, by gradient descent:

$$ w^{(k+1)} \leftarrow w^{(k)} - \alpha \, \nabla_w J_{CE}(w) $$

The cancellation that justifies everything

Now the payoff for choosing this exact loss. Compute the gradient for one example by the chain rule, through the three stages loss, sigmoid, score:

$$ \frac{\partial L_{CE}}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L_{CE}}{\partial y} \cdot \frac{\partial y}{\partial z} \cdot \frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = \underbrace{\frac{y - t}{y(1-y)}}_{\text{loss}} \cdot \underbrace{y(1-y)}_{\text{sigmoid}} \cdot \underbrace{x\vphantom{\frac{y}{y}}}_{\text{score}} = (y - t)\, x $$

Look at what happened. The sigmoid contributes the slope factor $y(1-y)$, the same factor that saturated Try #3. The cross-entropy contributes its exact reciprocal. They cancel, completely, and the surviving gradient over the dataset is

$$ \nabla_w J_{CE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( y^{(i)} - t^{(i)} \right) x^{(i)} $$

No saturation factor anywhere. A confident mistake has $|y - t| \approx 1$, the largest gradient an example can produce, which is exactly the behaviour we asked for in section 1. And the form should look familiar: prediction minus target, times input. It is the same gradient linear regression had, with $\sigma(w^\top x)$ in place of $w^\top x$. This cancellation is not luck. It is the reason cross-entropy is the loss: it is precisely the loss whose steepness grows to undo the sigmoid's flatness.

Worked by hand: one gradient step, real numbers

One training example with augmented input $x = (1, 2)^\top$ (bias feature first) and label $t = 0$. Current weights $w = (0, 1)^\top$, learning rate $\alpha = 0.1$.

Forward. $z = w^\top x = 0 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 = 2$, so $y = \sigma(2) \approx 0.881$. The model is 88% sure this class-0 point is class 1: a confident mistake. Loss: $-\log(1 - 0.881) = -\log(0.119) \approx 2.13$.

Gradient. $(y - t)\,x = (0.881 - 0)\,(1, 2)^\top = (0.881,\ 1.762)^\top$. Large, as a confident mistake deserves.

Step. $w \leftarrow (0, 1)^\top - 0.1\,(0.881, 1.762)^\top = (-0.088,\ 0.824)^\top$.

Did it help? New score $z = -0.088 + 0.824 \times 2 = 1.560$, new prediction $y = \sigma(1.560) \approx 0.826$, new loss $-\log(0.174) \approx 1.75$, down from 2.13. Still wrong, but less confident about it, and the next step pushes the same way.

The Try #3 comparison. On the same point, the sigmoid+squared gradient is $(y-t)\,y(1-y)\,x = 0.881 \times 0.105 \times (1,2)^\top \approx (0.092,\ 0.185)^\top$, about 9.5 times smaller. Same mistake, one-tenth the correction.

The training loop in code

Everything above compresses into a few honest lines. The loss for bookkeeping, and the loop that does the work: 

def cross_entropy(y, t):
    return np.mean(-t * np.log(y) - (1 - t) * np.log(1 - y))

def fit(X, t, alpha=0.3, epochs=5000):
    w = np.zeros(X.shape[1])
    for _ in range(epochs):
        y = sigmoid(X @ w)              # forward: probabilities
        grad = X.T @ (y - t) / len(t)   # the clean (y - t) x gradient
        w -= alpha * grad               # step downhill
    return w

And here is that exact loop wired to the exam dataset from Fig 3.2, really executing. Change the learning rate, the epochs, or the regularization strength $\lambda$ (a preview of section 5), and rerun it. And try $\alpha = 3$ once, on purpose: the step is too big for this valley, so the loss stops falling and starts bouncing between the walls, the same too-big-step failure you met in Lecture 4:

From probabilities to decisions

One loose end. The model outputs probabilities, but the world eventually demands an action: flag the email or not, treat the patient or not. So the pipeline ends the way it started, with a threshold, now applied to the probability: predict class 1 when $y \ge \tau$. The default is $\tau = 0.5$, matching the boundary of section 3, but $\tau$ is yours to move, and moving it trades one kind of error against the other. Lowering $\tau$ catches more true positives at the cost of more false alarms; raising it does the reverse. The probabilities do not change at all. Only the decision rule does, which is exactly the freedom a probabilistic classifier buys you:

Fig 4.3, the threshold and what it costs. Every training point is placed at its predicted probability; drag the threshold $\tau$ through them and watch the confusion matrix and the precision, recall, and F1 readouts update live. The model is fixed the whole time. Only your decision rule moves.

5. Regularization

Logistic regression inherits linear regression's disease. With many features, or few examples, or a feature map that bends the boundary, the fit can chase noise, and the symptom is the same as before: weights ballooning to extreme values. There is an extra twist specific to classification: bigger weights mean a steeper sigmoid, which means more confidence everywhere, so an unregularized fit on accommodating data keeps inflating its weights to claim certainty it has not earned. The cure is the one from Lecture 4, an L2 penalty on the weights:

$$ J_{\text{reg}}(w) = J_{CE}(w) + \lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2 $$

The first term wants to fit the labels. The second keeps the weights small, which keeps the sigmoid's ramp gentle and the probabilities humble, and it is the standard defence when features are many or data is limited. The hyperparameter $\lambda$ sets the exchange rate, and you choose it the honest way, on a validation split:

Fig 5.1, the regularization dial. The model uses quadratic features so the boundary can bend, and three training labels are deliberately noisy. Slide $\lambda$: at zero the boundary contorts around the noise with high confidence; as $\lambda$ grows the weight bars shrink, the boundary relaxes, and validation loss dips before underfitting sets in. Every fit is computed for real.

The Bayesian view, in one paragraph

The penalty looks like an engineering hack. It has a principled reading, marked as extra reading in the slides but worth thirty seconds. Suppose that before seeing any data you believed small weights were more plausible than large ones, encoded as a prior distribution $p(w) \propto \exp(-\lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2)$, a Gaussian centred at zero. Bayes' rule combines that belief with the data's likelihood into a posterior, $p(w \mid t, X) \propto p(t \mid X; w)\, p(w)$. Take the negative log of the posterior and the likelihood term becomes our cross-entropy while the prior term becomes exactly $\lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2$. So minimizing the regularized objective is MAP estimation, maximum a posteriori: finding the most probable weights given the data and a Gaussian preference for small ones. L2 regularization is a prior wearing a loss function's clothes.

Check your understanding

You swap the L2 penalty $\lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2$ for an L1 penalty $\lambda \sum_j |w_j|$. What does L1 do to the weights that L2 does not, and why is that useful?

Both penalties push weights toward zero, never away from it. They differ in how they push.
Right. L2's pull weakens as a weight approaches zero (the gradient $2\lambda w$ vanishes), so weights get small but almost never exactly zero. L1's pull is a constant $\lambda$ all the way down, so weak features are snapped to exactly zero and drop out of the model entirely. That sparsity is the lasso, introduced by Robert Tibshirani, and it doubles as automatic feature selection: the surviving nonzero weights name the features that matter.
The penalties' shapes matter enormously near zero. One has a rounded floor, the other a kink, and the kink is what pins weights at exactly zero.

6. Softmax: more than two classes

Everything so far is binary. The generalization to $K$ classes is almost embarrassingly direct, and the slides file it under extra reading, but you will meet it in every neural network for the rest of the course, so build it now. Give each class its own score, a full linear model per class:

$$ z_k = w_k^\top x + w_{k0}, \qquad k = 1, \dots, K $$

Now convert $K$ raw scores into a probability distribution. The recipe is the softmax: exponentiate every score (making everything positive and amplifying gaps), then normalize so the results sum to 1:

$$ p(C_k \mid x) = \frac{\exp(z_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(z_j)}, \qquad \sum_{k=1}^{K} p(C_k \mid x) = 1 $$

Predict the most probable class, $\arg\max_k p(C_k \mid x)$, and train by the exact logic of section 4: maximize the likelihood of the observed labels, which means minimizing the multi-class cross-entropy, $-\log p(C_{\text{true}} \mid x)$ averaged over the data. The correspondence to keep: binary logistic regression is one score into a sigmoid, out comes the probability of class 1. Multi-class is one score per class into a softmax, out comes a distribution over all the classes. The sigmoid is the $K = 2$ special case in disguise.

Worked by hand: a softmax over three scores

Scores $z = (2,\ 1,\ -1)$.

Exponentiate. $e^{2} \approx 7.389$, $e^{1} \approx 2.718$, $e^{-1} \approx 0.368$. Sum: $7.389 + 2.718 + 0.368 = 10.475$.

Normalize. $p(C_1) = 7.389 / 10.475 \approx 0.705$,  $p(C_2) = 2.718 / 10.475 \approx 0.259$,  $p(C_3) = 0.368 / 10.475 \approx 0.035$.

Check. $0.705 + 0.259 + 0.035 = 0.999 \approx 1$ (rounding). It always sums to exactly 1 by construction: the denominator is the sum of the numerators.

Use it. The prediction is $\arg\max$, class 1. If the true class is 1, the per-example loss is $-\log(0.705) \approx 0.349$. If the true class had been 3, the loss would be $-\log(0.035) \approx 3.35$: the same confident-mistake asymmetry as the binary case.

The fourth and final function of the lesson:

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max())   # subtract the max for numerical safety
    return e / e.sum()
Check: compute a softmax yourself, then write the loss

A three-class model produces scores $z = (1,\ 3,\ 0)$ for some input. Compute the softmax probabilities, verify they sum to 1, state the predicted class, and write the negative log-likelihood if the true class is class 3.

Answer. Exponentials: $e^1 \approx 2.718$, $e^3 \approx 20.086$, $e^0 = 1$; sum $\approx 23.804$. Probabilities: $p(C_1) \approx 2.718/23.804 = 0.114$, $p(C_2) \approx 20.086/23.804 = 0.844$, $p(C_3) \approx 1/23.804 = 0.042$. Sum: $0.114 + 0.844 + 0.042 = 1.000$. Predicted class: $\arg\max = C_2$. If the truth is $C_3$, the negative log-likelihood is $-\log p(C_3) = -\log(0.042) \approx 3.17$, a heavy penalty, because the model put only 4.2% of its belief on the right answer.

7. Summary and method card

The whole model is one pipeline, each stage earned by a failure in section 1. A linear score $z = w^\top x + w_0$, because linear models are trainable and interpretable. A sigmoid $y = \sigma(z)$, because decisions need graded confidence, not verdicts. Cross-entropy training by gradient descent, because it is the likelihood in disguise and its gradient $(y - t)\,x$ never saturates. And a threshold $\tau$ at the very end, because actions are discrete even when beliefs are not. Logistic regression is a probabilistic linear classifier: honest probability estimates attached to a boundary that is still a straight line in whatever feature space you chose.

Method card · Logistic regression
AssumptionThe log-odds of the positive class are linear in the features.
Model classA linear score through a sigmoid: $p(t{=}1 \mid x) = \sigma(w^\top x + w_0)$. Boundary at $p = 0.5$, a hyperplane.
Learning / selectionMinimize binary cross-entropy (maximum likelihood), usually by gradient-based optimization, with regularization; pick $\lambda$ on validation data.
PredictionProbability $y = \sigma(w^\top x + w_0)$; classify by thresholding at $\tau$ (default 0.5, movable when error costs are asymmetric).
StrengthsFast, interpretable (each weight shifts the log-odds), probabilistic, a strong binary baseline.
LimitationsThe boundary is linear in the chosen feature space; probabilities may need calibration before you trust them.
Next stepSoftmax for multi-class, or nonlinear feature maps and nonlinear models.

Where this sits in the course story: similarity (kNN), rules (decision trees), parametric regression, linear classification, and now the first differentiable classifier, trained end to end by gradient descent on a principled loss. That template, score, activation, cross-entropy, gradients, is not a stop on the way to neural networks. It is the first neural network, one neuron large, and the coming lectures mostly stack more of it.

8. End-of-lesson quiz

Two quick checks, then three transfer questions that ask you to use the ideas, not recite them. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Quiz 1

A colleague trains a sigmoid classifier with squared loss. Early on it improves, then it plants a confident wrong prediction on a mislabelled point and never fixes it, even after thousands more epochs. Diagnosis?

A high $\alpha$ causes oscillation or divergence, loss jumping around, not a frozen mistake. This model is calm and stuck, a different signature.
More data dilutes the point's influence but does not restore its gradient. The muting factor is in the loss's own chain rule.
Right, and you reproduced this in Fig 4.1: sabotaged sigmoid+squared training barely moves, while cross-entropy recovers, because its loss derivative exactly cancels the $y(1-y)$ factor.
Quiz 2

One feature in your dataset is measured in millimetres (values around 10,000) and another in metres (values around 10). You train logistic regression with gradient descent and it crawls. Why does scaling matter here when it never mattered for decision trees?

Exactly. Look at the gradient $(y - t)x$: the feature's raw scale multiplies straight into the update. Mismatched scales stretch the loss bowl into a canyon, and one global $\alpha$ cannot suit both the steep and the shallow directions. This is why scaling matters for optimization-based models generally.
The sigmoid saturates gracefully at extreme scores rather than overflowing (with the usual numerically safe implementation). The real damage is to the geometry of optimization.
kNN suffers through its distance metric, and optimization-based models suffer through their gradients. Different mechanism, same prescription: standardize.
Quiz 3 (transfer): derive the log-odds form, then defend the weights' interpretability

Starting from $p = \sigma(w^\top x + w_0)$, solve for $\log \frac{p}{1-p}$, and explain what each weight means.

Answer. Write $z = w^\top x + w_0$, so $p = 1/(1 + e^{-z})$. Rearranged: $1 + e^{-z} = 1/p$, so $e^{-z} = (1-p)/p$, so $-z = \log\frac{1-p}{p}$, and finally

$$ \log \frac{p}{1-p} = w^\top x + w_0 $$

The quantity on the left is the log-odds: odds are $p/(1-p)$, the ratio of belief for to belief against, and the model makes their logarithm exactly linear in the features. This is the assumption on the method card, derived rather than asserted. Interpretability falls out: increase feature $x_j$ by one unit, holding the rest fixed, and the log-odds change by exactly $w_j$, equivalently the odds multiply by $e^{w_j}$, regardless of where you started. A weight of 0.7 means each unit of that feature roughly doubles the odds ($e^{0.7} \approx 2.01$). Sign gives direction, magnitude gives strength, and zero means irrelevant, which is why section 5's L1 penalty doubles as feature selection.

Quiz 4 (transfer): when do you move the threshold off 0.5, and what does it trade?

Give a scenario for lowering $\tau$, a scenario for raising it, and name the quantity being traded.

Answer. Move $\tau$ whenever the two kinds of error have different costs, which is nearly always. Lower it (say $\tau = 0.2$) when missing a positive is expensive: cancer screening, fraud detection, smoke alarms. You flag anything with even modest probability, catching more true positives (recall rises) while accepting more false alarms (precision falls). Raise it (say $\tau = 0.9$) when acting on a false positive is expensive: auto-blocking a user's account, sending police to an address. Now flags are rare but trustworthy: precision rises, recall falls. The trade is precision against recall, false alarms against misses, and you walked it with your own hands in Fig 4.3. The deeper point: the model did not change, so training and deciding are separable problems. Learn calibrated probabilities once; let each application choose its own $\tau$ from its own cost structure.

Quiz 5 (transfer): logistic regression is a one-neuron neural network. Unpack that.

Map each part of logistic regression onto the anatomy of a single artificial neuron, then say what stacking would add.

Answer. A neuron computes a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias, then applies a nonlinearity. That is logistic regression, term for term: the weights $w$ are the connection strengths, $w_0$ is the bias, the logit $z = w^\top x + w_0$ is the pre-activation, and the sigmoid is the activation function producing the output $y \in [0,1]$. Training matches too: cross-entropy loss, gradients by the chain rule (the three-factor product in section 4 is backpropagation through one unit), weight updates by gradient descent. What one neuron cannot do is bend its boundary: the decision surface is a hyperplane, full stop. Stacking fixes exactly that. Feed the input through a layer of such units and their outputs become learned features; a second layer draws a linear boundary in that learned space, which is a nonlinear boundary in the original one. Section 5's quadratic features did this by hand; a network learns the feature map itself. Everything in this lesson, the sigmoid, the cross-entropy, the $(y-t)x$ gradient, gets reused verbatim when we build networks.

That is logistic regression: four tries, three instructive failures, and a model whose every part is there for a defensible reason. A linear score for trainability, a sigmoid for graded belief, cross-entropy because it is the likelihood and because its gradient refuses to saturate, a threshold you place deliberately, a penalty that is secretly a prior, and a softmax waiting when two classes become many.
