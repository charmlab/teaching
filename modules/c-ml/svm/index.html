Support Vector Machines

A linear classifier draws one line and stops. But on separable data there are infinitely many lines that get every training point right, and they are not equally trustworthy: some pass so close to the data that a whisper of noise would flip a label. This lesson builds the classifier that takes the geometry seriously. It picks the boundary with the widest empty buffer, discovers that only a handful of points actually determine it, learns to forgive messy data with slack, and then pulls one trick, the kernel, that bends straight lines into circles. Every idea comes with a figure you can grab.

0. Infinitely many perfect lines

Take a two-class dataset that a line can separate. How many separating lines are there? Infinitely many. Tilt a working line by a hair, or slide it a millimetre, and it still gets every training point right. Training accuracy cannot tell them apart: every one of them scores 100%. So the question that launches this lecture is not "can I separate the data", it is "which of the infinitely many perfect separators should I trust on tomorrow's data?"

Here is the intuition to hold onto. A line that squeaks past the training points is living dangerously. New samples land near old ones, and a boundary that clears the data by a wide buffer keeps classifying them correctly when they wobble. A boundary that skims the data flips labels at the first hint of noise. Try it yourself. Below are three candidate boundaries on the same separable data; each one is scored by its margin, the buffer between the boundary and the closest training point:

Fig 0.1, three perfect classifiers, three very different buffers. Drag any endpoint to reshape a line; each line's margin (distance to its nearest point, computed live) updates in the readout, and the dashed tick marks the point that limits it. All three separate the training data. Only one of them would survive noisy test data comfortably.

The rest of the lesson is what happens when you take "pick the widest buffer" seriously. Section 1 turns it into an optimization problem. Section 2 finds the surprise hiding in its solution: most of the training data is irrelevant. Section 4 handles data that no line can separate cleanly, and section 5 handles data that no line can separate at all.

1. Max-margin: the widest buffer

The model itself is the same linear classifier you already know. Compute a score, take its sign:

$$ z = w^\top x + b, \qquad y = \operatorname{sign}(z) $$

One notation change from logistic regression, and it is load-bearing: labels are now $t \in \{-1, +1\}$ instead of $\{0, 1\}$. The payoff is a one-expression test for correctness. A point is correctly classified exactly when its label and its score agree in sign, that is, when $t^{(i)}(w^\top x^{(i)} + b) > 0$. One product tells you everything: positive means right, negative means wrong, and its size measures how confidently right or wrong.

The big idea of the lecture: keep this classifier, but among all parameter settings that classify the training data correctly, choose the boundary that is most robust to small changes in the data, the one with the largest margin. That is the whole design philosophy, and everything below is bookkeeping to make it precise.

Measuring the buffer: the two margin hyperplanes

Scores give us a natural way to draw the buffer. The decision boundary is the set of points where $z = 0$. Now draw two more lines, the level sets $w^\top x + b = +1$ and $w^\top x + b = -1$. These are the margin hyperplanes, and the strip between them is the margin. A point with score $z \in [-1, 1]$ lies inside that strip (the classifier still predicts with $\operatorname{sign}(z)$ there; the strip is a buffer, not a dead zone).

How wide is the strip? Walk from the $-1$ line to the $+1$ line along the perpendicular direction $\hat{w} = w / \lVert w \rVert$. Each unit step in that direction changes the score by exactly $\lVert w \rVert$, because $w^\top \hat{w} = \lVert w \rVert$. The total score change across the strip is $2$, so the distance walked, the margin width, is

$$ M = \frac{2}{\lVert w \rVert} $$

Read that formula twice, because it is the engine of the whole lecture: a small weight vector means a wide margin. Watch it happen:

Fig 1.1, where $2/\lVert w \rVert$ comes from. The three lines are the level sets $w^\top x + b = -1, 0, +1$. Slide $\lVert w \rVert$: the boundary does not move, but the $\pm 1$ lines do, because a longer $w$ makes the score climb faster per step. Shrink $\lVert w \rVert$ and the buffer balloons.

Worked by hand: margin width for a given w

Take $w = (3, 4)^\top$ with any offset $b$. Then

$$ \lVert w \rVert = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5, \qquad M = \frac{2}{5} = 0.4 $$

Now rescale to $w' = (0.6,\, 0.8)^\top$, $b' = b/5$. The decision boundary $w'^\top x + b' = 0$ is the same line (both sides just got divided by 5), but $\lVert w' \rVert = 1$, so $M = 2$. Same boundary, five times the margin? Not for free: the $\pm 1$ hyperplanes moved outward, and the data now has to clear them. That requirement is what stops $w$ from shrinking to zero, and it is the constraint in the program below.

The hard-margin program

Two requirements pin down the optimal parameters. First, every training point must be correctly classified with a full margin, meaning it clears its margin hyperplane: $t^{(i)}(w^\top x^{(i)} + b) \ge 1$ for all $i$. Second, the margin $M = 2/\lVert w \rVert$ should be as large as possible. Maximizing $2/\lVert w \rVert$ is the same as minimizing $\lVert w \rVert$, and minimizing $\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2$ is the same again but differentiable everywhere, so the optimization we actually pose is:

$$ \min_{w, b} \; \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad t^{(i)}(w^\top x^{(i)} + b) \ge 1 \;\; \forall i $$

This is the hard-margin support vector machine: "hard" because the constraints demand perfect separation with a full buffer, no exceptions. It is a quadratic objective with linear constraints, a convex problem with one global optimum. Note what kind of animal this is: unlike least squares there is no closed-form formula, and unlike logistic regression it is not an unconstrained problem you can just run gradient descent on. It is a constrained optimization, and section 3 shows the machinery built for exactly that. First, though, the solution has a structure worth meeting in person.

2. Support vectors: the points that matter

Think about which constraints in that program actually do any work. A point sitting far from the boundary satisfies $t z \ge 1$ with room to spare; its constraint is slack, and deleting it would change nothing. The only constraints that bite are the ones that hold with equality, the points sitting exactly on the margin hyperplanes. Those points are called support vectors: they support the margin the way pillars support a roof. Everything else in the training set could move around, or vanish entirely, and as long as it stays outside the buffer the optimal boundary would not shift by a hair.

Solving the training problem makes this literal. The solution (section 3 derives it) hands back one number per training point, a weight $\alpha^{(i)} \ge 0$, and the optimal weight vector is built from the data itself:

$$ w = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha^{(i)}\, t^{(i)}\, x^{(i)} $$

Here is the punchline: for every point that is not a support vector, $\alpha^{(i)} = 0$. The support vectors are precisely the points with nonzero weight, $S = \{\, i : \alpha^{(i)} > 0 \,\}$, and in the hard-margin case they lie exactly on the margin. Prediction on a new point $\hat{x}$ therefore consults only them:

$$ y = \operatorname{sign}\!\left( b + \sum_{i \in S} \alpha^{(i)}\, t^{(i)}\, x^{(i)\top} \hat{x} \right) $$

A dataset of a million points might be summarized by a dozen. Now feel it. This is the signature figure of the lesson: drag the boundary yourself and watch your buffer, then let the machine solve for the widest one, then grab individual points and learn, physically, which ones matter:

Fig 2.1, the max-margin explorer. Press Solve and the exact max-margin boundary appears with its support vectors ringed and $M = 2/\lVert w \rVert$ live. Give the geometric description and the algebraic description, and say what the other points contribute.

Answer. Geometrically (hard-margin case): the training points that lie exactly on the margin hyperplanes $w^\top x + b = \pm 1$, the closest points to the boundary; they are the ringed points in Fig 2.1. You can delete every non-support point and retrain; the classifier that comes back is identical.

3. Optional depth: Lagrangians and the dual

Optional depth · the slides mark this "interested reader, not on the exam"

You can use SVMs without this section. But two ideas here explain why support vectors exist and why kernels will be possible, so it earns its place as an aside.

Constrained minima live on boundaries

An unconstrained minimum sits where the function is flat, $\nabla f = 0$: roll downhill until nothing pushes you anywhere. A constrained minimum is different. It is the best legal point, and often the unconstrained optimum is illegal, so the answer gets pressed up against the edge of the feasible set, at a point where a contour of $f$ just touches the constraint boundary. The SVM is exactly this situation: the unconstrained minimizer of $\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2$ is $w = 0$, which classifies nothing, so the solution lands on the constraint boundary, where some points hold $t z = 1$ with equality. Those are the support vectors, seen through an optimization lens.

The Lagrangian, and a game

The standard machine for constraints. Write each constraint as $g_i(x) \le 0$ and price violations with a multiplier $\lambda_i \ge 0$:

$$ L(x, \lambda) = f(x) + \sum_i \lambda_i\, g_i(x), \qquad \lambda_i \ge 0 $$

The constrained problem becomes a min-max, $\min_x \max_{\lambda \ge 0} L(x, \lambda)$, called the primal. The story is a game between two players. The optimizer picks $x$ to make $L$ small. The enforcer picks the multipliers to make it large, and the enforcer's only leverage is violation: if $g_i(x) > 0$ they can send $\lambda_i \to \infty$ and blow the objective up, so the optimizer is forced to stay feasible. If instead $g_i(x) < 0$ strictly, the term $\lambda_i g_i$ is negative and the enforcer's best move is $\lambda_i = 0$. That last observation has a name, complementary slackness, and it is exactly why non-support points get $\alpha = 0$.

Swap the players and you get the dual, $\max_{\lambda \ge 0} \min_x L(x, \lambda)$. Weak duality always holds: the dual value never exceeds the primal value (moving second is an advantage, so the inner minimizer drags the dual down). Strong duality is equality of the two, which holds under regularity conditions, and the hard-margin SVM, being convex with affine constraints, qualifies. So we are allowed to solve the dual instead of the primal, and for SVMs the dual is the better door.

The SVM dual

Run the machinery on the hard-margin program (multipliers renamed $\alpha$, one per training point) and out comes:

$$ \max_{\alpha} \;\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha^{(i)} \;-\; \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j=1}^{N} \alpha^{(i)} \alpha^{(j)}\, t^{(i)} t^{(j)}\, x^{(i)\top} x^{(j)} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \alpha^{(i)} \ge 0, \;\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} \alpha^{(i)} t^{(i)} = 0 $$

with the optimal weights recovered as $w = \sum_i \alpha^{(i)} t^{(i)} x^{(i)}$, the formula section 2 used. Now look closely at where the data appears in the dual. Not as raw coordinates. Only inside the products $x^{(i)\top} x^{(j)}$, dot products between pairs of training points. The dual never needs to know where the points are, only how similar every pair is. File that away: in section 5 we will swap those dot products for something more interesting, and the entire algorithm will come along for the ride.

Check (optional depth): why can a point strictly outside the margin never be a support vector?

Argue it from complementary slackness.

Answer. Write the SVM constraint as $g_i(w, b) = 1 - t^{(i)}(w^\top x^{(i)} + b) \le 0$. At the optimum, complementary slackness holds for each point: $\alpha^{(i)} g_i = 0$, so at least one factor is zero. A point strictly outside the margin has $t^{(i)} z^{(i)} > 1$, hence $g_i < 0$ strictly, hence $\alpha^{(i)} = 0$: the enforcer never prices a constraint that is not tight. With $\alpha^{(i)} = 0$ the point contributes nothing to $w = \sum_i \alpha^{(i)} t^{(i)} x^{(i)}$, so it cannot be a support vector. The game version: the enforcer only pushes on points that are actually leaning against the fence.

4. Soft margins: paying for violations

The hard-margin program has a brittle failure mode: if even one training point sits on the wrong side, or merely inside the buffer, there is no feasible solution at all. Real data is rarely perfectly separable. Labels are noisy, classes overlap, and a single mislabelled point should not be allowed to veto the entire classifier, or worse, wrench the boundary into a terrible position just to satisfy its constraint.

The fix is to make violations legal but expensive. Give every training point a slack variable $\xi_i \ge 0$ measuring how badly it misses the full-margin requirement, relax each constraint by that amount, and charge for the total slack in the objective:

$$ \min_{w, b, \xi} \;\; \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2 + C \sum_{i=1}^{N} \xi_i \quad \text{s.t.} \quad t^{(i)}(w^\top x^{(i)} + b) \ge 1 - \xi_i, \;\; \xi_i \ge 0 $$

This is the soft-margin SVM. The slack value tells you exactly where a point sits, and the four regimes are worth memorizing:

  • $\xi_i = 0$: outside the margin or exactly on it, correctly classified. The point needed no forgiveness.
  • $0 < \xi_i < 1$: inside the margin but still on the correct side. Correct, but inside the buffer.
  • $\xi_i = 1$: sitting exactly on the decision boundary, ambiguous.
  • $\xi_i > 1$: on the wrong side, misclassified.

A useful consequence falls out immediately: every misclassified point has $\xi_i > 1$, so the number of training errors is at most $\sum_i \xi_i$. The total slack is an upper bound on the error count, which is why penalizing it is a sane proxy for penalizing mistakes.

Worked by hand: read off each point's slack regime

Boundary: $w = (1, 1)^\top$, $b = -3$, so $z = x_1 + x_2 - 3$ and the margin width is $M = 2/\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41$. Classify four points, all with true label $t = +1$, using $\xi = \max(0,\, 1 - t z)$:

x = (3, 2):    z = 3+2−3 = 2,  tz = 2,   ξ = 0   → outside the margin, correct
x = (2, 1.5):  z = 0.5,         tz = 0.5, ξ = 0.5 → inside the margin, correct side
x = (1.5, 1.5): z = 0,           tz = 0,   ξ = 1   → on the boundary, ambiguous
x = (1, 1):    z = −1,         tz = −1,  ξ = 2   → wrong side, misclassified

Total slack $\sum_i \xi_i = 0 + 0.5 + 1 + 2 = 3.5$, and indeed the actual number of clear errors, 1, sits below that bound.

C: the price of a violation

The hyperparameter $C > 0$ sets the exchange rate between the two things the objective wants: a wide margin (small $\lVert w \rVert^2$) and few violations (small $\sum \xi_i$). Large $C$ makes violations expensive, so the optimizer buys a narrow margin that almost nothing crosses. Small $C$ makes violations cheap, so the optimizer buys an enormous margin and lets points pile up inside it. In the dual, the entire change is one line: the multipliers get capped, $0 \le \alpha^{(i)} \le C$. Slide the price and watch the trade happen, with every point coloured by its slack regime:

Fig 4.1, the C dial on genuinely non-separable data. Each setting of $C$ retrains a real soft-margin SVM; the readout shows the true margin width $2/\lVert w \rVert$, the total slack $\sum \xi_i$, and the error count it bounds. Rings mark each point's slack regime. Small $C$: wide margin, small weights, slack everywhere. Large $C$: narrow margin, violations nearly forbidden.

Check: the two limits of C, in full (margin, weights, slack)

For linearly separable data, describe the soft-margin solution as $C \to \infty$ and as $C \to 0$: what happens to the margin, the weights, and the slack variables?

Answer. $C \to \infty$ (separable data): each unit of slack costs infinitely much, so the optimum sets every $\xi_i = 0$. The constraints collapse back to $t^{(i)} z^{(i)} \ge 1$ and the problem becomes the hard-margin program: finite $\lVert w \rVert$, margin equal to the widest separating buffer, support vectors on the margin. $C \to 0$: slack is free, so the objective reduces to minimizing $\tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2$ with no effective constraints. The weights collapse, $w \to 0$, so the margin width $2/\lVert w \rVert \to \infty$; the score of every point heads toward the constant $b$, so the slacks head toward $\xi_i = 1 - t^{(i)} b$, roughly 1 on average, and the classifier degenerates toward predicting a single class by the sign of $b$. Neither extreme is a model you would ship; $C$ is a knob you tune on validation data, exactly like $k$ in KNN.

5. Kernels: nonlinear boundaries, linear machinery

Slack forgives noise near a boundary that is basically right. It cannot rescue a boundary that is the wrong shape. Picture one class sitting in a disk and the other class wrapped around it in a ring, two concentric circles. No line separates them, and no amount of slack fixes that, because the failure is not noise, it is geometry: the true boundary is a circle and our model can only draw lines.

Feature maps: change the space, not the model

Here is the move, and it is one of the great ideas in machine learning. Keep the linear classifier. Change the coordinates. A feature map $\phi$ transforms each input $x \mapsto \phi(x)$ into a higher-dimensional space where a linear boundary can separate the data; a flat cut in feature space then folds back into a curved boundary in input space. For the circles the right extra feature is staring at us: the squared radius $r^2 = x_1^2 + x_2^2$, since the inner class has small $r^2$ and the ring has large $r^2$. Lift with $\phi(x) = (x_1,\, x_2,\, x_1^2 + x_2^2)$ and the two classes separate by height. Press the button:

Fig 5.1, the lift. Two concentric classes, hopeless for a line in 2-D. Lift each point to height $x_1^2 + x_2^2$ and a flat plane separates them; drag to orbit the 3-D view. Drop the plane back down and the cut $x_1^2 + x_2^2 = c$ lands in the input space as a circle of radius $\sqrt{c}$, with $c$ computed from the actual data gap. Linear in feature space, nonlinear in input space.

One honest caution before the enthusiasm. With enough hand-built features you can separate any finite training set, in the extreme by giving every training point its own indicator feature and memorizing the data. Separating the training set is cheap; the lift only earns its keep when the new coordinates capture structure that also holds for tomorrow's data. Generalization has to be checked, not assumed, and the tuning figure at the end of this section shows exactly how an over-flexible feature space fails.

The kernel trick: similarity without coordinates

Feature maps have a cost problem. Useful maps explode the dimension: a degree-$d$ polynomial map over many inputs generates combinatorially many features, and we would have to build and store every lifted vector. Now recall the observation from section 3: the SVM dual, and the prediction rule, touch the data only through dot products. In feature space, that means we only ever need $\phi(u)^\top \phi(v)$, a single number per pair, never the coordinates of $\phi(u)$ themselves. So define a kernel, a similarity function that computes that number directly from the raw inputs:

$$ K(u, v) = \phi(u)^\top \phi(v) $$

If $K$ is cheap to evaluate, we get the geometry of the huge feature space at the price of the small input space. That bargain is the kernel trick. The classic example, and the one worth expanding once by hand, is the polynomial kernel $K(x, y) = (1 + x^\top y)^d$. Take $d = 2$ in two dimensions and just expand the square:

$$ (1 + x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2)^2 = 1 + x_1^2 y_1^2 + x_2^2 y_2^2 + 2 x_1 y_1 + 2 x_2 y_2 + 2 x_1 x_2 y_1 y_2 $$

Stare at the right side: it is exactly $\phi(x)^\top \phi(y)$ for the six-dimensional feature map

$$ \phi(x) = \left(1,\; x_1^2,\; x_2^2,\; \sqrt{2}\,x_1,\; \sqrt{2}\,x_2,\; \sqrt{2}\,x_1 x_2\right) $$

which contains the raw coordinates, their squares, and their product: enough to express circles, ellipses, and parabolas as linear cuts. We never built that six-vector. Squaring one number smuggled in the whole thing.

Worked by hand: the trick, verified on real numbers

Take $x = (1, 2)^\top$ and $y = (2, 1)^\top$.

Direct route. $x^\top y = 1 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 = 4$, so $K(x, y) = (1 + 4)^2 = 25$. Three multiplications and a square.

Long route. Build both feature vectors: $\phi(x) = (1,\, 1,\, 4,\, \sqrt{2},\, 2\sqrt{2},\, 2\sqrt{2})$ and $\phi(y) = (1,\, 4,\, 1,\, 2\sqrt{2},\, \sqrt{2},\, 2\sqrt{2})$. Their dot product:

$$ 1 + 4 + 4 + \underbrace{\sqrt{2}\cdot 2\sqrt{2}}_{4} + \underbrace{2\sqrt{2}\cdot \sqrt{2}}_{4} + \underbrace{2\sqrt{2}\cdot 2\sqrt{2}}_{8} = 25 $$

Same answer, 25, and the long route had to construct two six-dimensional vectors to get it. At degree 10 with a hundred input features, the long route is astronomically wide and the direct route is still one dot product and one power.

Fig 5.2, the kernel trick, verified live. Drag $x$ and $y$; both routes are recomputed on every move, the cheap $(1 + x^\top y)^2$ and the expensive $\phi(x)^\top \phi(y)$ with the six-vectors shown in full. They agree to machine precision at every position, which is the whole trick.

The RBF kernel: similarity that decays with distance

The most used kernel of all skips the feature map entirely and just writes down a sensible similarity. The RBF kernel (radial basis function, also called the Gaussian kernel) says two points are similar when they are close:

$$ K(u, v) = \exp\!\left( -\gamma\, \lVert u - v \rVert^2 \right), \qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{2\sigma^2} $$

It equals 1 when $u = v$, decays toward 0 as the points separate, and $\gamma$ sets how fast: large $\gamma$ means similarity dies within a tiny neighbourhood, small $\gamma$ means everyone is somewhat similar to everyone. The remarkable fact is that this one-line formula corresponds to a feature map into an infinite-dimensional space (the end quiz unpacks why, via the Taylor series of the exponential). An infinitely expressive feature space from a formula you can compute on a napkin.

Worked by hand: RBF for a near pair and a far pair

Take $\gamma = 0.5$.

Near pair. $u = (1, 1)$, $v = (1.2,\, 0.9)$: $\lVert u - v \rVert^2 = 0.2^2 + 0.1^2 = 0.05$, so $K = e^{-0.5 \times 0.05} = e^{-0.025} \approx 0.975$. Almost maximally similar.

Far pair. $u = (1, 1)$, $v = (4, 3)$: $\lVert u - v \rVert^2 = 3^2 + 2^2 = 13$, so $K = e^{-0.5 \times 13} = e^{-6.5} \approx 0.0015$. Essentially strangers.

And always $K(u, u) = e^{0} = 1$: every point is perfectly similar to itself.

The kernelized SVM

Now cash the cheque. Take the dual from section 3 and replace every input-space dot product $x^{(i)\top} x^{(j)}$ with a kernel evaluation:

$$ \max_{\alpha} \;\; \sum_i \alpha^{(i)} - \tfrac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \alpha^{(i)} \alpha^{(j)}\, t^{(i)} t^{(j)}\, K\!\left(x^{(i)}, x^{(j)}\right) $$

subject to the same constraints as before, $\sum_i \alpha^{(i)} t^{(i)} = 0$ with $\alpha^{(i)} \ge 0$ in the hard case or $0 \le \alpha^{(i)} \le C$ in the soft case. Prediction kernelizes the same way: $y = \operatorname{sign}\big(b + \sum_{i \in S} \alpha^{(i)} t^{(i)} K(x^{(i)}, \hat{x})\big)$. Nothing else changes. The same solver, the same support vectors, the same margins, now living in a feature space we never visit. This program of "make it linear somewhere else, pay only for similarities" is the heart of kernel methods, the research program Bernhard Schölkopf's group built out into a whole field.

Expressive does not mean automatically good. The RBF kernel can draw almost any boundary, which means it can also draw nonsense around noise, and $\gamma$ and $C$ must be tuned, on validation data, to land between underfitting and overfitting. Feel the whole trade-off:

Fig 5.3, the kernel boundary explorer. A real kernel SVM (solved by SMO on every change) on interleaved two-moon data. Toggle the kernel: linear fails, polynomial bends, RBF flexes. Then push $\gamma$: tiny values underfit toward a nearly straight cut, huge values grow overfit islands around individual points. Support vectors are ringed; the lighter band is the margin region $|f(x)| < 1$.

XOR: the classic impossibility, dissolved by one feature

The oldest counterexample to linear classification is XOR: four points on the corners of a square, labelled by whether their coordinates agree in sign. No line gets more than three of the four right, which is exactly the failure the classification lecture left as a cliffhanger. But one multiplication solves it. The product $x_1 x_2$ is positive on one diagonal and negative on the other, so the feature map $\phi(x_1, x_2) = (x_1,\, x_2,\, x_1 x_2)$ lifts the four corners into $\mathbb{R}^3$, where the flat plane $x_3 = 0$ separates them perfectly:

Fig 5.4, XOR, before and after. Left: drag the line's endpoints and try to beat 3 of 4; the correct-count is computed live and the fourth point always escapes. Right: apply $\phi(x) = (x_1, x_2, x_1 x_2)$ and the corners split by height, separated by the plane $x_3 = 0$; drag to orbit. The induced kernel is $K(u, v) = u_1 v_1 + u_2 v_2 + u_1 u_2 v_1 v_2$.

The kernels, in code

Everything in this lecture that touches numbers fits in a few honest lines: 

def margin_width(w):
    return 2 / np.linalg.norm(w)          # M = 2 / ||w||

def polynomial_kernel(x, y, d):
    return (1 + x @ y) ** d               # never builds phi

def rbf_kernel(x, y, gamma):
    return np.exp(-gamma * np.sum((x - y) ** 2))

And in practice you rarely hand-roll the solver. The library call exposes exactly the two knobs this lesson taught, and everything else is the dual being solved for you by a quadratic-programming routine: 

from sklearn.svm import SVC

clf = SVC(kernel="rbf", C=1.0, gamma=0.5)  # C and gamma: the two knobs
clf.fit(X_train, t_train)                  # solves the dual with a QP solver
clf.predict(X_test)                        # consults only the support vectors

The four points $(\pm 1, \pm 1)$ are labelled by $t = \operatorname{sign}(x_1 x_2)$: the diagonal pair $(1,1), (-1,-1)$ is one class, the anti-diagonal pair the other. Give a map $\phi : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3$ that makes them linearly separable, and the kernel it induces.

Answer. Take $\phi(x_1, x_2) = (x_1,\, x_2,\, x_1 x_2)$. 6. Summary and method card

One classifier, three escalating ideas. First: among all perfect linear separators, prefer the one with the widest margin, because the buffer is insurance against the gap between training data and the future. Second: the solution is built entirely from the few points that touch the margin, the support vectors; everyone else has $\alpha = 0$ and might as well not exist. Third: since training and prediction consume the data only through dot products, a kernel swaps in the geometry of an enormous feature space at input-space prices, and the same linear machinery draws circles, moons, and XOR. Soft margins glue it to reality by pricing violations with $C$, and $C$ and $\gamma$ are tuned the only honest way, on validation data.

Method card · Support Vector Machines
AssumptionGood boundaries separate classes with a large margin; nonlinear separation comes from a suitable similarity (kernel) function.
Model classMargin-based linear classifiers $y = \operatorname{sign}(w^\top x + b)$, extended to nonlinear boundaries by kernels.
Learning / selectionSolve a constrained optimization (a convex QP); in the dual, the solution and all predictions depend only on support vectors and kernel evaluations.
Prediction$y = \operatorname{sign}\big(b + \sum_{i \in S} \alpha^{(i)} t^{(i)} K(x^{(i)}, \hat{x})\big)$, a kernel vote over the support set only.
StrengthsStrong geometric intuition, effective in high dimensions, a robust soft-margin extension for noisy data, flexible nonlinear classification via kernels.
LimitationsKernel and regularization choices matter and must be tuned; training is expensive at large scale; the nonlinear features are implicit but still hand-designed, via the choice of kernel.
Key hyperparameters$C$ (price of a margin violation) and the kernel with its parameters (degree $d$, RBF $\gamma$), tuned on validation.

Where this sits in the course story: we have gone from similarity (KNN) to rules (trees) to parametric fits (linear and logistic regression) to committees (ensembles), and now to margins and kernels, the most geometric model in the sequence and the last of the classical ones. The kernel idea, hand-designing the similarity instead of the features, is also this course's final act of manual feature engineering. Perfect training separation, no generalization: overfitting in its purest visual form, the islands in Fig 5.3.
Both knobs regulate capacity: $C$ prices violations, $\gamma$ sets the geometry's flexibility. Either one, pushed to its extreme, can overfit or underfit on its own.
Quiz 3 (transfer): design a kernel for a similarity of your own

Invent a kernel for some non-vector notion of similarity (documents, sets of tags, anything) and argue that it is a valid kernel, meaning it really equals a dot product in some feature space.

Answer (one good version). Suppose "similar" for two documents means "share many vocabulary words". Define $K(A, B)$ = the number of distinct words appearing in both documents. Validity argument: exhibit the feature map. Let $\phi(A)$ be the binary indicator vector over the whole vocabulary, one coordinate per word, 1 if the word occurs in $A$. Then $\phi(A)^\top \phi(B)$ counts exactly the shared words, so $K = \phi^\top \phi$ by construction and the kernel is valid: the geometry the SVM will optimize genuinely exists. That construction, "define the similarity you want, then exhibit (or prove the existence of) a $\phi$ that realizes it", is the general recipe. Handy closure facts when the direct construction is awkward: sums of kernels, products of kernels, and positive scalings of kernels are kernels again, so you can assemble complicated valid similarities from simple verified parts.

Quiz 4 (transfer): SVM or logistic regression? Argue both ways

Name a situation where you would prefer an SVM over logistic regression, and one where you would prefer logistic regression, and justify each with what you know about the two models.

Answer (one good version). Prefer the SVM when the boundary is what matters and the data is modest in size but awkward in shape: a few thousand samples, high-dimensional features, genuinely nonlinear structure. Kernels give you nonlinear boundaries without designing features, the margin objective cares only about the hard cases near the boundary, and the solution is sparse in the support vectors. Prefer logistic regression when you need calibrated probabilities rather than bare labels (risk scores, ranking, downstream expected-value decisions), or when the dataset is enormous: its unconstrained objective trains happily with stochastic gradient methods at scales where solving a kernel SVM's dual, whose kernel matrix grows as $N^2$, stops being practical. The models even disagree philosophically: logistic regression listens to every point a little (every sample moves the gradient), while the SVM listens only to the points near the boundary and ignores the rest entirely. Which behaviour you want depends on whether the mass of easy points carries information you care about.

Quiz 5 (transfer): what would a truncated RBF kernel do to the boundary?

The RBF kernel corresponds to an infinite-dimensional feature map. Suppose you truncate that expansion at a finite order. What kind of kernel do you get, and how would the decision boundary change?

Answer. Factor the RBF kernel: $e^{-\gamma\lVert u - v\rVert^2} = e^{-\gamma\lVert u\rVert^2}\, e^{-\gamma\lVert v\rVert^2}\, e^{2\gamma\, u^\top v}$, and Taylor-expand the last factor: $e^{2\gamma u^\top v} = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \frac{(2\gamma)^k}{k!} (u^\top v)^k$. Each term $(u^\top v)^k$ is a polynomial kernel of degree $k$, so RBF is an infinite, factorially-damped sum of polynomial kernels of every degree: that is the sense in which its feature space is infinite-dimensional, containing polynomial features of all orders. Truncating at order $k_{\max}$ keeps only finitely many terms, which is a valid kernel (a positive-weighted sum of polynomial kernels) whose feature space is finite: polynomial features up to degree $k_{\max}$. Consequences for the boundary: it becomes a polynomial surface of bounded degree, so the razor-thin local flexibility of true RBF disappears. No more islands around single points, smoother and more rigid cuts, less overfitting risk, but also no ability to carve fine local structure, and the similarity no longer decays all the way to zero for far pairs the way the true exponential does. Truncation is, in effect, a capacity knob: it moves the model back down the ladder from "infinitely flexible" toward the polynomial kernels of fixed degree.

That is the support vector machine: pick the boundary with the widest buffer, discover that only the points touching the buffer matter, pay for violations with slack priced by $C$, and swap dot products for kernels so the same linear machinery draws curves. It is also the end of an era in this course: the last model whose features, or similarities, a human designs by hand. Next lecture the model designs its own. Neural networks.
