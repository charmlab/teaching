Backpropagation

Last lecture built a multilayer perceptron and then hit a wall: the network has thousands of weights, and we have no way to set them. This lecture knocks the wall down. The answer is not a new learning rule, it is the chain rule you already know, reorganized so brilliantly that one forward pass and one backward pass deliver the derivative of the loss with respect to every single parameter. That algorithm is backpropagation, and it trains essentially every neural network in use today.

0. A million knobs, one method

Picture the network from last lecture, scaled up to something useful: a few hidden layers, a few hundred units each. Count the weights. Every unit in one layer connects to every unit in the next, so the parameters multiply out to hundreds of thousands, and real networks carry millions. Every one of those numbers is a knob, and the network only works when all of them are set right. Nobody can tune a million knobs by hand. We need gradient descent, and gradient descent needs one thing from us: the derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter, recomputed at every step.

So why is that hard? Here is the trap. You could estimate each derivative numerically: nudge one weight up a little, rerun the whole network, and see how the loss moves. That works, but it costs a full forward pass per nudge, two if you nudge both directions, so a million-parameter network pays roughly two million forward passes for a single gradient. Per step. Differentiating the loss as one giant formula by hand is worse: the expression for a deep network fills pages, and most of its pieces repeat.

The big idea of this lecture kills both problems at once. Backpropagation is an efficient, systematic application of the chain rule on a computation graph. Run the network forward once to get the loss. Then walk backward through the same graph once, and by the time you reach the inputs you hold the exact derivative for every parameter, all of them, for about the cost of one extra forward pass. The idea is old and the people behind it were finally, spectacularly recognized: Geoffrey Hinton, the "Godfather of Deep Learning," and John Hopfield of Hopfield-network fame shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for this line of work. This lecture is that prize, from scratch.

1. Gradient descent at scale

Nothing about the learning rule changes. An MLP is a learned transformation $y = \phi_\theta(x)$ whose parameters are all the weights and biases of all the layers collected into one bag:

$$ \theta = \{ W^{(1)}, b^{(1)}, \dots, W^{(L)}, b^{(L)} \} $$

Training is the same gradient descent this course has used since linear regression, just with a much bigger $\theta$:

$$ \theta^{(t+1)} \leftarrow \theta^{(t)} - \alpha \, \nabla_\theta J(\theta) $$

Pick a loss $J$, compute its gradient with respect to every parameter, step against the gradient, repeat. One honest warning before we start descending: a network is a highly nonlinear function of its parameters, so the loss landscape $J(\theta)$ is usually non-convex. There is no single bowl with one bottom like least squares gave us; there are valleys, plateaus, and multiple minima. Gradient descent still works remarkably well in practice, but it comes with no global-optimum guarantee anymore. That trade is the price of expressive models, and we pay it.

So the whole problem reduces to one question: how do we compute $\nabla_\theta J$ for a million parameters without a million recomputations? That question has a beautiful answer, and it starts with a function small enough to hold in your head.

2. The chain rule, reorganized

Take the smallest model that still has all the moving parts: one input, one weight, one bias, a sigmoid, a squared error. Call it logistic least squares:

$$ z = wx + b, \qquad y = \sigma(z), \qquad L = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - t)^2 $$

Here $x$ is the input, $t$ is the target, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid from the logistic regression lecture, and we want $\partial L / \partial w$ and $\partial L / \partial b$ so we can descend. The brute-force route is to substitute everything into one expression, $L = \tfrac{1}{2}(\sigma(wx+b) - t)^2$, and differentiate the whole thing. It works here. It does not survive contact with a real network, and worse, it hides the structure that saves us.

The better route: differentiate part by part. Each equation in the model is simple, so each has a trivial derivative with respect to its own direct input. I call these local derivatives, because each one only knows about a single arrow of the model, nothing else:

$$ \frac{dz}{dw} = x, \qquad \frac{dy}{dz} = \sigma'(z), \qquad \frac{dL}{dy} = y - t $$

The chain rule then snaps the pieces together. The effect of $w$ on $L$ flows through $z$ and then through $y$, so the derivatives multiply along the path:

$$ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial w} = (y - t)\,\sigma'(z)\,x $$

Nothing new mathematically, this is first-year calculus. What is new is the bookkeeping discipline: never expand, always multiply local pieces. Notice what the pieces are made of: $y - t$ and $\sigma'(z)$ only use values the forward computation already produced. For the sigmoid this is especially tidy, since $\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\,(1 - \sigma(z)) = y(1-y)$, so the derivative is free once you know $y$. Let me make all of this concrete with real numbers, because we are about to reuse them for the rest of the lesson.

Worked by hand: the forward pass

Take $x = 2$, $w = 0.5$, $b = -0.5$, target $t = 1$. Compute each node in order:

$$ z = wx + b = (0.5)(2) - 0.5 = 0.5 $$ $$ y = \sigma(0.5) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-0.5}} = 0.6225 $$ $$ L = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - t)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(0.6225 - 1)^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(-0.3775)^2 = 0.0713 $$

The prediction is 0.6225, the target is 1, the loss is 0.0713. Every derivative we compute next is built from these three numbers plus the inputs. Hold onto them.

3. The computation graph

Now draw the model instead of writing it. A computation graph is a diagram whose nodes are the inputs and every computed quantity, and whose edges are the functional dependencies: an arrow from $w$ to $z$ means $z$ is computed directly from $w$. Our model becomes six nodes: $x, w, b$ feed $z$, which feeds $y$, which joins $t$ to produce $L$. The forward pass is exactly what the worked example just did: sweep left to right, computing every node's value from its parents.

The payoff of drawing the graph is the backward sweep. Give every node $v$ an error signal, written with bar notation:

$$ \bar{v} \;=\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial v} $$

Read $\bar{v}$ as "how much the loss changes per unit nudge of $v$." The trick that makes everything efficient is to compute these in reverse order, loss first, inputs last, because each error signal is just the previous one times a local derivative:

$$ \bar{y} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial y} = y - t, \qquad \bar{z} = \bar{y}\,\sigma'(z), \qquad \bar{w} = \bar{z}\,x, \qquad \bar{b} = \bar{z} $$

Look at what got reused. The product $(y-t)\,\sigma'(z)$ appears inside both $\bar{w}$ and $\bar{b}$, and the backward ordering computes it once, names it $\bar{z}$, and hands it to both. In a deep network this sharing is the entire ballgame: every error signal is computed once and feeds every parameter behind it. This is the backward pass. Watch it run:

Fig 3.1, the signature picture of the lesson. Step the forward pass left to right to fill in values, then step the backward pass right to left and watch each error signal get built from the one before it times a local derivative. Every number is computed live from the sliders; nothing is staged.

Worked by hand: the backward pass

Same numbers as before: $x=2$, $w=0.5$, $b=-0.5$, $t=1$, which gave $z = 0.5$, $y = 0.6225$. First the one local derivative that needs computing, using $\sigma'(z) = y(1-y)$:

$$ \sigma'(0.5) = (0.6225)(1 - 0.6225) = (0.6225)(0.3775) = 0.2350 $$

Now sweep right to left:

$$ \bar{y} = y - t = 0.6225 - 1 = -0.3775 $$ $$ \bar{z} = \bar{y}\,\sigma'(z) = (-0.3775)(0.2350) = -0.0887 $$ $$ \bar{w} = \bar{z}\,x = (-0.0887)(2) = -0.1774, \qquad \bar{b} = \bar{z} = -0.0887 $$

Sanity check the honest way: nudge $w$ numerically. With $\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$, $\big(L(w{+}\varepsilon) - L(w{-}\varepsilon)\big)/2\varepsilon = -0.177447$, which matches $\bar{w}$ to six decimals. Fig 4.2 lets you run this check yourself for any inputs.

Finally, use the gradient. One step with $\alpha = 1$: $w \leftarrow 0.5 - (-0.1774) = 0.6774$ and $b \leftarrow -0.5 - (-0.0887) = -0.4113$. Rerun the forward pass and the prediction rises to $y = 0.7198$, closer to the target of 1, and the loss falls from 0.0713 to 0.0392. The machinery works.

Every edge carries one local derivative

A useful way to internalize the backward pass: each edge of the graph owns exactly one local derivative, the derivative of its head with respect to its tail. The backward pass never does anything more clever than walking backward and multiplying the running error signal by each edge's local derivative as it crosses it. Backprop is the chain rule, reused, one edge at a time. Convince yourself by inspecting the edges:

Fan-out: when one node feeds two children

One rule is still missing. What if a node influences the loss along two paths? Calculus already answers this: for a function of several intermediate variables, the total derivative sums over the paths. This is the multivariate chain rule:

$$ \frac{d}{dt} f\big(x(t),\, y(t)\big) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt} $$

On a graph, a node that feeds several children is said to have fan-out greater than 1, and its error signal is the sum of the contributions flowing back from all of its children. The cleanest real example is L2-regularized regression: penalize large weights by adding $R = \tfrac{1}{2}w^2$ to the loss, so the total is $L_{\text{reg}} = L + \lambda R$. Now $w$ has two children, $z$ and $R$, and both send gradient back to it:

$$ \bar{w} = \bar{z}\,x + \bar{R}\,w, \qquad \text{where } \bar{R} = \lambda $$

Watch the two contributions arrive and add up:

Fig 3.3, fan-out made concrete. The weight $w$ feeds both the prediction path (through $z$) and the regularizer $R$. Step the backward pass and both contributions, $\bar{z}\,x$ and $\bar{R}\,w$, flow back and sum into $\bar{w}$. Slide $\lambda$ to change how loudly the regularizer argues.

That is $\frac{d}{dt}f(x(t),y(t)) = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{dx}{dt} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{dy}{dt}$ wearing a graph costume.

One more piece of vocabulary and the picture is complete. Computing derivatives mechanically from the computation rule itself, rather than from a hand-derived formula, is called automatic differentiation. Backpropagation is the special case that modern frameworks implement: run the loss forward through the graph, then propagate gradients backward. When PyTorch or JAX "just knows" your gradients, this graph walk is what it is doing.

4. The backpropagation algorithm

Everything so far generalizes to any graph in three lines. Let $v_1, \dots, v_N$ be a topological ordering of the nodes, meaning every parent comes before its children, with $v_N = L$ the final loss.

  1. Forward pass. For $i = 1, \dots, N$, compute $v_i = f_i(\text{Parents}(v_i))$.
  2. Backward pass. Initialize $\bar{v}_N = \frac{\partial L}{\partial L} = 1$, then for $i = N{-}1, \dots, 1$: $$ \bar{v}_i = \sum_{j \,\in\, \text{Children}(v_i)} \bar{v}_j\, \frac{\partial v_j}{\partial v_i} $$
  3. Gradient step. For every trainable parameter, $w_j^{(t+1)} \leftarrow w_j^{(t)} - \alpha\, \frac{\partial J}{\partial w_j}$.

Read the backward formula slowly, because it contains the whole lesson. Each node collects one term per child: the child's already-computed error signal times the local derivative of that child with respect to this node. One child means one product, exactly the chain rule of section 2. Several children means a sum, exactly the fan-out rule of Fig 3.3. Initializing $\bar{L} = 1$ just says the loss changes one-for-one with itself. That is the entire algorithm; everything else is applying it.

Applied to a real network

Apply the recipe to the two-layer MLP from last lecture: inputs $x_j$, hidden pre-activations $z_i = \sum_j w_{ij}^{(1)} x_j + b_i^{(1)}$, hidden activations $h_i = \sigma(z_i)$, outputs $y_k$ built from the $h_i$, then a loss. The backward pass falls straight out of the sum-over-children formula. Each hidden unit feeds every output, fan-out again, so:

$$ \bar{h}_i = \sum_k \bar{y}_k\, w_{ki}^{(2)}, \qquad \bar{z}_i = \bar{h}_i\, \sigma'(z_i), \qquad \bar{w}_{ij}^{(1)} = \bar{z}_i\, x_j $$

The same three moves as the scalar case: sum the children's error signals through the weights, squeeze through the activation's local derivative, multiply by the input that fed the weight. Layer by layer, right to left, each layer's error signals computed once and reused by everything behind them. Step through it on a live network:

Fig 4.1, backprop on a 2-3-1 MLP with fixed, visible weights. Forward steps light up activations layer by layer; backward steps flow the error signals $\bar{y} \to \bar{h}_i \to \bar{z}_i \to \bar{w}_{ij}$ with every product shown numerically. The narration prints the exact instance of $\bar{h}_i = \sum_k \bar{y}_k w_{ki}$ being computed.

In code, the backward pass is barely longer than the math. This function reuses the forward pass from the neural-networks lesson and returns the gradient of the loss with respect to every parameter: 

def backward(x, t, W1, b1, w2, b2):
    z = W1 @ x + b1              # forward pass, values saved
    h = sigmoid(z)
    y = w2 @ h + b2
    y_bar  = y - t               # dL/dy for L = 0.5*(y-t)**2
    w2_bar = y_bar * h           # each w2_i sees  y_bar * h_i
    b2_bar = y_bar
    h_bar  = y_bar * w2          # fan-in reversed: h_bar_i = y_bar * w2_i
    z_bar  = h_bar * h * (1 - h) # through sigma'(z) = h(1-h)
    W1_bar = np.outer(z_bar, x)  # w_bar_ij = z_bar_i * x_j
    b1_bar = z_bar
    return W1_bar, b1_bar, w2_bar, b2_bar

Never trust a backward pass you have not tested. The universal test is the one from the worked example: compare against finite differences. It is too slow to train with, but perfect as a unit test. Run it live:

Fig 4.2, the backward pass as a code exercise. The backward() shown really executes on the logistic-least-squares graph with your inputs, and every run is verified against central finite differences $\big(L(w{+}\varepsilon)-L(w{-}\varepsilon)\big)/2\varepsilon$. The agreement to ten decimal places is the proof that backprop computes true derivatives, not approximations.

5. Batch, mini-batch, and stochastic gradient descent

One question remains before this scales to real datasets: gradients of what, exactly? The training cost is the average loss over the dataset, and because differentiation is linear, the gradient is the average of the per-example gradients:

$$ J(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} L^{(i)}(\theta), \qquad \nabla J(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \nabla L^{(i)}(\theta) $$

So one honest gradient costs $N$ backprop runs, one per example. When $N$ is a million, that is a million backprops for a single parameter update. This is batch gradient descent: exact, and painfully expensive per step. The alternative sounds reckless: update from one random example,

$$ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \alpha\, \nabla L^{(i)}(\theta), \qquad i \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1, \dots, N\} $$

This is stochastic gradient descent (SGD), and it is less reckless than it looks, because the random single-example gradient is an unbiased estimate of the full gradient. Averaging over the random index recovers the true gradient exactly:

$$ \mathbb{E}_{i \sim \mathrm{Unif}\{1,\dots,N\}}\!\left[\nabla L^{(i)}(\theta)\right] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\nabla L^{(i)}(\theta) = \nabla J(\theta) $$

Each step points in the right direction on average; individual steps jitter around that direction. Between the extremes lives the workhorse. Mini-batch gradient descent averages over a random batch $S$ of examples:

$$ \hat{g}_S(\theta) = \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{i \in S} \nabla L^{(i)}(\theta) $$

Still unbiased, and for independently drawn examples the variance of each gradient coordinate shrinks in proportion to the batch size, $\operatorname{Var}[\hat{g}_{S,j}] = \frac{1}{S}\operatorname{Var}\big[\partial L^{(i)}/\partial \theta_j\big]$. Bigger batches buy smoother gradients, at a price of more computation per update. Smaller batches are noisier but cheaper. That trade-off is the whole design space, and you should feel it before believing it:

Worked by hand: one SGD step vs one batch step

Tiny model $y = wx$ with squared loss $L^{(i)} = \tfrac{1}{2}(w x^{(i)} - t^{(i)})^2$, so $\nabla L^{(i)} = (w x^{(i)} - t^{(i)})\,x^{(i)}$. Dataset of three: $(1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 5)$. At $w = 1$ the three per-example gradients are:

$$ \nabla L^{(1)} = (1 - 2)(1) = -1, \quad \nabla L^{(2)} = (2 - 3)(2) = -2, \quad \nabla L^{(3)} = (3 - 5)(3) = -6 $$

Batch step ($\alpha = 0.1$): the full gradient is the average, $\nabla J = \tfrac{1}{3}(-1 - 2 - 6) = -3$, so $w \leftarrow 1 - (0.1)(-3) = 1.3$. Cost: three backprops.

SGD step: sample one example, say the third. $w \leftarrow 1 - (0.1)(-6) = 1.6$. Cost: one backprop. A different draw gives 1.1 or 1.2, so the step is noisy. But average the three possible SGD steps and you get $w = 1.3$, the batch step, exactly. Unbiased, in one line of arithmetic.

In code, the two steps differ only in how many examples they touch:

def sgd_step(theta, X, T, alpha, rng):
    i = rng.integers(len(X))               # one random example
    return theta - alpha * grad_L(theta, X[i], T[i])

def minibatch_step(theta, X, T, alpha, S, rng):
    idx = rng.choice(len(X), size=S, replace=False)
    g = np.mean([grad_L(theta, X[i], T[i]) for i in idx], axis=0)
    return theta - alpha * g               # S = len(X) gives batch GD

One pass through the whole training set, however you slice it into updates, is called an epoch. Counting floating-point operations per epoch is revealing: 100 updates at batch size 1 cost the same FLOPs as 1 update at batch size 100. But the 100 sequential updates are not equivalent to the 1 big one, because each small update computes its gradient at fresher parameters, already improved by the updates before it. If wall-clock time were proportional to FLOPs, tiny batches would win outright. In practice they do not, because hardware runs a batch of 100 in parallel for nearly the cost of 1, which is why moderate mini-batches dominate real training. Now race the three:

6. Training tricks

Backprop plus SGD will happily minimize training loss all the way into overfitting. Why does overfitting happen at all? Because data contains both real regularities and junk: sampling noise, and accidental regularities specific to the sample you happened to collect. The lecture's example is worth remembering: collect car images for a classifier but forget to balance across seasons, and the network may learn that snow means truck. A big network has the capacity to memorize all of it. The standard fixes each attack a different part of the problem.

Trick 1: data augmentation

The cheapest way to get more training data is to manufacture it. Transform the examples you have, translate, flip, rotate, warp smoothly, add noise, and each transform of a training image is a new training image with the same label. Two rules keep it honest. Only the training data may be warped, never the test data, because the test set's job is to represent reality untouched. And the valid transformations depend on the task: a horizontal flip of a car is still a car, but a horizontal flip of a handwritten digit is not a digit anymore:

Fig 6.1, the augmentation gallery. One training digit, four augmented clones per resample; toggle shift, rotation, and noise, all label-preserving. Then switch on the mirror and notice the figure objecting: a flipped 7 is no longer a 7. What counts as label-preserving is a property of the task, not of the transform.

Trick 2: parameter reduction

Fewer parameters, less capacity to memorize noise. Use fewer layers or fewer units per layer, or insert a linear bottleneck: a narrow linear layer between two nonlinear ones. A linear layer adds no expressive power at all, two linear maps compose into one, yet the narrow waist can cut the parameter count dramatically, and that alone can improve generalization. Capacity you do not have is capacity you cannot waste on noise.

Trick 3: weight decay

The L2 regularization from the logistic-regression lecture, now viewed through the update rule. Penalize the squared weights:

$$ J_{\text{reg}} = J + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_j w_j^2 $$

Backprop through the penalty (fan-out again, exactly Fig 3.3) adds $\lambda w$ to every weight's gradient, and the gradient step rearranges into something you can read:

$$ w \leftarrow w - \alpha\!\left(\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} + \lambda w\right) = (1 - \alpha\lambda)\,w - \alpha\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} $$

Before the data gets a word in, every weight is multiplied by $(1 - \alpha\lambda)$, a number slightly below 1. With $\alpha = 0.1$ and $\lambda = 0.5$ each weight literally decays by a factor of 0.95 every step, hence the name weight decay. Why do small weights fight overfitting? Remember what an overfit polynomial looks like: wild oscillations that require enormous coefficients to produce. Keep the coefficients small and the wildness is not expressible:

Fig 6.2, weight decay taming a degree-9 polynomial fit on 10 noisy points. Slide $\lambda$ up and watch the fitted coefficients in the table shrink from the thousands to single digits while the curve calms from wild interpolation to a sane fit. The $(1-\alpha\lambda)$ shrink factor is printed for the current $\lambda$.

Check: derive the shrink factor, and connect it back to L2 regularization

Starting from $J_{\text{reg}} = J + \frac{\lambda}{2}\sum_j w_j^2$, derive the update and explain the connection to the L2-regularized logistic regression from earlier in the course.

Answer. Differentiate the penalty: $\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\big(\frac{\lambda}{2}w^2\big) = \lambda w$, so $\frac{\partial J_{\text{reg}}}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial J}{\partial w} + \lambda w$. Substitute into gradient descent: $w \leftarrow w - \alpha\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} - \alpha\lambda w = (1-\alpha\lambda)w - \alpha\frac{\partial J}{\partial w}$. The first term shrinks the weight multiplicatively every step regardless of the data; the second is the ordinary descent step. It is the same L2 penalty we added to logistic regression, nothing new in the objective; what is new is reading it as a per-step decay inside the update, which is how deep learning practitioners actually implement and tune it. Note the equilibrium intuition: a weight survives only if the data gradient consistently pushes it up harder than the decay pulls it down, so only weights that earn their size keep it.

Trick 4: early stopping

Start from small random weights and just stop training at the right moment. This works for a reason specific to sigmoidal networks: with small weights, each sigmoid operates in its nearly linear middle region, so the whole network starts out roughly linear, low capacity, honest. As training grows the weights, the units saturate into their nonlinear regimes and the network's effective capacity rises. Training time becomes a capacity dial. So watch the validation error: it falls while the network learns real structure, then turns and rises as the network starts memorizing noise. Stop at the turn. It is dramatically cheaper than retraining across many weight decay settings, and it makes initialization matter: start too big and you begin already nonlinear, with no linear phase to pass through. Trick 5: ensembling

Train several networks and combine their predictions. Vary the random initialization, or the architecture, or train each on a different subset of the data, which is exactly the bagging idea from the ensembles lecture. Ensembles frequently top benchmark leaderboards, and they cost what you would expect: several times the training and inference compute, and a combined model that is harder to interpret than any single network.

Always watch the training run

Whatever tricks you use, monitor two curves during every training run: the training loss and the validation accuracy. They are your only instruments for catching a learning rate that is too hot, a bug in the backward pass, or the onset of overfitting. And do not panic when the training loss bounces: with mini-batches, every update measures the loss on a different random subset, so the curve is bumpy by construction. The trend is the signal; the bumps are the batch noise you chose in section 5.

7. Summary and method card

Backpropagation efficiently computes the gradients that gradient descent needs: run the loss forward through the computation graph, then sweep backward, building each node's error signal from its children's error signals times local derivatives, summing when a node fans out. It is a special case of automatic differentiation, and it is not an approximation; it produces exact derivatives, which is why one finite-difference check can certify an implementation. Around that core, stochastic and mini-batch gradient descent trade gradient noise for update frequency using unbiased estimates, and a toolbox of tricks, augmentation, parameter reduction, weight decay, early stopping, ensembling, keeps the minimization aimed at generalization instead of memorization. Gradient descent with backprop trains the overwhelming majority of neural networks in use today, including every model in the rest of this course.

Method card · Backpropagation + SGD
Problem solvedCompute $\nabla_\theta J$ for all parameters of a network at once, so gradient descent can run at scale.
Core ideaThe chain rule on a computation graph: forward pass computes values, backward pass computes error signals $\bar{v} = \partial L/\partial v$ in reverse topological order, $\bar{v}_i = \sum_{j \in \text{Ch}(v_i)} \bar{v}_j\, \partial v_j / \partial v_i$.
CostOne forward plus one backward pass per example, roughly twice a forward pass, for all gradients; versus two forward passes per parameter for finite differences.
Update rulesBatch: exact gradient, expensive. SGD: one example, unbiased but noisy. Mini-batch of size $S$: unbiased, variance $\propto 1/S$. One pass over the data = one epoch.
CaveatsThe loss landscape is non-convex, so no global-optimum guarantee; gradients must be verified (finite differences); loss curves are bumpy under mini-batch noise.
Generalization aidsData augmentation (train set only, task-appropriate transforms), parameter reduction and linear bottlenecks, weight decay $(1-\alpha\lambda)w$, early stopping from small weights, ensembling.
Key hyperparametersLearning rate $\alpha$, batch size $S$, weight decay $\lambda$, stopping epoch; all tuned on validation data, never on training loss.

Where this sits in the course story: we now have machinery that can train any differentiable architecture, which frees us to design architectures for structure in the data. Next lecture does exactly that for images, where sharing weights across spatial positions turns the MLP into a convolutional network, and backprop trains it without changing a single line of this lesson's algorithm.

8. End-of-lesson quiz

Two quick checks, then three transfer questions that ask you to use the machinery, not recite it. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Quiz 1

Your network has 500,000 parameters. Compared with central finite differences, how does backprop change the cost of getting one full gradient?

The costs are wildly different. Finite differences rerun the whole network per parameter; backprop reuses one backward sweep for all of them. (Backprop is also exact, while finite differences approximate.)
That would already be an improvement, but backprop does better: the sum-over-children recursion computes each node's error signal once, and every parameter reads its gradient off the error signal next to it. No per-parameter passes at all.
Right. That ratio, about a million passes versus roughly two, is the entire reason deep learning is computationally feasible, and why Fig 4.2 uses finite differences only as a spot check.
Quiz 2

You quadruple your mini-batch size from 16 to 64, keeping everything else fixed. What happens to the gradient estimate and the cost?

Exactly the $\operatorname{Var} \propto 1/S$ trade: smoother steps, fewer of them per epoch. Whether that is a good deal depends on hardware parallelism, which is why moderate batch sizes win in practice.
Averaging more independent samples never introduces bias; expectation is linear. Every batch size gives an unbiased estimate of $\nabla J$.
The means match but the variances do not, and the number of updates per epoch differs by 4×. Fig 5.1 shows the paths behaving very differently step by step.
Quiz 3 (transfer): draw the graph, then backprop a new expression

Consider $L = w\,\sigma(wx)$ with $w = 1$, $x = 1$. Draw the computation graph, note the fan-out, and compute $\bar{w}$.

Answer. Nodes: $z = wx$, $h = \sigma(z)$, $L = w \cdot h$. The weight $w$ has fan-out 2: one edge into $z$ and one edge directly into $L$. Backward pass: $\bar{L} = 1$; $\bar{h} = \bar{L}\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial h} = w = 1$; $\bar{z} = \bar{h}\,\sigma'(z) = \sigma'(1) = (0.7311)(0.2689) = 0.1966$. Now sum $w$'s two contributions: through the direct edge, $\bar{L}\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = h = 0.7311$; through $z$, $\bar{z}\,x = 0.1966$. Total $\bar{w} = 0.7311 + 0.1966 = 0.9277$. Forget the fan-out sum and you get 0.7311 or 0.1966, both wrong, and a finite-difference check (which gives 0.9277) catches it instantly.

Quiz 4 (transfer): the bumpy loss curve

A friend shows you their training loss curve: trending down but wobbling wildly from update to update. They suspect a bug. What do you check, and how could the curve be smoothed?

Answer. First ask the batch size. Under mini-batch or stochastic gradient descent, each update reports the loss on a different random subset, so the curve wobbles by construction: the gradient estimate is unbiased but has variance proportional to $1/S$, and so does the reported loss. A downward trend with bounded wobble is healthy SGD, not a bug. To smooth it: increase the batch size (variance falls as $1/S$), lower the learning rate so each noisy step is smaller, or simply plot a running average of the loss and judge the trend. A real bug looks different: the loss trending up (learning rate too hot, or a sign error in the update), or gradients that fail a finite-difference check. Check the gradient once with finite differences and the backward pass is either certified or convicted.

Quiz 5 (transfer): when does early stopping substitute for weight decay?

Argue when early stopping can play the role of weight decay, and when it cannot.

Answer. Both are capacity controls that keep weights small. Weight decay does it explicitly, shrinking every weight by $(1-\alpha\lambda)$ per step forever. Early stopping does it implicitly: starting from small weights, a sigmoidal network begins in its nearly linear low-capacity regime, and weights (hence capacity) grow with training time, so stopping early caps how large the weights ever get. When training is the expensive resource, early stopping is the better deal: one run with a validation curve replaces retraining across many $\lambda$ values, and you get a stopping epoch for free. Where the substitution fails: it needs the small-weights starting point (initialize large and there is no low-capacity phase to stop in), it ties regularization strength to the optimization trajectory rather than to the objective, so you cannot tune it independently of the learning rate or continue training later without losing the effect, and if you need training loss driven very low and small weights simultaneously, only the explicit penalty delivers both. In practice the two are often combined.

That is backpropagation: values forward, error signals backward, local derivatives multiplied along edges and summed at fan-outs, exact gradients for every parameter at the cost of about one extra pass. Add unbiased mini-batch noise for speed and a handful of tricks for generalization, and you have the training loop behind essentially all of modern deep learning. Next lecture we point it at images.
