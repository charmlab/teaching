Decision Trees

Last lecture's model answered every question by rummaging through its memories. This one does the opposite: it studies the data once, distills it into a short list of yes/no questions, and then predicts by playing twenty questions with each new example. That shift, from similarity to rules, forces two beautiful ideas into the course: a way to measure uncertainty (entropy) and a way to measure how much a question reduces it (information gain). This lesson builds both from scratch, with an interactive figure for every concept.

0. The hook: twenty questions

You already know this algorithm from a road trip. In twenty questions, one player thinks of a thing and the other identifies it by asking a sequence of yes/no questions, each one chosen to narrow the possibilities as fast as possible. "Is it alive?" splits the world in half. "Is it bigger than a breadbox?" splits it again. A good player reaches the answer in a handful of questions; a bad player wastes turns on questions that rule out almost nothing. Hold onto that difference between good questions and bad ones, because by section 4 it becomes a formula.

Now make it mechanical. Picture a citrus sorting facility: fruit rolls down a conveyor, sensors measure each one's height and width, and a machine has to call it, lemon or orange, in milliseconds. The machine's strategy is exactly twenty questions: is the width over some cutoff? If yes, ask a follow-up; if no, ask a different follow-up; after two or three questions, commit to a label. Try it below. The tree in this figure was learned from real measurements, and the walk from top to bottom is the entire prediction algorithm:

Fig 0.1, prediction is a walk. Set the mystery fruit's height and width, press Classify, and follow it from the root to a leaf. Each box asks one question about one measurement; the answer picks the branch; the leaf at the bottom is the verdict.

That is a decision tree: a model that predicts by asking a sequence of feature-based questions, routing an example from the root to a leaf. Notice what we did not need: no distances, no scan over stored examples, no memory of the training data at all. The tree is the model. Two questions drive the rest of the lesson. What exactly is this object and what can it express? And, the harder one, where do good questions come from?

1. Anatomy of a tree

Name the parts first, because every later formula refers to them. An internal node tests one feature (one attribute of the input). A branch is one possible answer to that test, and it routes the example onward. A leaf, also called a terminal node, outputs a prediction. To classify a test instance you start at the root (the top node), answer its test, follow the matching branch, and repeat until you land in a leaf. One root-to-leaf path, one prediction.

Which parts are learned and which are chosen? The parameters, the things training determines, are the splits themselves (which feature each node tests, at what threshold) and the overall shape of the tree. The hyperparameters, chosen by us before training, are the limits we place on that shape: the maximum depth, the minimum number of examples in a leaf, the minimum improvement a split must offer. Section 6 shows why those limits are a matter of survival, not taste.

Every leaf owns a region of space

Here is the picture that makes trees geometric. Take any input in the feature space and run it through the tree; it lands in exactly one leaf. So each leaf $m$ owns a decision region $R_m$: the set of all inputs that reach it. The regions tile the whole space with no gaps and no overlaps. And because every internal node tests a single feature against a threshold, every cut is a straight line perpendicular to one axis. The resulting boundary is axis-aligned: a patchwork of rectangles, never a diagonal, never a curve. A tree and its region map are the same object drawn two ways:

Fig 1.1, the tree ↔ regions duality. Click a leaf and its rectangle lights up; click a rectangle and its leaf lights up. Each internal node's threshold is one axis-aligned cut, and stacking the cuts tiles the feature space into one region per leaf.

Two kinds of questions

The kind of test a node can ask depends on the feature's type, using exactly the feature vocabulary from lecture 2:

  • Numerical features split on a threshold: $\text{feature} > \text{threshold}$. Width over 6.5 cm? Height over 8 cm? One number, two branches.
  • Categorical features split on category: is the feature this value, or which of its values is it? The classic example we will use all lesson: deciding whether to wait for a table at a restaurant (a True/False target) from features like Type (French, Italian, Thai, Burger) and Patrons (None, Some, Full).

Numerical splits are the ones you can feel. Below is the citrus data as a scatter, with one split under your control. Drag the threshold and watch the two groups it creates. 2. Expressiveness and the overfitting trap

How much can this rectangle language express? Everything, given enough rectangles. A decision tree is a universal function approximator: stack enough axis-aligned cuts and you can carve feature space as finely as you like. Here is the blunt construction that proves it. For any training set, keep splitting until every training point sits alone in its own leaf, then label each leaf with its point's class. One leaf per training point. This tree classifies the training set perfectly, every single time, no matter how noisy the data is.

And that is precisely the trap. A tree with one leaf per training point has not learned the difference between lemons and oranges; it has memorized 32 individual fruits, mislabeled ones included. Ask it about a new fruit and it answers with whatever noise happened to live in that sliver of space. Perfect training accuracy, and nothing to say about the world. The lecture 2 lesson applies unchanged: what we want is generalization, and an unconstrained tree is a memorization machine. So the real goal is a small tree whose few splits capture real structure, and we need a principled way to find one.

Why not just search for the smallest tree that fits the data? Because that search is computationally hopeless: finding the optimal tree is NP-complete (Laurent and Rivest, 1976). The number of possible trees explodes with the number of features and thresholds, and no known algorithm finds the best one efficiently. So we lower our ambitions and go greedy: build the tree top-down, and at each node choose the single split that most reduces our uncertainty about the target, then recurse. Greedy needs a number, a score that says how uncertain a node is, so that "most reduces uncertainty" means something. The requirements are easy to state:

  • A leaf holding examples of only one class should score low (we are certain; good).
  • A leaf holding an even mix of every class should score high (we are clueless; bad).

Claude Shannon built exactly this number in 1948, for a completely different purpose (how many bits does a message need?), and it drops into our problem unchanged. It is called entropy, and it is the next section.

Check: convince yourself the one-leaf-per-point tree exists

Sketch the argument that for any training set (with no two identical inputs labelled differently) you can always build a tree with zero training error. Then say why we refuse to.

Answer. Take any node holding two or more points. Since the points are distinct, some feature differs between two of them, so a threshold between those two values splits the node into two non-empty parts. Repeat; each split strictly shrinks the largest group, so the recursion ends with every point alone in a leaf, and labelling each leaf by its own point gives zero training error. We refuse because this tree encodes the training set, noise and all: its decision regions are slivers shaped by accidents of sampling, so its error on new data can be terrible. Zero training error is not evidence of learning; lecture 2's validation discipline is how we catch the difference. And searching for the smallest zero-error tree instead is NP-complete, which is why the next sections build a greedy alternative.

3. Measuring uncertainty: entropy

Start with the simplest uncertain thing in the world: a coin. If the coin is fair, you are maximally uncertain about the next flip. If the coin is loaded so that heads comes up 90% of the time, you are much less uncertain; betting heads is nearly safe. If the coin has heads on both sides, you are not uncertain at all. Entropy is the number that grades this scale. For a discrete random variable $X$ taking values $x$ with probabilities $p(x)$:

$$ H(X) := -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x) \log_2 p(x) $$

Read it as an average of surprises. An outcome with probability $p(x)$ carries $-\log_2 p(x)$ bits of surprise: rare outcomes are big news, certain outcomes are no news. Entropy is the expected surprise, the average information content of one draw, measured in bits because of the base-2 log. For a coin with $P(\text{heads}) = p$, the sum has two terms:

$$ H(X) = -p \log_2 p - (1-p) \log_2 (1-p) $$
Worked by hand: two coins

Fair coin, $p = 0.5$:

$$ H = -\tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} - \tfrac{1}{2}\log_2 \tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}(1) + \tfrac{1}{2}(1) = 1 \text{ bit} $$

One full bit of uncertainty per flip, the maximum a two-outcome variable can carry.

Loaded coin, $p = 0.9$: using $\log_2 0.9 \approx -0.152$ and $\log_2 0.1 \approx -3.322$,

$$ H = -0.9(-0.152) - 0.1(-3.322) = 0.137 + 0.332 \approx 0.469 \text{ bits} $$

Less than half the fair coin's uncertainty. And at $p = 0$ or $p = 1$ the entropy is exactly 0: a certain outcome carries no surprise at all. (We adopt the convention $0 \log 0 = 0$.)

Fig 3.1, the binary entropy curve. Drag either coin along the $p$ axis. The curve peaks at exactly 1 bit at $p = 0.5$ and falls to 0 at both ends. Park the coins at two different biases and the readout says which one is more loaded, meaning more predictable, meaning lower entropy.

The curve is the whole story of one variable: certainty at the edges, maximal confusion in the middle. But splits involve two variables, the target and the feature we are testing, so we need entropy's social life too.

Joint and conditional entropy

The joint entropy of two variables measures the uncertainty in the pair taken together:

$$ H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} \sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} p(x, y) \log_2 p(x, y) $$

The lecture's example: let $X$ be whether it is raining and $Y$ whether it is cloudy, with the joint table $P(R, C) = \tfrac{24}{100}$, $P(R, \neg C) = \tfrac{1}{100}$, $P(\neg R, C) = \tfrac{25}{100}$, $P(\neg R, \neg C) = \tfrac{50}{100}$. Plugging the four cells into the formula gives $H(X, Y) \approx 1.56$ bits. (Homework fact from the slides: for two binary variables the maximum possible is $H(X,Y) = 2$ bits, reached when all four cells are equally likely.)

More useful for us: how uncertain is $Y$ once we know the value of $X$? That is the conditional entropy given a specific value:

$$ H(Y \mid X = x) = -\sum_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} p(y \mid x) \log_2 p(y \mid x) $$

Work the weather numbers. If it is raining ($P(R) = 0.25$), then $P(C \mid R) = \tfrac{24}{25} = 0.96$, so

$$ H(Y \mid X = \text{R}) = -0.96 \log_2 0.96 - 0.04 \log_2 0.04 \approx 0.057 + 0.186 \approx 0.24 \text{ bits} $$

Knowing it rains makes cloudiness nearly certain. If it is not raining, $P(C \mid \neg R) = \tfrac{25}{75} = \tfrac{1}{3}$, and the same computation gives $H(Y \mid X = \neg\text{R}) \approx 0.92$ bits, much more uncertain. So the leftover uncertainty depends on which answer we got. To get one number for the feature overall, average over the answers, weighted by how likely each answer is. That is the expected conditional entropy:

$$ H(Y \mid X) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim X}\big[ H(Y \mid X = x) \big] = \sum_{x \in \mathcal{X}} p(x)\, H(Y \mid X = x) $$

For the weather: $H(Y \mid X) = 0.25(0.24) + 0.75(0.92) \approx 0.75$ bits. A tidy consistency check, using the chain rule below: $H(Y \mid X) + H(X) = 0.75 + 0.81 = 1.56 = H(X, Y)$, where $H(X) = H(0.25) \approx 0.81$ bits. The pieces fit.

Properties worth memorizing

  • Non-negative: $H(X) \ge 0$ always.
  • Chain rule: $H(X, Y) = H(X \mid Y) + H(Y) = H(Y \mid X) + H(X)$. Total uncertainty = uncertainty of one part + leftover uncertainty of the other.
  • Independence: if $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $H(Y \mid X) = H(Y)$. Knowing $X$ tells you nothing.
  • Information never hurts: $H(Y \mid X) \le H(Y)$. Knowing something can only reduce (or preserve) your uncertainty, never increase it.
  • Self-knowledge: $H(Y \mid Y) = 0$. Once you know $Y$, there is no uncertainty about $Y$.

4. Information gain

Now assemble the split-scoring machine. Before a split, our uncertainty about the target $Y$ at a node is $H(Y)$. After splitting on a feature (or test) $X$, the leftover uncertainty is the expected conditional entropy $H(Y \mid X)$: each child's entropy, weighted by the fraction of examples that flow into it. The difference is what the question bought us. That difference is the information gain:

$$ IG(Y \mid X) := H(Y) - H(Y \mid X) $$

It is measured in bits: how many bits of uncertainty about $Y$ did knowing $X$ remove? (This quantity is also called the mutual information between $Y$ and $X$; same formula, two names.) By the "information never hurts" property it is never negative, it is zero when the feature is independent of the target, and it is capped by $H(Y)$ itself, which is achieved only by a split into perfectly pure children. High information gain is exactly the "good question" from twenty questions, now with units.

Worked by hand: one full information-gain computation

A node holds 7 fruits: 2 lemons and 5 oranges, with heights (cm): oranges at 6.2, 6.5, 6.8, 6.9, 7.4 and lemons at 8.6, 9.4. Step 1: entropy before. The class proportions are $\tfrac{2}{7}$ and $\tfrac{5}{7}$:

$$ H(Y) = -\tfrac{2}{7}\log_2\tfrac{2}{7} - \tfrac{5}{7}\log_2\tfrac{5}{7} = 0.2857(1.807) + 0.7143(0.485) = 0.516 + 0.347 \approx 0.863 \text{ bits} $$

Step 2: split. Try the test $\text{height} > 7$. Left child (height $\le 7$): 4 oranges, 0 lemons. Right child (height $> 7$): 1 orange (the 7.4) and both lemons.

Step 3: children's entropies. Left is pure: $H = 0$. Right is a 2-lemon/1-orange mix:

$$ H_{\text{right}} = -\tfrac{2}{3}\log_2\tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3}\log_2\tfrac{1}{3} = 0.6667(0.585) + 0.3333(1.585) \approx 0.918 \text{ bits} $$

Step 4: weight and subtract. The left child gets $\tfrac{4}{7}$ of the examples, the right $\tfrac{3}{7}$:

$$ H(Y \mid \text{split}) = \tfrac{4}{7}(0) + \tfrac{3}{7}(0.918) \approx 0.394, \qquad IG = 0.863 - 0.394 \approx 0.470 \text{ bits} $$

Decent, but not the best available. Slide the threshold up to 8.0 and the split becomes perfectly pure on both sides: $IG = 0.863 - 0 = 0.863$ bits, the entire entropy of the node. You will verify this in code in Fig 5.2.

5. Building the tree greedily

We have the score; now the algorithm writes itself. At each node, consider every available split $s$ from the set of candidates $S$ (every feature, and for numerical features every threshold between consecutive values), and take the one that maximizes the gain:

$$ s^\star = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{s \in S} \left[ H(Y) - \sum_{v \in \text{children}(s)} p(v)\, H(Y \mid v) \right] $$

The full procedure, top to bottom:

  1. Start with the whole training set $D_{tr}$ at the root.
  2. At the current node, pick the best split $s^\star$ by the criterion above.
  3. Partition the node's examples according to $s^\star$, one child per answer.
  4. Recurse on each child, until its examples are pure or no useful split remains.
  5. Label each leaf by its class (the majority class, if it ends impure).

"Greedy" is the honest word for step 2: the algorithm grabs the best local improvement and never reconsiders. It might miss a globally better tree that starts with a mediocre first split. We accept that, because section 2 showed the alternative is NP-complete.

The same algorithm in Python

Three short functions and the whole learner exists. Entropy first, straight from the definition: 

import numpy as np

def entropy(labels):
    _, counts = np.unique(labels, return_counts=True)
    p = counts / counts.sum()
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()          # H = -Σ p log2 p

Information gain compares a parent to its children, weighting each child by the fraction of examples it receives: 

def information_gain(parent, children):
    n = len(parent)
    h_children = sum(len(c) / n * entropy(c) for c in children)
    return entropy(parent) - h_children     # IG = H(Y) - H(Y|split)

And the builder is the recursion from the algorithm box: find the best split, partition, recurse, stop at pure leaves or a depth limit: 

def build_tree(X, y, depth=0, max_depth=3):
    if len(np.unique(y)) == 1 or depth == max_depth:
        return Leaf(majority(y))            # pure enough, or out of budget
    feat, thr, gain = best_split(X, y)      # max information gain over all
    if gain <= 0:                           # features and thresholds
        return Leaf(majority(y))
    mask = X[:, feat] <= thr
    return Node(feat, thr,
                left=build_tree(X[mask], y[mask], depth + 1, max_depth),
                right=build_tree(X[~mask], y[~mask], depth + 1, max_depth))

6. Good trees, overfitting, and pruning

What makes a tree good? It has to be expressive enough to capture the real structure and simple enough to generalize, and those pull in opposite directions. Too small a tree underfits: its leaves stay impure because it never got to ask enough questions, and it misses structure that is genuinely there. Too large a tree overfits: its leaves become pure for the wrong reasons, purified by memorizing noise, and as a bonus it becomes unreadable, which throws away the interpretability that made us like trees in the first place. The tie-breaking principle is Occam's razor: prefer the simplest tree that explains the data. In practice that means small trees with the informative splits near the root, which is exactly what greedy information gain encourages.

Greedy growing has three specific failure modes worth naming. First, greedy choices are local: the best split now may not lead to the best tree overall. Second, every split slices the data thinner, so nodes deep in the tree make their decisions from tiny subsets, and statistics estimated from six examples are guesses. Third, grow deep enough and you arrive back at section 2's monster, one leaf per example. Watch all three happen to real data:

Fig 6.1, the depth dial. Left: the decision regions fracture into tiny slivers as depth grows, each sliver built to capture a few noisy points. Right: training accuracy climbs toward 100% while validation accuracy turns back down. Click the depth you would ship and the figure checks you against the computed optimum.

The fixes all limit tree size, and each one is a hyperparameter from section 1. Restrict the depth: stop asking questions after $d$ levels. Set a minimum leaf size: refuse splits that would leave a child with fewer than $m$ examples, so no rule ever rests on a handful of points. Require a minimum information gain: refuse splits that barely help, since they are usually fitting noise. And the fourth fix works after the fact: prune. Grow the tree generously, then delete branches that do not improve performance on a validation set, collapsing each deleted branch into a majority-vote leaf. Pruning gets to see the whole grown tree before deciding, which lets it undo greedy's early enthusiasm:

Fig 6.2, pruning an overgrown tree. Click internal nodes to collapse them into leaves; validation accuracy (computed on a real held-out set) updates instantly. The overgrown tree wins on training data and loses on validation; a few well-chosen cuts reverse that.

Two extensions the same machinery buys you

Continuous features we have already been handling all lesson: sort the observed values, try the threshold between each consecutive pair, and keep the threshold with maximum information gain. Nothing new is needed; the candidate set $S$ just includes all those thresholds. Regression trees take one more step: when the target is a continuous number rather than a class, entropy no longer applies, so choose splits that most reduce the squared error of each child around its mean instead, and let each leaf predict the mean target of its examples. Same greedy recursion, different purity ruler. (The end quiz asks you to design this yourself before you read it again.)

A second ruler: Gini impurity

Entropy is not the only way to score a node's confusion, and Assignment 2 uses the main alternative, so meet it now. The Gini impurity of a dataset $D$ with $C$ classes in proportions $p_i$ is

$$ \operatorname{Gini}(D) = 1 - \sum_{i=1}^{C} p_i^2 $$

Read it as: draw one example, guess a label by drawing again from the same mix, and Gini is the probability you guess wrong. Worked once, on the 2-lemon/5-orange node from section 4: $\operatorname{Gini} = 1 - (\tfrac{2}{7})^2 - (\tfrac{5}{7})^2 = 1 - \tfrac{4}{49} - \tfrac{25}{49} = \tfrac{20}{49} \approx 0.408$. Like entropy it is zero for a pure node and maximal at an even mix (0.5 instead of 1 bit for two classes), and splits are scored the same way, weighted parent-minus-children. The two rulers have slightly different shapes but nearly always rank splits the same way:

Fig 6.3, Gini vs entropy. Left: both curves over $p$; toggle the ÷2 scaling to see how close their shapes are. Right: both criteria scoring the same candidate split on the seven fruits from Fig 5.2, with the best split under each marked. Here, as usually, they agree.

k-NN or a tree? Same data, different bets

We now have two classifiers, and they disagree in an instructive way. k-NN bets that local similarity drives labels: it stores everything, compares at query time, and draws boundaries that follow the data's geometry. A tree bets that a short sequence of single-feature questions drives labels: it compresses training data into rules, never looks at it again, and draws axis-aligned boundaries. Reach for a tree when you want an interpretable rule you can read aloud, when features are mixed, discrete, missing, or badly scaled (a threshold does not care about units, which is why trees skip lecture 2's standardization ritual), or when prediction has to be fast: one root-to-leaf path instead of a scan of all $N$ points. Reach for k-NN when labels really are driven by local similarity, when you want a minimal-assumption baseline, or when a meaningful distance exists, as with images, text, and shapes. And when the truth is diagonal, watch what each one does:

Fig 6.4, k-NN vs tree on the same data, where the true boundary is the diagonal $x_2 = x_1$. k-NN's similarity boundary follows the diagonal naturally; the tree can only build a staircase out of axis-aligned cuts, and buying more steps costs depth and leaves.

One deployment story to make the stakes real: the Xbox Kinect classified the body part of every pixel of a depth image, in real time, using decision trees (grown into forests), trained on millions of examples (Shotton et al., CVPR 2011). Fast prediction is not a small perk; it is why a tree-based model sat inside a living room device instead of a datacentre.

Check your understanding

In bias/variance terms, what does pruning a grown tree do?

If pruning were free, we would prune to a stump. The gain in stability is paid for in expressiveness; the art is pruning only branches whose "structure" was noise.
Backwards on both counts: removing capacity makes a model more biased and less variable, not the reverse.
Right. Every capacity-limiting fix in this section (depth caps, minimum leaf size, minimum gain, pruning) trades bias up for variance down. The win in Fig 6.2 happens because the variance removed was hurting validation more than the added bias does.
Check: explain overfitting in trees, and three ways to fight it

Explain what overfitting looks like in a decision tree, name three concrete ways to reduce it, and give the bias/variance trade-off of each.

Answer. An overfit tree has grown deep enough to carve decision regions around individual training points, so its leaves are pure on training data (accuracy near 100%) while validation accuracy sags: the tree has encoded sampling noise as if it were structure, exactly the pattern in Fig 6.1. Three fixes: (1) a maximum-depth limit: caps how fine the partition can get; raises bias (real structure deeper than the cap is unreachable) and lowers variance (no more noise-sized slivers). (2) a minimum leaf size: every prediction must rest on at least $m$ examples; raises bias near genuine fine boundaries and lowers variance by refusing rules justified by a couple of points. (3) pruning on validation data: grow first, then delete branches that fail to help held-out performance; raises bias only where a branch is deleted and lowers variance most surgically, since the deletion decision is made with evidence the training fit never saw. (A minimum information gain per split is a fourth, with the same flavour.)

7. Summary and method card

The whole lesson in one paragraph. A decision tree predicts by playing twenty questions: internal nodes test single features, branches route the example, and one leaf answers. Every leaf owns an axis-aligned decision region, and a big enough tree can fit anything, including noise, which is why finding a good tree is a search for a small one. The optimal search is NP-complete, so we build greedily, and greedy needs a score: entropy measures a node's uncertainty in bits, and information gain, $IG = H(Y) - H(Y \mid X)$, measures how much a split reduces it. Grow by maximum gain, then defend against overfitting with depth limits, minimum leaf sizes, minimum gains, and pruning judged on validation data, exactly the discipline lecture 2 taught.

Method card · Decision Trees
AssumptionUseful decisions can be made by asking a sequence of simple feature-based questions.
Model classTree-structured rules: internal nodes test features, branches route examples, leaves output predictions.
Learning / selectionBuild greedily by choosing informative splits, typically scored by entropy and information gain (or Gini impurity).
PredictionRoute the input from the root to a leaf; output the leaf's class (majority) or mean target (regression trees).
StrengthsInterpretable, fast at test time, handles discrete/mixed features naturally, models nonlinear (axis-aligned) decision regions.
LimitationsCan overfit badly when grown too deep; greedy splitting may miss the globally best tree; axis-aligned cuts approximate diagonal structure poorly.
Key hyperparametersMaximum depth, minimum leaf size, minimum information gain, pruning policy (all tuned on validation data).

Where this sits in the course story: similarity (k-NN), then rules, this lecture, and next a bigger leap: parametric functions. Linear regression stops asking questions and stops storing examples; it summarizes data into a small set of numbers, the parameters of a function, chosen by minimizing a loss. Trees introduced the idea that training means optimizing a criterion; the rest of the course makes that criterion differentiable. Two names to remember from this one: Claude Shannon, whose entropy became our split score, and Leo Breiman, who took the tree you just built and grew it into random forests, the ensemble method waiting later in the course.

Quiz 3 (transfer): design the regression-tree split criterion

Targets are now continuous numbers (say, house prices), so entropy over class proportions no longer applies. Adapt the splitting criterion, and say how a leaf should predict.

Answer (one good version). Replace "uncertainty" with "spread". Score a set of targets by its squared error around its own mean, $SSE = \sum_i (t^{(i)} - \bar{t})^2$, and score a split by how much it reduces the total: choose the split maximizing $SSE_{\text{parent}} - (SSE_{\text{left}} + SSE_{\text{right}})$, the exact analogue of parent-minus-weighted-children. A leaf predicts the mean of its training targets, because the mean is the constant that minimizes squared error, which makes the criterion and the prediction rule two halves of one decision. The learned function is piecewise constant: one flat value per decision region. Everything else, greedy recursion, depth limits, minimum leaf size, pruning on validation, transfers without modification.

Quiz 4 (transfer): design a stopping rule and defend it

You must decide when to stop splitting. Propose a concrete stopping rule and argue what it trades off.

Answer (one good version). Stop splitting a node when any of the following holds: the node is pure; it holds fewer than $2m$ examples (so both children could not reach a minimum leaf size of $m$, say $m = 5$); or the best available split's information gain is below a threshold $\varepsilon$ (say 0.01 bits). The trade-off is bias versus variance, tuned by $m$ and $\varepsilon$. Set them high and you stop early: low variance, but real fine structure is never discovered (higher bias), and there is a subtle failure, since a split with near-zero gain can unlock high-gain splits below it (an XOR-style pattern, where neither feature helps alone but together they separate perfectly), which early stopping never sees. Set them low and you approach the one-leaf-per-point monster. That blindness of early stopping is the argument for the grow-then-prune strategy of Fig 6.2: growing sees the future, pruning judges it on validation data.

Quiz 5 (transfer): when does an axis-aligned tree struggle?

Describe a dataset geometry where decision trees are inherently clumsy, explain why, and say what that implies about choosing or fixing the model.

Answer (one good version). Any boundary that is genuinely diagonal, for instance "approve when income is at least three times the loan payment", a rule about $x_1 - 3x_2$, not about either feature alone. A tree must approximate that line with a staircase of axis-aligned cuts (Fig 6.4): each extra stair costs another split, accuracy improves only slowly with depth, and the many small leaves are each estimated from few examples, inviting the overfitting of section 6. Implications: representation matters, because a single engineered feature (the income-to-payment ratio) turns the diagonal into an axis-aligned threshold and collapses the tree to depth 1; alternatively pick a model whose boundaries suit the geometry (k-NN here, or later, linear models, which draw exactly the tilted lines trees cannot). The general lesson: every model family has boundary shapes it finds cheap and shapes it finds expensive, and knowing which is which is half of model selection.

That is decision trees: twenty questions, made rigorous. Prediction is a root-to-leaf walk, learning is a greedy hunt for questions with maximum information gain, and the danger is a tree big enough to memorize its own training noise, held back by depth limits, leaf sizes, and pruning judged on validation data. Next lecture we stop asking questions altogether: instead of rules or memories, we fit a function, and learning becomes the minimization of a loss. Linear regression.
