Attention and Transformers

The sequence-models lecture ended on a cliffhanger: seq2seq squeezes an entire input sentence into one vector, and one vector forgets. The fix is not a better memory, it is a different question. Instead of asking "how do I preserve everything I have read?", attention asks "which tokens are relevant right now?", and lets every word look directly at every other word, in parallel. That one idea is the transformer, and the transformer is every modern LLM. This capstone builds attention from a single dot product up to the full block, then switches hats and treats the result as an engineering artifact: what it costs to train and to call, how a raw text predictor becomes a helpful assistant, and how to choose a model without burning the budget.

0. The hook: one vector is not enough

Recall exactly where the last lecture left us. A basic seq2seq model reads the whole input, one token at a time, and hands the decoder a single final hidden state: one fixed-size vector that is supposed to summarize everything. The decoder leans on that summary, and the summary favours whatever arrived last. Forget one early word and the meaning flips: drop the "Don't" from "Don't eat the pizza" and you have changed the instruction into its opposite. Or take "The animal did not cross the street because it was exhausted"; resolving what "it" refers to requires holding "animal" in mind across the whole clause, which is precisely the kind of early information a running summary overwrites.

Two problems, and they share one root cause. Memory: early information gets overwritten, and gradients vanish across long ranges, so the model cannot even learn to keep what matters. Speed: computation is sequential, token $t$ waits for token $t-1$, so no amount of parallel hardware helps. The shared cause is that information must travel through every step in between. Gates made the memory more selective, bidirectional and stacked cells helped at the margins, but nothing removed the relay race itself.

So ask the different question. What if a word did not need information to be carried to it, step by step, because it could simply look at any other word directly, all pairs at once, in parallel? That mechanism exists, it is called attention: a learned mechanism that lets each position retrieve information from other positions by relevance. Here is what it feels like before any math. Pick a word and watch which other words it lights up:

Fig 0.1, attention as a learned highlighter. Click any word to make it the query; every other word is shaded by its attention weight, computed live by a softmax over dot-product similarities. "he" lights up "Amir" from four words away with no relay through the words in between, which is exactly what the seq2seq bottleneck could not do.

That reframing is the big idea of the whole lecture: attention takes sequence modeling from a memory-preservation problem to a retrieval problem. The rest of the technical half makes the highlighter precise, and the second half of the lecture deals with what happened when the world scaled it up.

1. Attention: retrieval, not memory

Seq2seq attention: retrieve from every encoder state

The first version of the idea patches seq2seq directly. The encoder already produces a hidden state for every input position, $h_1, \dots, h_T$; the bottleneck was that we threw away all but the last. Attention keeps them all, and at each decoder step $i$ it lets the decoder state $s_i$ ask every encoder state how relevant it is. A dot product scores the match, a softmax turns scores into weights, and a weighted sum builds a custom context vector for this step:

$$ \operatorname{score}(s_i, h_j) = s_i^\top h_j, \qquad \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\operatorname{score}(s_i, h_j))}{\sum_{j'=1}^{T}\exp(\operatorname{score}(s_i, h_{j'}))}, \qquad c_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T} \alpha_{ij} h_j. $$

Read the three pieces left to right: score everything, normalize the scores into a probability distribution over positions (the same softmax we built in the logistic-regression lecture, doing the same job), then blend the states with those probabilities. No single vector ever has to summarize the whole input again, because each decoder step retrieves exactly what it needs, from wherever it sits.

Self-attention: queries, keys, and values

Now drop the encoder-decoder scaffolding and turn the mechanism inward. Self-attention is attention within one sequence, token to token, so "he" can retrieve from "Amir" directly and every token's representation becomes aware of its relationships to the rest of the sentence. To do that cleanly, each token emits three different vectors, because it plays three different roles:

🔎
Query $q_i$
What am I looking for? The probe this token sends out when it scans the sentence.
🏷️
Key $k_i$
What do I offer? The label other tokens' queries are matched against.
📦
Value $v_i$
What do I pass on? The content that actually flows to whoever attends here.

The mechanics are the same score, softmax, weighted-sum pipeline, with one new ingredient:

$$ \operatorname{score}(q_i, k_j) = \frac{q_i^\top k_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}, \qquad \alpha_{ij} = \frac{\exp(\operatorname{score}(q_i, k_j))}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{T}\exp(\operatorname{score}(q_i, k_\ell))}, \qquad z_i = \sum_{j=1}^{T} \alpha_{ij} v_j. $$

The new ingredient is the $1/\sqrt{d_k}$. It is a temperature scaling: in high dimensions, dot products between random-ish vectors grow with the dimension, and a softmax fed huge scores saturates into a one-hot spike with vanishing gradients everywhere else. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the scores numerically moderate before the softmax, so the weights stay soft and trainable. You will see the effect directly in Fig 1.2's toggle.

The matrix form

Stack the token embeddings as the rows of $X \in \mathbb{R}^{T \times d_{\text{model}}}$. Three learned projection matrices turn every token's embedding into its query, key, and value in one shot, and the whole mechanism collapses to one line:

$$ Q = X W_Q, \quad K = X W_K, \quad V = X W_V, \qquad \operatorname{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V. $$

The object in the middle, $\operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})$, is a $T \times T$ matrix worth staring at: entry $(i, j)$ is how much token $i$ attends to token $j$, and every row sums to 1. Notice what self-attention does and does not change. The shape is preserved, $T \times d_{\text{model}}$ in and $T \times d_{\text{model}}$ out, but the meaning is transformed: each input row was an independent token embedding, and each output row is a context-aware blend of the whole sentence.

The code

The whole mechanism is four lines of NumPy. Note where the mask argument enters; section 4 will use it to change the model family without touching anything else: 

import numpy as np

def softmax(z):
    e = np.exp(z - z.max(axis=-1, keepdims=True))
    return e / e.sum(axis=-1, keepdims=True)

def self_attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, M=None):
    Q, K, V = X @ Wq, X @ Wk, X @ Wv
    S = Q @ K.T                        # T x T raw scores
    if M is not None:                  # mask: 0 (allowed) or -inf (forbidden)
        S = S + M
    A = softmax(S / np.sqrt(K.shape[1]))   # rows sum to 1
    return A, A @ V                    # attention matrix, output

Check: what exactly does the 1/√dk buy us?

Suppose we deleted the $1/\sqrt{d_k}$ from the score. Nothing in the formula becomes ill-defined, so why is it there?

Answer. It is a temperature control on the softmax. Dot products grow with the dimension of the vectors: at $d_k = 512$, typical scores are an order of magnitude larger than at $d_k = 2$, purely from summing more terms. Feed a softmax scores like 40 and 20 and it returns weights like 0.9999999 and 0.0000001: effectively one-hot. That has two costs. The retrieval loses its softness (no partial credit for second-best matches), and the gradient of the softmax at saturation is nearly zero, so the projections stop learning. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ cancels the dimension-driven growth and keeps the scores numerically moderate, so the weights stay soft and the gradients stay alive. Flip the toggle in Fig 1.2 and read the max score and the row entropy with and without it.

2. Multi-head attention: several lenses at once

One attention head learns one $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ triple, so it highlights one kind of relationship. But a sentence has several things going on at once: who refers to whom, who does what, what follows what, where things happen. Asking one head to encode all of those in a single $T \times T$ pattern is asking one highlighter to mark grammar, plot, and geography in the same colour. So run several attention operations in parallel, each with its own projections, each a different relational lens:

$$ Q^{(h)} = X W_Q^{(h)}, \quad A^{(h)} = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q^{(h)}K^{(h)\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right), \quad O^{(h)} = A^{(h)} V^{(h)}, $$ $$ \operatorname{MHA}(X) = \operatorname{Concat}(O^{(1)}, \dots, O^{(H)})\, W_O. $$

Each head produces its own attention matrix and its own output; the outputs are concatenated side by side and a final learned projection $W_O$ mixes them back down to $d_{\text{model}}$ columns. Nothing about any single head changed; multi-head attention is just $H$ copies of section 1 plus one linear layer. What you gain is coverage: different heads catch different patterns.

Fig 2.1, four heads, four lenses, one sentence. Switch heads and the attention matrix reorganizes: one head tracks reference (pronoun to name), one tracks actions, one tracks local sequence, one tracks location. The Combine view concatenates the four head outputs and applies $W_O$, all computed live.

Check: why several small heads instead of one big one?

Multi-head attention with $H = 4$ heads of dimension $d_k$ costs about the same as one head of dimension $4 d_k$. What does splitting buy?

Answer. A single head produces a single $T \times T$ weight pattern, so at each position it commits to one blend of the sentence. Four heads produce four independent patterns, so the same token can simultaneously attend to its antecedent through one lens, the verb through another, and its neighbours through a third, as in Fig 2.1. One wide head has more capacity per score but still only one softmax per row; it must average competing relationships into a compromise pattern. Several lenses at once beats one thick lens, which is the source of the name: each head is a different relational view, and $W_O$ learns how to combine the views.

3. The transformer block

"Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017) did something more radical than adding attention to an RNN: it removed the recurrence entirely and kept only attention. No hidden state carried step to step means no waiting for token $t-1$, which makes training massively parallel, and that parallelism is what unlocked the scale behind modern LLMs. Worth pausing on: the architecture you license when you "buy an LLM" today is nearly a decade old. What changed since 2017 is scale, data, and the system around the model, a point the second half of this lecture takes seriously.

But attention alone is not a full layer. Three gaps need filling, and each addition earns its seat.

Positional encoding: attention is order-blind

Look back at $\operatorname{softmax}(QK^\top/\sqrt{d_k})V$ and notice what it never uses: the positions. Shuffle the rows of $X$ and each token's output is the same blend of the same values. That is fatal for language, because order changes meaning: "good, not bad" and "bad, not good" contain identical tokens and opposite judgements. The fix is to add a position-dependent vector to each embedding, $z_i = x_i + p_i$, so the same word at a different position presents a different vector to the attention. The original transformer uses sinusoids at geometrically spaced frequencies:

$$ P_{i, 2j} = \sin\!\left(\frac{i}{10000^{2j/d_{\text{model}}}}\right), \qquad P_{i, 2j+1} = \cos\!\left(\frac{i}{10000^{2j/d_{\text{model}}}}\right). $$

Modern LLMs use other schemes, but the role is always the same: inject order. See both halves of the claim below, the failure without it and the striped structure of the fix:

Fig 3.1, positional encoding, off and on. Left: with the encoding off, attention computes the identical output vector for "not" in "good, not bad" and "bad, not good", the two sentences are literally indistinguishable to it; switch the encoding on and the outputs separate, all values computed live. Right: the sinusoidal $P$ matrix as a heatmap (positions down, dimensions across), fast stripes in early dimensions and slow stripes in later ones, giving every position a unique fingerprint.

Check your understanding

Why does self-attention need positional encoding when an RNN never did?

Gates control how much information flows, not where it came from. The RNN's order-awareness comes from its sequential reading, not its gating.
It is not optional: without it the model cannot represent word order at all, and Fig 3.1 shows two opposite sentences becoming identical. That is a broken model of language, not a small accuracy dip.
Right. Order was implicit in the RNN's processing sequence. Attention traded that away for parallelism, so order has to be injected back into the data itself, $z_i = x_i + p_i$.

The full block: mix, then process, with safety rails

The second gap: attention only ever computes weighted averages of value vectors, so we add a position-wise feed-forward network, the same two-layer MLP applied to every token independently, to actually transform each blended representation. The third gap: deep stacks of anything are hard to train, which is backpropagation-lecture territory, so each sub-layer is wrapped in a residual connection and a normalization. One transformer block, updating the whole sequence in parallel:

$$ \tilde{X} = X + P \quad\text{(inject order)} $$ $$ U = \operatorname{Norm}\!\big(\tilde{X} + \operatorname{MHA}(\tilde{X})\big) \quad\text{(mix across tokens)} $$ $$ X' = \operatorname{Norm}\!\big(U + \operatorname{FFN}(U)\big), \qquad \operatorname{FFN}(u_i) = W_2\, \sigma(W_1 u_i + b_1) + b_2 \quad\text{(process each token)} $$

The division of labour is clean: attention is the only place tokens talk to each other, and the FFN is where each token digests what it heard. Walk through it, shape by shape:

Fig 3.2, one transformer block, executed step by step on the toy sentence. Each press advances one sub-step, $X \to X + P \to \operatorname{MHA} \to \operatorname{Norm} \to \operatorname{FFN} \to \operatorname{Norm} \to X'$, lighting up the active stage and showing the actual $T \times d_{\text{model}}$ matrix at that point, with shapes annotated. Then stack blocks and watch the same sequence get reread, pass after pass.

The stack control at the end of that figure is the right mental model for depth. Stacking blocks is rereading. Each pass builds on the last: early passes resolve tokens, middle passes assemble syntax, later passes work at the level of meaning. It is the same spirit as the CNN hierarchy from Lecture 11, edges to parts to objects, transplanted to language. Depth is how many times the model effectively rereads, and more layers buy abstraction at more compute per token.

4. Architecture families: same block, different masks

Every headline model you have heard of stacks the block from section 3. The differences between the families are wiring, training objective, and above all who is allowed to look at whom:

  • Encoder-only (BERT, RoBERTa): full bidirectional attention, every token sees the whole sentence. Great for understanding tasks: search, classification, ranking.
  • Decoder-only (GPT, Llama, Claude): causal left-to-right attention, each token sees only its past. The dominant chat and LLM design: prompt in, text out.
  • Encoder-decoder (T5, the original translation transformer): read the input with an encoder, generate with a decoder. Natural for "transform A into B": translation, summarization.

Here is the elegant part: the core formula is shared, and one extra matrix decides the family. A mask $M$ is added to the scores before the softmax, with $M_{ij} = 0$ where token $i$ may attend to token $j$ and $M_{ij} = -\infty$ where it may not:

$$ A = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top + M}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right), \qquad M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & i \text{ may attend to } j \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases} $$

A score of $-\infty$ exponentiates to exactly zero, so forbidden cells vanish from the softmax and the remaining weights renormalize over the allowed ones. Encoder-only uses the all-zeros mask; decoder-only uses the causal, lower-triangular mask; encoder-decoder combines encoder self-attention, masked decoder self-attention, and cross-attention where decoder queries meet encoder keys and values. In code: 

def causal_mask(T):
    M = np.zeros((T, T))
    M[np.triu_indices(T, k=1)] = -np.inf   # j > i: the future is forbidden
    return M

A, O = self_attention(X, Wq, Wk, Wv, M=causal_mask(4))

Fig 4.1, one attention computation, two model families. Toggle the mask: full (bidirectional, the encoder-only family, BERT) versus causal (lower-triangular, the decoder-only family, GPT). The mask matrix shows its 0 and $-\infty$ entries, the forbidden score cells grey out, and the softmax rows renormalize live over what remains; every row still sums to 1.

Worked by hand: the causal mask hits the "went" row

Take the toy sentence's second row. Unmasked, "went" scored all four tokens: scaled scores $[0, 1.41, 0.71, 1.41]$, weights $[0.089, 0.365, 0.180, 0.365]$. Apply the causal mask and the "to" and "class" cells get $-\infty$: they exponentiate to zero and disappear. The two surviving scores $[0, 1.41]$ renormalize:

αwent = [e⁰, e¹⋅⁴¹] / (e⁰ + e¹⋅⁴¹) = [1, 4.11] / 5.11 = [0.196, 0.804, 0, 0]
zwent = 0.196·[1,1] + 0.804·[2,2] = [1.804, 1.804]

Same formula, same weights matrices, and "went" now writes its representation using only "Amir" and itself. That single change of mask is the entire difference in attention pattern between a BERT-style layer and a GPT-style layer.

And what is GPT, exactly? A decoder-only transformer trained on next-token prediction, the very objective from the language-modeling lecture:

$$ L = -\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}). $$

Not a different attention mechanism, not a new theory of language: the causal mask makes every position a training example for "predict the next token", and the cross-entropy loss you already know does the rest.

5. Building useful LLMs: the training pipeline

Now put the architect's hat down and pick up the builder's. A short history locates us. 2017: the transformer. 2018: the field splits, BERT takes understanding and GPT-1 takes generation. 2019 to 2020: GPT-2 and GPT-3 show that scale alone yields few-shot ability and emergent properties nobody explicitly trained for. 2022: InstructGPT and ChatGPT make models usable via alignment, and the world notices. 2023 onward: open-weight families (Llama, Mistral, Qwen), multimodal models, long context, and steadily falling prices. The 2022 inflection deserves emphasis because it was not a smarter architecture: it was alignment plus product. Capability and usability are different investments, and the pipeline below is where usability comes from.

From raw text to helpful assistant takes three stages, and they are wildly unequal in cost:

  1. Pretraining: predict the next token on internet-scale text. Buys broad knowledge and fluency. This is the expensive step: months on thousands of GPUs, tens of millions of dollars, and it is what you are really paying for in a frontier model.
  2. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): imitate curated instruction-answer pairs. Buys instruction following: the difference between a text continuer and something that answers you.
  3. Preference tuning (RLHF or DPO): learn from human "which answer is better?" comparisons. Buys a helpful, harmless tone and safer refusals.

Fig 5.1, the training-pipeline map. Click each stage for what it buys, what it costs, and its characteristic failure mode: pretraining bakes in stale facts and biases, SFT teaches the style of good answers rather than guaranteed correctness, and preference tuning can induce sycophancy and over-refusal. The bracket marks what "fine-tuning" actually touches: stages 2 and 3, on top of someone else's pretraining.

Two consequences matter for anyone building on these models. First, SFT and preference tuning are comparatively cheap but decide usability and safety, so when you "fine-tune" a model you are almost always adjusting behaviour and style in stages 2 and 3, not adding knowledge to stage 1. Second, a fluent, confident answer is a trained behaviour, not evidence of truth. The preference stage rewarded answers people liked, and people like confidence. Alignment improves manners and safety; it does not improve factual grounding. Grounding is what retrieval (RAG) and evaluation are for, which is precisely where section 7 goes.

6. Foundation models and multimodality

Why did the transformer, of all architectures, take over? Three properties compound into trainability at scale. Parallelism: all positions compute at once, a perfect fit for GPUs, where an RNN's step-by-step loop leaves the hardware idle. Direct connections: any token reaches any other in one attention step, so no gradient has to survive a long relay. Generality: nothing in the block knows what a "token" is. It can be a word, an image patch, an audio slice, or a line of code, and the same architecture processes all of them.

That last property is where multimodality comes from. A Vision Transformer (ViT) cuts an image into patches and treats the patches like words: embed each one, add positions, feed the same block. CLIP trains a text encoder and an image encoder to place matching text and images near each other in one shared embedding space, which is what makes cross-modal search and text-to-image generation possible. Try the tokenizer move yourself:

Fig 6.1, an image becomes a sentence. Slide the patch size: the image is cut into patches, each patch is flattened into a token vector, and the resulting sequence (length computed live) feeds the identical transformer block from section 3. Smaller patches mean a longer sequence, a trade-off section 7 will price via the $n^2$ attention cost.

Scale plus generality produced a new kind of artifact. A foundation model is a model pretrained once at huge cost and reused across many tasks, off the shelf, via prompting, fine-tuning, or retrieval. Most organizations consume foundation models rather than train their own, and that sentence has teeth: it means a core capability of your product may come from an external vendor, with their pricing, their availability, their deprecation schedule, and their policies. Building on a foundation model is a supply-chain decision, the same category of decision as choosing a cloud provider or a critical component supplier, and the sane response is the same: plan for portability.

Check: why did transformers scale where RNNs could not?

LSTMs were state of the art for a decade and had plenty of parameters available. What specifically stopped "just make the LSTM bigger" from producing GPT-3, and what did the transformer change?

Answer. Two of the three scaling properties were missing. First, parallelism: an RNN must compute step $t-1$ before step $t$, so training time grows with sequence length no matter how many GPUs you buy, while a transformer computes every position of every sequence simultaneously and soaks up all the hardware you give it. Second, direct connections: in an RNN, a gradient linking word 1 to word 500 crosses 499 multiplications, the vanishing-gradient relay from last lecture, while attention connects the two words in one step, so long-range structure is learnable at scale. Add generality (the same block for any token type) and you get an architecture where more compute and more data reliably buy more capability, which is what the scaling laws in section 7 quantify.

7. Choosing a model: cost, context, scaling laws

Build, buy, or adapt

Every "we need an LLM" conversation lands on one of three options. Buy a closed API (GPT, Claude, Gemini): the fastest and strongest option, least control, and your data leaves your boundary. Adapt open weights (Llama, Mistral, Qwen): control, on-prem privacy, no per-token vendor fee, but you own the ops burden, the GPUs, the serving stack, the upgrades. Build, as in pretrain your own: almost never justified outside a handful of labs, for reasons the scaling numbers below make brutal. And within adapt, keep the two tools straight: fine-tuning changes behaviour and format, not knowledge; RAG injects knowledge without retraining. Most "we need a custom model" requests dissolve, on inspection, into prompting plus RAG plus light fine-tuning on a strong base.

Tokenization and the cost of a call

The model does not see words; it sees tokens, sub-word chunks from the same tokenization story as the language-modeling lecture. The English rule of thumb is about 1 token per 4 characters, so a 20-page document is roughly 12,000 to 15,000 tokens. You pay per token, in and out, at different rates:

$$ \text{cost} = (\text{tokens in} \times p_{\text{in}}) + (\text{tokens out} \times p_{\text{out}}). $$ 
def cost_per_call(tokens_in, tokens_out, p_in, p_out):
    # p_in, p_out are dollars per 1M tokens
    return tokens_in * p_in / 1e6 + tokens_out * p_out / 1e6

cost_per_call(12000, 500, 3.00, 15.00)   # 0.036 + 0.0075 = 0.0435, about $0.044

Run the worked example: a support bot that stuffs a 12,000-token product manual into every prompt and gets 500 tokens back, at $3 per million input tokens and $15 per million output tokens, costs $12000 \times 3/10^6 + 500 \times 15/10^6 = \$0.036 + \$0.0075 \approx \$0.044$ per query. Harmless, until you multiply: at 100,000 queries a month that is about $4,400 a month, and the bill is utterly dominated by the stuffed input. Now notice what RAG does to it. Retrieve the two relevant paragraphs instead of shipping the manual, roughly a 20x cut in input tokens, and the same traffic costs about $930 a month. Feel the arithmetic move:

Right, three cents. And note the structure: the input term is twice the output term here, so if this call is stuffing context, retrieval is where the savings live. At a million calls a month this is $30,000, which is why the formula deserves respect.
Output tokens are billed too, and typically at a several-times-higher rate, because generating them is the slow sequential part. Both terms count.

The n² tax on context

Why does everyone obsess over input length? Because self-attention compares every token to every other token: the score matrix is $T \times T$, so compute and memory grow like $n^2$ in the sequence length. Double the context, pay roughly four times the attention cost. Engineering like FlashAttention cuts the memory traffic and makes the constant factors far friendlier, but the fundamental $n^2$ scaling remains, which is why retrieving the right few chunks so often beats brute-forcing a longer context:

Fig 7.2, the $n^2$ attention-cost curve against an ideal linear line, both normalized to an 8k-token baseline and computed live. Slide the context length or press Double: doubling $n$ multiplies attention compute by 4, and the gap between the curves is the price of every token attending to every other token.

Latency: the decoder is the slow part

One asymmetry to internalize. The encoder side of a call reads all input tokens at once, in parallel; the decoder emits output one token at a time, each conditioned on the last. So output length drives latency more than input length does. Two numbers to watch: time-to-first-token, the responsiveness a user feels, and tokens-per-second, the sustained throughput. The standard playbook follows directly: stream tokens so users see progress immediately, cap output length, and put smaller, faster models on latency-sensitive paths.

Context windows: working memory, not a database

The context window is the maximum number of tokens the model can consider at once, and everything competes for it: the system prompt, the retrieved documents, the conversation so far, and the answer being generated. Everything outside the window is invisible, and there is no memory between calls unless you re-send it yourself. Bigger windows exist, but they raise cost and latency (that $n^2$ again), and models often attend poorly to the middle of very long inputs, the "lost in the middle" effect: material near the start and end gets used, material buried in the centre gets skimmed.

Fig 7.3, the context window as working memory. Grow the conversation and watch the oldest turns fall off the fixed window and become invisible to the model; drag the key fact along the input and its retrieval strength follows the "lost in the middle" curve, strong near the edges and weak in the centre. A window is working memory, not a database: the durable fix is retrieving the right few chunks per call.

Training compute and scaling laws

Finally, the numbers that explain why pretraining concentrates in a few labs. Training cost is roughly (model size) times (tokens seen), and both factors are limited by GPUs, memory, and time; frontier runs use thousands of GPUs for weeks to months and cost tens of millions of dollars. The surprising part is how predictable the returns are: scaling laws show loss falling smoothly as parameters, data, and compute grow, smoothly enough that performance can be budgeted in advance like any other engineering quantity.

The refinement with teeth is Chinchilla (2022): many headline models were undertrained, all parameters and not enough data. For a fixed compute budget, a smaller model trained on more tokens can beat a bigger one trained on fewer. That is why today's strong models are often smaller but trained longer, and, as a bonus, cheaper to serve. The buyer's version of the lesson: the cheapest model that clears your quality bar is often a mid-size, well-trained one, not the biggest name on the price list.

Fig 7.4, the Chinchilla trade-off, qualitatively. Fix a compute budget, then slide the split between parameters and training tokens: the stylized loss curve is computed live and its minimum sits away from the "biggest possible model" end, so an oversized, undertrained model loses to a smaller, longer-trained one. Raise the budget and the whole curve drops, predictably.

The cost is quadratic, not linear: 4x the tokens means roughly $4^2 = 16$x the attention compute, before counting the lost-in-the-middle quality problem.

8. Summary and method card

One idea carried the technical half: replace memory with retrieval. A query asks, keys answer, a softmax turns match quality into weights, and a weighted sum of values builds each token a context-aware representation, all pairs at once, in parallel. Multi-head attention runs several relational lenses simultaneously; positional encoding puts order back; the feed-forward network processes what attention gathered; residuals and normalization make the stack trainable; and a single mask matrix decides whether the result behaves like BERT, GPT, or a translator. One idea carried the systems half: the model is the easy part now. The hard parts of enterprise AI are the data, the cost, and the system around the model: the pipeline that makes it usable, the tokens that make it expensive, the window that bounds its working memory, and the scaling laws that say what a budget can buy.

Where this sits in the course story: this is the destination the whole neural arc was walking toward. Backpropagation gave us training, CNNs showed that architecture should mirror the structure of the data, language modeling turned text into prediction, sequence models hit the memory wall, and attention dissolved the wall by turning memory into retrieval. What remains, beyond this course's finish line, is the systems discipline of making these models reliable.

9. End-of-lesson quiz

Two quick checks, then four transfer questions that ask you to decide and justify, not recall. Attempt each before expanding the answer.

Quiz 1

In self-attention, what are the roles of the query, key, and value vectors that each token emits?

Position enters through the positional encoding added to the embedding, not through the key; and none of the three is a part-of-speech tag. Think retrieval: probe, label, content.
Right. Queries score against keys to decide the weights, and the weights blend values. Three roles, three learned projections of the same embedding.
They come from three different learned matrices $W_Q, W_K, W_V$ precisely so the three roles can differ; identical copies would force a token to offer exactly what it seeks.
Quiz 2

What did each stage of the LLM pipeline buy, and what is each one's characteristic failure mode?

Backwards on all three: safety work happens in preference tuning, knowledge comes from pretraining, and no stage buys speed (that is serving engineering).
The later stages are small and cheap compared to pretraining and adjust behaviour, not knowledge. That is exactly why fine-tuning on your docs does not keep a model current.
Right, and the one-line consequence: a fluent, confident answer is a trained behaviour, not evidence of truth, so grounding needs retrieval and evaluation, not more alignment.
Quiz 3 (transfer): build, buy, or adapt, and defend it

A hospital network wants an assistant that answers clinicians' questions from its internal care protocols. Requirements: patient data must never leave the hospital's infrastructure, protocols change monthly, the team has solid engineers but no ML research group, and the budget is real but not frontier-lab real. Build, buy, or adapt, and what is the knowledge strategy?

Answer. Adapt open weights, with RAG carrying the knowledge. The privacy constraint rules out closed APIs in their default form: prompts containing patient context would cross the vendor boundary. Pretraining their own model is out on cost and staffing; it is almost never justified outside a few labs. So: an open-weight model (Llama, Mistral, Qwen class) served on-prem, which trades a per-token vendor fee for an ops burden their engineers can carry. Monthly-changing protocols are the giveaway for the knowledge strategy: fine-tuning changes behaviour and format, not knowledge, and re-tuning every month would still leave facts baked in and stale. Retrieval injects the current protocol text into the context at query time, so an update is a document refresh, not a training run. Light fine-tuning is still sensible for tone and format (clinical style, citation habits), which is exactly what it is good at. This is the standard dissolution: prompting plus RAG plus light fine-tuning on a strong base, and plan for portability so the base model can be swapped when a better one ships.

Quiz 4 (transfer): when a bigger context window is the wrong fix

A legal-tech team's contract analyzer misses clauses buried deep in long agreements. The proposed fix is upgrading to a model with a 10x larger context window and stuffing entire contracts into every call. Argue the case against, and propose the alternative.

Answer. Three arguments, in increasing order of severity. Cost: every input token is billed on every call, so 10x the context is roughly 10x the input bill for the same answer, and the attention compute underneath grows like $n^2$, roughly 100x, which shows up as latency and price. Quality: long inputs suffer the lost-in-the-middle effect, and a clause buried at page 40 of 80 is sitting exactly where attention is weakest, so the fix targets the symptom with a mechanism that is weak at the very position causing the symptom. Architecture: a context window is working memory with no persistence between calls; the contract corpus would be re-shipped on every query forever. The alternative is retrieval: index the contracts, embed and search for the clauses relevant to the question, and place those few chunks near the start or end of a modest prompt. That converts an $n^2$ brute-force scan into a targeted lookup, cuts input tokens by an order of magnitude (the same 20x arithmetic as Fig 7.1), and puts the critical text where the model attends well. A bigger window is the right tool when the task genuinely needs simultaneous reasoning over the whole document; it is the wrong tool for finding needles.

Quiz 5 (transfer): the Chinchilla lesson as a buying guide

Two API models clear your quality bar in evaluation: a famous very large model and a mid-size model at a fifth of the per-token price. A stakeholder insists on the large one "because more parameters means more capability". Use the scaling-law story to respond.

Answer. The premise is the exact error Chinchilla exposed. Loss scales predictably with parameters, data, and compute together, and for a fixed compute budget many large models turn out undertrained: a smaller model trained on more tokens beats a bigger one trained on fewer. Parameter count alone is therefore not a capability ranking, and the market reflects this: today's strong models are often smaller but trained longer, and cheaper to serve precisely because serving cost scales with size. The decision procedure is already in the question: both models clear the quality bar, and the bar is the requirement. Paying 5x per token buys headroom you have measured no need for, on every call, forever. Pick the cheapest model that clears the bar, keep the evaluation harness so the decision can be revisited when either model or the bar changes, and spend the saved budget where this lecture says the real problems live: retrieval, evaluation, and the system around the model.

Quiz 6 (transfer): self-attention as a soft k-NN

Way back in Lecture 2, k-nearest-neighbours answered queries by finding similar stored points and combining their labels. Make the analogy to self-attention precise: what plays the role of the query, the stored points, the similarity, and the vote, and what is the crucial difference?

Answer. The mapping is nearly one-to-one. The query vector $q_i$ is the k-NN query point; the keys are the stored points it is compared against; the scaled dot product $q^\top k / \sqrt{d_k}$ is the similarity function standing in for negative distance; and the output $z_i = \sum_j \alpha_{ij} v_j$ is the vote, a similarity-weighted combination of stored content, with values as the "labels". Two differences matter. First, softness: k-NN makes a hard cut at k neighbours, while the softmax gives every token a nonzero, differentiable weight, a soft k-NN over the sequence, which is what lets gradients flow and the whole thing train end to end. Second, and deeper: k-NN's stored points are fixed training data with a fixed metric, while attention learns its representations. $W_Q$ and $W_K$ learn what "similar" should mean and $W_V$ learns what to return, and the database being queried is the sentence itself, rebuilt at every layer. Retrieval as the organizing idea, from the second lecture of the course to the last.

That is the capstone. Attention turned "remember everything" into "look it up", the transformer block made that idea stackable and trainable, masks turned one block into three model families, and scale turned the result into foundation models that most of the world consumes rather than builds. The engineering that follows, tokens and prices, context budgets, retrieval, scaling laws, is where ML meets the systems around it, and it is where your work as a practitioner will actually happen. The course started with a dot product deciding which neighbour to trust; it ends with a dot product deciding which word to read. It was retrieval all along.
