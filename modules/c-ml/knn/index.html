k-Nearest Neighbours

The first learning algorithm of the course, and the most honest one. It has no equations to fit and no parameters to train. It just remembers everything it has seen and answers every question the same way: find the most similar past examples and copy their answer. That one idea forces us to invent most of machine learning's vocabulary: data as vectors, distance, decision boundaries, hyperparameters, validation. This lesson builds all of it from zero, with an interactive figure for every concept.

0. Similar things, similar labels

You already run this algorithm in your head. A friend asks what show to watch next, and you think of the people whose taste is most like theirs, then recommend what those people loved. You find an unfamiliar fruit at the market, and you decide it is probably sweet because it looks and smells like a mango. No formula, no training, just one bet: similar things have similar labels.

That single bet, taken literally, is the entire algorithm we build today. Try it before any math. Drag the mystery point below around the scatter and watch which labelled example it trusts:

Fig 0.1, the whole idea in one picture. Drag the mystery point; its nearest labelled example lights up, and the mystery point simply copies that label. No equations were involved, yet.

Everything else in this lesson is what happens when you take that picture seriously. What exactly is a "point"? What does "nearest" mean, precisely? What happens when the nearest example is wrong? And when does the whole bet collapse? Those four questions are sections 1, 2, 3, and 5.

1. Preliminaries: turning the world into data

Before any algorithm can find "similar" examples, the world has to become something a computer can compare. Start with the vocabulary. Data is facts and statistics collected into a form that is efficient to move and process, and it arrives in many shapes: text, images, video, tables, graphs. A dataset is a structured collection of data tied to one body of work, one problem you are trying to solve.

Look inside a dataset and each column, each measured attribute, is a feature. Features come in four types, and the type decides what math is legal on them. The classic example is a table of planets:

  • Continuous: any real value in a range. A planet's mass.
  • Ordinal: ordered categories where order means something but gaps do not. A planet's order from the sun.
  • Discrete: countable whole numbers. How many moons.
  • Nominal: named categories with no order at all. Gas giant or rocky.

Prove to yourself you can tell them apart:

Fig 1.1, the feature-type sorter. Click an attribute, then the bucket it belongs in. Instant feedback; the distinctions matter because distance math treats each type differently.

Two numbers describe a dataset's overall shape. Cardinality is how many sample instances it holds, the number of rows. Dimensionality is how many features describe each instance, the number of columns. When people say big data, the informal meaning is that cardinality and/or dimensionality is heading toward infinity. Netflix's classic ratings matrix is 480,189 users by 17,770 movies, big in both directions at once.

Big data hides a saving grace: redundancy. Data can capture too little information (digitization approximates the world) or too much (temperature recorded in Celsius and Fahrenheit is one fact stored twice; near-duplicate users are near-duplicate rows). High-dimensional data usually varies along far fewer real degrees of variation than its raw dimensionality suggests: face images are huge vectors, but what actually changes between them is orientation, expression, lighting. Similar movies exist, and similar users exist, and that structure is exploitable. Later in the course, dimensionality reduction is the tool that surfaces it. Today, redundancy is the reason a nearest-neighbour bet has any hope: if nothing in your dataset resembled anything else, "most similar example" would be meaningless.

Representation: everything becomes a vector

A representation is a way to measure, compare, and transform data on a computer. The common strategy across all of machine learning is blunt and powerful: represent every data point as a vector in Euclidean space $\mathbb{R}^d$. Once data is a vector, the whole arsenal of linear algebra applies, and "how similar are these two things" becomes "how far apart are these two vectors", a question with a formula.

For tabular data this is natural, each row is already a list of numbers. The surprising case is images. An image is a grid of pixel intensities, which is a matrix, and a matrix can be unrolled row by row into one long vector. A tiny 8-by-8 digit becomes a point in $\mathbb{R}^{64}$. Watch it happen:

Fig 1.2, image to matrix to vector. Press Unroll and the pixel grid flattens, row by row, into a single vector in $\mathbb{R}^{64}$. Digitization is itself an approximation: resolution and bit depth were choices.

Supervised learning, and the discipline of the split

This course's first setting is supervised learning: we are given pairs $(x, t)$ of an input and its label (also called the target), and the job is to learn to predict $t$ from $x$. The target's type names the problem: a continuous $t$ is regression, a discrete label $t \in \{1, \dots, C\}$ is classification, and structured objects (sequences, graphs) come later. Tom Mitchell's 1997 definition is worth carrying around: a program learns from experience $E$ with respect to task $T$ and performance measure $P$ if its performance at $T$, measured by $P$, improves with $E$.

One more idea and the preliminaries are done, and it is the most important one in the course: generalization, performing well on new, unseen data drawn from the same distribution as the training data. The data your model will face after deployment does not exist yet, so you simulate it. Hold some data back. Train on one set, evaluate on the other:

$$ D_{tr} = \{(x^{(i)}, t^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{N}, \qquad D_{ts} = \{(x^{(i)}, t^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{M} $$

Notation that recurs all term: superscripts in parentheses are example indices, not exponents, $x \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is the feature vector, and $t$ is its target. The test set $D_{ts}$ gets touched once, at the very end. Section 4 returns to this rule and makes it stricter.

2. Nearest neighbours (1-NN)

Now formalize the hook. To classify a new input $\hat{x}$: find the training input nearest to it, and copy that input's label. Done. The entire behaviour of this algorithm hinges on one word, nearest, so the real content of this section is the question: what does distance mean?

Euclidean distance

The default answer is the straight-line distance you know from geometry, generalized to $d$ dimensions:

$$ \operatorname{distance}(x^{(a)}, x^{(b)}) = \lVert x^{(a)} - x^{(b)} \rVert_2 = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^{d} \big( x^{(a)}_j - x^{(b)}_j \big)^2} $$

Subtract coordinate by coordinate, square, add, square-root. A worked example in $d = 2$:

$$ \operatorname{distance}\!\left([0,1]^\top, [2,1]^\top\right) = \sqrt{(2-0)^2 + (1-1)^2} = \sqrt{4} = 2 $$

And one in $d = 3$ so the pattern is clear: $\operatorname{distance}([1,2,3]^\top, [3,0,3]^\top) = \sqrt{4 + 4 + 0} = \sqrt{8} \approx 2.83$. The formula scales to any $d$, which is exactly why we bothered turning everything into vectors.

Distance is a choice

Euclidean is a default, not a law. Other measures answer "how far" with a different personality:

  • Manhattan ($\ell_1$): $\sum_j |a_j - b_j|$. Distance walked along a city grid, no diagonals.
  • Chebyshev ($\ell_\infty$): $\max_j |a_j - b_j|$. Only the single worst coordinate counts.
  • General $\ell_p$: $\big(\sum_j |a_j - b_j|^p\big)^{1/p}$, a dial that sweeps from Manhattan ($p=1$) through Euclidean ($p=2$) toward Chebyshev ($p \to \infty$).

The cleanest way to feel the difference is the unit ball: the set of all points at distance 1 from a centre. Euclidean gives a circle, Manhattan a diamond, Chebyshev a square. Different ball, different meaning of "near", different nearest neighbour:

Fig 2.1, the distance-metric explorer. Drag either point; all three distances update live, and the selected metric's ball is drawn around A, passing through B, so its shape shows what that metric calls equally near. The cosine readout shows the angle between the two points seen from the origin, a different question entirely.

That cosine readout deserves its own formula, because it is not a distance at all. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors and ignores their lengths:

$$ \operatorname{cossim}(a, b) = \cos(\theta) = \frac{\langle a, b \rangle}{\lVert a \rVert \, \lVert b \rVert} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i b_i} {\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{d} b_i^2}} $$

When does the angle matter more than the gap? Shopping baskets. Consider three users: User 1 buys 1 egg, 1 flour, 1 sugar. User 2 buys 100 eggs, 100 flour, 100 sugar. User 3 buys 1 egg, 1 vodka, 1 Red Bull. Who is most similar to User 1? By Euclidean distance it is User 3, the raw coordinate differences are tiny. By cosine similarity it is User 2, who points in exactly the same direction with a bigger magnitude: same taste, bigger kitchen. For purchase profiles, text, and anything where proportions are the signal, cosine is the right lens. Check the actual numbers:

Fig 2.2, the e-commerce example, computed. Toggle the measure and watch who counts as closest to User 1. Euclidean sees small gaps; cosine sees shared direction. The baker and the bulk baker agree on everything except quantity.

The 1-NN algorithm, and what it draws

With distance chosen, the algorithm is two lines of math:

$$ n = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{i \in \{1, \dots, N\}} \operatorname{distance}\!\left(x^{(i)}, \hat{x}\right), \qquad \hat{y} = t^{(n)} $$

Find the index of the nearest training example, output its label. But an algorithm that labels every possible input is secretly drawing a map of the whole input space. The boundary between regions assigned to different categories is a decision boundary, and each region on one side, mapping to a single class, is a decision region. For 1-NN the map has a beautiful structure: the space partitions into a Voronoi diagram. Each training example owns a cell, the set of all points closer to it than to any other example, and every query inside that cell inherits its label.

Fig 2.3, 1-NN's decision map is a Voronoi diagram. Drag the training points and the cells recompute live; drop the query anywhere to see whose cell it lands in. Then press "corrupt one label" and watch a single bad point poison its entire cell.

That last button is the pitfall that motivates everything in section 3. Real data contains noise, mislabeled points, "class noise". Under 1-NN, one mislabeled training example does not cause one mistake, it owns a whole Voronoi cell, and every future query in that region is answered wrongly with full confidence. The model has no way to notice, because it never second-guesses its single nearest neighbour. The fix is democratic: ask several neighbours and average out the noise.

3. k-nearest neighbours

Instead of trusting one neighbour, take the $k$ nearest training examples and let them vote. The majority label wins:

$$ \hat{y} = \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{c \in C} \sum_{i=1}^{k} \mathbb{I}\!\left(t^{(i)} = c\right) $$

Here $C$ is the set of class labels, the sum runs over the $k$ training examples closest to $\hat{x}$, and $\mathbb{I}\{\cdot\}$ is the indicator function: 1 when the statement inside is true, 0 otherwise. So the sum just counts how many of the $k$ neighbours carry label $c$, and arg max picks the biggest count.

Worked by hand: one vote, odd and even k

B (3.5, 0.5)  d = 0.71

B (4.0, 1.5)  d = 1.12
A (1.5, 2.0)  d = 1.80
A (1.0, 1.0)  d = 2.00
B (4.5, 2.5)  d = 2.12
A (1.0, 3.0)  d = 2.83

$k = 3$: neighbours are B, A, B. Tally: B gets 2, A gets 1. Predict B.

$k = 4$: neighbours are B, A, B, A. Tally: 2 vs 2, a dead tie with no principled winner. This is exactly why practitioners prefer odd $k$ for two-class problems.

$k = 5$: neighbours are B, A, B, A, A. Tally: A gets 3, B gets 2. Predict A. Same query, different $k$, opposite answer, which is the whole next discussion.

k is a hyperparameter, and it sets the model's personality

Nothing in the training data tells you what $k$ should be. You choose it before the algorithm runs, which makes it a hyperparameter. Contrast this with parameters, numbers learned from data during training. KNN is unusual: it has no learned parameters at all, only the hyperparameters $k$ and the distance metric. Choosing $k$ trades two failure modes against each other:

  • Small $k$ captures fine-grained patterns, but it is at the mercy of idiosyncrasies and outliers. The boundary goes jagged, wrapping around individual noisy points: overfitting.
  • Large $k$ averages over many examples, so predictions are stable, but real structure gets washed out along with the noise. The boundary over-smooths: underfitting.

The best $k$ depends on the dataset, its noise level, the distance metric, and the sample size. Theory offers asymptotic comfort: as $n \to \infty$, let $k \to \infty$ while $k/n \to 0$, and KNN behaves well. Practice offers a rule of thumb: try several values, often small and odd, and below $\sqrt{n}$. Now feel the trade-off yourself. This is the signature figure of the lesson:

Fig 3.1, the k-NN classifier playground. Drag the query, slide $k$, and watch the highlighted neighbours, the live vote tally, and the decision boundary. At $k = 1$ the boundary is jagged and owns every noisy point; crank $k$ and it melts smooth, eventually too smooth.

KNN for regression

Nothing about "ask the neighbours" requires a discrete label. If the targets are continuous numbers, replace the vote with an average, and KNN becomes a regression method. Three variants, in increasing sophistication:

  • $k = 1$: predict the single nearest neighbour's target. Across $x$, the prediction is a step function, it jumps wherever the nearest neighbour changes.
  • $k = 2$, unweighted: predict the plain average of the two nearest targets. Steps become plateaus, smoother but still blocky.
  • $k = 2$, distance-weighted: closer neighbours should count for more, so weight each neighbour by the inverse of its distance:
$$ \hat{y}(x) = \frac{\tfrac{1}{d_1}\, y_1 + \tfrac{1}{d_2}\, y_2} {\tfrac{1}{d_1} + \tfrac{1}{d_2}} $$

where $d_1, d_2$ are the distances from $x$ to its two nearest points. Worked with real numbers: neighbours at distances $d_1 = 1$ and $d_2 = 2$ with targets $y_1 = 4$ and $y_2 = 1$ give $\hat{y} = \frac{4/1 + 1/2}{1/1 + 1/2} = \frac{4.5}{1.5} = 3$, twice as close pulls twice as hard, so the prediction lands nearer 4 than 1.

Fig 3.2, k-NN regression. Slide $k$ and toggle distance weighting. At $k=1$ the predicted curve is a staircase; unweighted averaging flattens it into plateaus; inverse-distance weighting bends it into a smooth curve that leans toward whichever neighbour is closer.

Answer. $k=1$ is a step function: constant inside each nearest-neighbour cell, jumping at cell borders. $k=2$ unweighted is still piecewise constant, but each piece is the average of two targets, so the jumps are smaller. $k=2$ distance-weighted is the smoothest: as $x$ slides between two neighbours, the weights $1/d_1, 1/d_2$ change continuously, so the prediction morphs continuously from one target toward the other instead of jumping. KNN predicted a label for $\hat{x} = (3, 1)$ by a vote among the 3 nearest training points. A decision tree (next lecture) answers the same query by a different rule. Contrast the two prediction rules.

A decision tree's rule is a fixed sequence of single-feature threshold tests, learned once: "is $x_1 > 2.5$? then is $x_2 \le 1.8$? then predict B". The tree never measures distance and never revisits the training points at query time, it summarized them into rules. Consequences you can already predict: the tree's boundaries are axis-aligned rectangles while KNN's follow the data's geometry, and the tree answers in a handful of comparisons while KNN pays a full scan of the dataset (section 5 makes that cost precise).

4. Choosing k the right way

So $k$ matters, and the data cannot tell us directly. Here is the tempting shortcut, and why it is a trap: pick the $k$ with the lowest error on the training set. Under $k = 1$, every training point's nearest neighbour is itself, at distance zero. Training error: a perfect 0, always, regardless of how noisy the data is. By that yardstick, $k = 1$ "wins" every time, and you have learned nothing except that a model can memorize. Training error does not measure generalization, and generalization is the thing we actually want.

The honest procedure needs a third dataset. Split into three:

  • $D_{tr}$ (training): fit the model. For KNN that just means store the data.
  • $D_{vl}$ (validation): make design decisions, like choosing $k$. Try several values, keep whichever performs best here.
  • $D_{ts}$ (test): touched exactly once, at the very end, to report final generalization. The proverb to memorize: the test set is not a development set. If you use it to make choices, its verdict on your final model is no longer honest.

The whole procedure in one picture. Below, a real dataset was split three ways, and the error of k-NN was computed for every $k$ on all three splits. Before revealing the answer, read the curves and click the $k$ you would ship:

Fig 4.1, the validation curve, with you in the loop. Training error is a flattering lie (zero at $k=1$). Validation error dips at the sweet spot. Click your pick of $k$ on the chart; the figure checks it against the computed optimum and only then reveals the test curve.

The recipe, compressed: train on $D_{tr}$, sweep $k$, pick the best on $D_{vl}$, report once on $D_{ts}$. In code:

best_k, best_acc = None, 0.0
for k in [1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 15, 21]:
    acc = accuracy(knn_predict(X_tr, y_tr, X_vl, k), y_vl)
    if acc > best_acc:
        best_k, best_acc = k, acc
final_report = accuracy(knn_predict(X_tr, y_tr, X_ts, best_k), y_ts)  # once.

5. Where KNN breaks

KNN's bet is that points close in input space have similar outputs. Three things attack that bet in practice: high dimensions make "close" meaningless, arbitrary feature scales make it lopsided, and the no-training-cost lunch gets paid for, with interest, at query time.

Pitfall 1: the curse of dimensionality

In high dimensions, geometry stops behaving like your 2-D intuition. The short version: as dimension $d$ grows, most pairs of points become roughly equidistant, so "nearest" stops carrying information, and distances get dominated by many possibly irrelevant coordinates.

Why equidistance happens, in two steps

Step 1: one dimension. Take two independent uniform draws $X, Y \sim \mathrm{Uniform}[0,1]$. The squared gap $Z = (X-Y)^2$ is a random variable with mean $\mathbb{E}[Z] = \tfrac{1}{6}$ and some fixed variance.

Step 2: add dimensions. In $\mathbb{R}^d$ the squared Euclidean distance is a sum of $d$ independent copies, $R = \sum_{i=1}^d Z_i$, so $\mathbb{E}[R] = d\,\mathbb{E}[Z]$ while $\operatorname{Var}[R] = d\,\operatorname{Var}[Z]$. The mean grows like $d$, the spread grows like $\sqrt{d}$, so the relative spread shrinks like $1/\sqrt{d}$. Every pairwise distance crowds toward the same value, and Markov's inequality makes it formal: the probability that $R$ sits far from its mean vanishes as $d \to \infty$.

The edge-length view. To capture $k$ of $n$ points inside a sub-cube of edge $l$ in the unit $d$-cube, you need $l^d \approx k/n$, so $l = (k/n)^{1/d}$. With $k=10$, $n=1000$: $l = 0.01$ in $d=1$, but $l \approx 0.63$ in $d=10$ and $l \approx 0.95$ in $d=100$. Your "local" neighbourhood spans nearly the whole range of every feature. Locality is gone.

Fig 5.1, the curse, sampled live. Push the $d$ slider: the histogram of pairwise distances (real samples, recomputed) collapses toward a spike, the contrast ratio (max−min)/min falls toward zero, and the edge-length readout $l = (k/n)^{1/d}$ rushes toward 1.

The fix, previewed: reduce dimensions. High-dimensional data usually has low intrinsic dimensionality (section 1's degrees of variation), and dimensionality-reduction methods later in the course project onto it.

Check: explain equidistance to a friend in three sentences

Answer (one honest version). Each added dimension contributes an independent random amount to the squared distance between any two points, so the total grows in proportion to $d$. But random fluctuations only accumulate like $\sqrt{d}$, so as a fraction of the total, the differences between pairs shrink toward nothing. When every pair of points is the same distance apart to within a hair, "the nearest one" is decided by noise, and copying its label is a coin flip. (Equivalent edge-length version: a neighbourhood holding 1% of the data needs edge $(0.01)^{1/d}$, which is nearly 1 for large $d$, so "local" spans the whole cube.)

Pitfall 2: feature scale decides the vote

Euclidean distance adds squared differences as if all features spoke the same units. They do not. Put salary in dollars (spread: tens of thousands) next to age in years (spread: tens), and the salary axis contributes millions of times more to every squared distance. The neighbour search effectively ignores age, not because age is uninformative, but because of an arbitrary choice of units. The standard fix is to standardize every feature to zero mean and unit variance:

$$ \tilde{x}_j = \frac{x_j - \mu_j}{\sigma_j} $$

using each feature's mean $\mu_j$ and standard deviation $\sigma_j$. Worked once: ages $[20, 30, 40, 50]$ have $\mu = 35$ and $\sigma \approx 11.18$, so age 50 standardizes to $\tilde{x} = (50 - 35)/11.18 \approx 1.34$: "1.34 standard deviations above average", a unit every feature shares. Two cautions before you standardize on reflex: for some problems scale is genuinely meaningful and you would be erasing signal, and when scale matters, the choice of distance measure matters right along with it.

Fig 5.2, the standardization demo. Left: raw units, where the large-scale feature owns the neighbour choice. Right: the same data standardized, where the axes equalize and the neighbour set changes. The readout counts how many neighbours survive the switch, and prints the real $\mu, \sigma$ used in $\tilde{x} = (x-\mu)/\sigma$.

X_std = (X - X.mean(axis=0)) / X.std(axis=0)   # per-feature standardization

Pitfall 3: the bill arrives at query time

KNN is lazy learning: training costs nothing, because training is storing the dataset. That also makes it a non-parametric model, it never summarizes the data into a fixed set of parameters; the data itself is the model. The price appears when a query shows up. Count the work for one prediction:

  • One $D$-dimensional distance: $O(D)$.
  • Distances to all $N$ training points: $O(ND)$.
  • Sorting to find the closest $k$: $O(N \log N)$.
  • Total, per query: $O(ND + N \log N)$. And the entire dataset must sit in memory forever.

Fig 5.3, lazy learning's bill. Press Run and one query animates against the stored dataset: every distance computation ticks the counter ($O(ND)$), then the sort ($O(N\log N)$). Slide $N$ up and watch a single prediction get expensive.

Scale makes it visceral. KNN performs respectably on digit images given good preprocessing (warping for invariance) and a sensible similarity measure. But on "80 Million Tiny Images", an early very-large image dataset of 32-by-32 colour images, a single naive nearest-neighbour search took on the order of tens of minutes from disk. (The dataset was later deprecated by its authors; it survives here only as a historical scale example.) A model that takes minutes per prediction is not a product. The escape hatch is a rich research area: data structures for fast, approximate nearest neighbours, trading a little accuracy for orders of magnitude of speed. You will meet the trade-off again in the end quiz.

6. Summary and method card

KNN does all of its work at test time; in a sense there is no learning at all, which is why it is called lazy. It is non-parametric, no parameters are learned, yet its complexity is genuinely controlled by a hyperparameter, $k$, chosen by you, on validation data, never on training error. Its honest weaknesses are the curse of dimensionality, its sensitivity to feature scaling, and a heavy memory and runtime bill at query time. And its core bet, similar inputs have similar labels, is the quiet assumption underneath every model in this course; the rest of the term is a sequence of increasingly sophisticated ways of making that bet pay in situations where raw memorization cannot.

Method card · k-Nearest Neighbours

Same bet, better and better machinery.

Right. The gap between perfect training and mediocre validation accuracy is overfitting, and in KNN the smoothing knob is $k$. Sweep it and pick on validation, as in Fig 4.1.
Quiz 2

You add 500 useless random features to a dataset where KNN was working well on 5 informative ones. What happens and why?

Exactly, this is the curse of dimensionality applied. Every pairwise distance gains 500 independent noise contributions; the informative 5 dimensions become a rounding error in the metric. Fig 5.1's collapsing histogram is this picture.
KNN has no mechanism to weight or discard features; the metric mixes everything equally. That is precisely its fragility.
Only informative features add signal; these add variance to every distance and drown the signal that was there.
Quiz 3 (transfer): design a distance for something that is not a vector

Propose a distance or similarity measure for one non-vector data type (text documents, sets of tags, or categorical records) and justify why it captures "similar" for that data.

Answer (one good version per type). Sets of tags: Jaccard similarity, $|A \cap B| / |A \cup B|$: it rewards shared tags, normalizes by total tags so prolific taggers are not automatically "close" to everyone, and lives in $[0,1]$. Text: represent documents as word-count vectors and use cosine similarity: direction captures topic proportions while ignoring document length, exactly the magnitude-vs-direction lesson of Fig 2.2. Categorical records: Hamming distance, count the attributes on which two records disagree, optionally weighting rare agreements more heavily than common ones. In every case the justification has the same shape: name what "similar" should mean for the data, then check the measure is large/small exactly then, and check it is insensitive to the nuisances (length, popularity, encoding) you do not want to matter.

Quiz 4 (transfer): buy speed with accuracy

Per-query KNN costs $O(ND + N \log N)$. Propose a data structure or approximate scheme that trades accuracy for speed, and reason about the trade-off.

Answer (one good version). Cluster the training set offline into $m$ groups (say with $m \approx \sqrt{N}$ centroids). At query time, compute distances only to the $m$ centroids, pick the closest cluster or two, and search only inside them: roughly $O(mD + \frac{N}{m}D)$ per query, a huge cut from $O(ND)$. The price is correctness at cluster borders: the true nearest neighbour can sit just across a boundary you chose not to search, so the returned neighbours are approximate. You tune the trade with how many clusters you probe. Alternatives with the same flavour: k-d trees (exact in low $d$, degrade in high $d$), locality-sensitive hashing (probabilistic, tunable). The pattern to remember: precompute structure to avoid touching all $N$ points, accept a small chance of a slightly wrong neighbour.

Quiz 5 (transfer): KNN has no training step. Invent one.

Decide what you would precompute at "training time" to cut query cost, and name what it costs you.

Answer (one good version). Spend training time on three precomputations. (1) Standardize: compute and store each feature's $\mu_j, \sigma_j$ so queries are scaled consistently, cost: almost nothing, no downside. (2) Prototype selection: keep only points near class boundaries (points whose removal changes some prediction) and drop interior points whose cells agree with all their neighbours, cost: storage drops a lot, but you bias the boundary and lose robustness to distribution shift. (3) Build a search index (k-d tree, clusters, or hash tables) over the stored points, cost: build time and memory, plus approximate answers in high dimensions. Notice what just happened: the moment KNN precomputes summaries instead of keeping raw data, it starts inching toward the parametric models of the next lectures. That is the course's actual trajectory.

That is k-nearest neighbours: one honest bet, similar things have similar labels, plus everything the bet forces you to invent: vector representations, a chosen distance, decision boundaries, a hyperparameter, and the train/validation/test discipline.
