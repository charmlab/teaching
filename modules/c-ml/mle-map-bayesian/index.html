MLE, MAP & Bayesian Prediction

Flip a coin 100 times and count 55 heads. What is the coin's probability of heads? Every instinct says 0.55, and every instinct is doing maximum likelihood without knowing it. But a second question hides inside the first: how sure are you? A single number cannot answer it. This lesson opens the probabilistic block of the course by building the machinery that can: likelihoods, priors, posteriors, and prediction. We start from one best-guess number, add a prior to get a better-behaved number, and end by refusing to pick a number at all, keeping the entire distribution over parameters and averaging predictions over everything the data has not ruled out. That last move is Bayesian prediction, and it is what saves your model when the data is thin.

0. The hook: one coin, two questions

The transformer capstone closed the neural arc of the course. This lecture changes register entirely: no architectures, no gradients on parade, just a coin and a question. The question sounds trivial and is not. You flip a coin 100 times and see 55 heads. Let $\theta$ be the probability the coin lands heads. What is $\theta$?

Say 0.55 and you are in good company. But now answer the second question: how confident are you? Would you bet at 0.55 after 100 flips? After 10 flips with 5.5 heads' worth of evidence? Clearly the two situations feel different, yet the single number 0.55 is identical in both. Something is missing from the answer, and the missing thing is the subject of this lecture. The big idea: treat the data $D$ as generated by a probabilistic model with unknown parameters $\theta$, learn $\theta$ from $D$, then predict future observations. We will do that three ways, in increasing order of honesty about uncertainty:

  • Maximum likelihood (MLE): pick the single $\theta$ that best fits the data. One point, no uncertainty.
  • Maximum a posteriori (MAP): pick the single most probable $\theta$ given the data and a prior belief. Still one point, but a better-behaved one.
  • Fully Bayesian: refuse to pick. Keep the whole distribution over $\theta$ and average predictions over it.

Here is the destination before any derivation. The curve below is a genuine posterior distribution over $\theta$ for the 55-heads data, computed live. The three answers above are three different ways of reading the same curve:

Fig 0.1, where this lesson ends up: the full posterior belief over $\theta$ after 55 heads in 100 flips, with the three estimators marked. MLE reads off the likelihood peak, MAP reads off the posterior mode, and the Bayesian predictive reads off the posterior mean. Press the button to flip 100 more tosses from a hidden true coin and watch the curve narrow: more data means less uncertainty, and the three answers squeeze together. Every curve and number is computed, not drawn.

Notice what the distribution gives you that 0.55 alone never could: width. After 100 flips the curve is already fairly tight; after 500 it is a spike. The width is the answer to "how sure are you?". The rest of the lesson builds this picture from first principles, and the first brick is a distinction most people have never been forced to make.

1. Probability vs likelihood

One formula, two readings. For a coin flipped $n$ times, the Binomial distribution gives the chance of seeing exactly $k$ heads:

$$ p(D = \{n, k\} \mid \theta) = \binom{n}{k}\, \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n-k}. $$

Everything depends on which symbol you treat as the variable and which you hold fixed.

Probability fixes the parameter and varies the data. Set $\theta = 0.5$ and ask: what is the chance of 55 heads under a fair coin? That is $p(D \mid \theta = 0.5)$, a number you get by plugging into the formula, and summing it over all possible outcomes $k = 0, \dots, n$ gives exactly 1, as a distribution over data must.

Likelihood fixes the data and varies the parameter. The flips already happened: $N_H$ heads and $N_T$ tails are frozen facts. Now read the same formula as a function of $\theta$:

$$ L(\theta; D) = p(D \mid \theta), $$

a curve over $[0, 1]$ that scores every candidate coin by how well it explains what you saw. Same symbols, opposite roles. And one warning before the figure: a likelihood is not a probability distribution over $\theta$. Nothing forces its area to be 1, and in general it is not. It is a scoring function, full stop. Turning it into an honest distribution over $\theta$ is precisely what Bayes' rule will do for us in section 4.

Fig 1.1, one Binomial formula, two readings. In the probability view, $\theta$ is fixed by the slider and the bars show $p(k \text{ heads} \mid \theta)$ over all outcomes; the bars sum to 1 (printed, not asserted). In the likelihood view, the data $(N_H, N_T)$ is fixed by the inputs and the curve shows $L(\theta; D)$ over every candidate $\theta$; its area is not 1 and the readout says so. Toggle between them until the switch feels mechanical: same formula, different variable held fixed.

Likelihood values are probabilities of data, so they sit in $[0,1]$ here; nothing about the arithmetic is wrong. The error is in the interpretation, not the number.

2. Maximum likelihood estimation

The principle, and the log trick

If the likelihood scores every candidate $\theta$ by how well it explains the data, an obvious learning rule follows: a good $\theta$ is one that assigns high probability to the $D$ we actually observed, so pick the maximizer. That rule is maximum likelihood estimation. For $N = N_H + N_T$ independent, identically distributed coin tosses:

$$ L(\theta; D) = \binom{N}{N_H} \theta^{N_H}(1 - \theta)^{N_T} \;\propto\; \theta^{N_H}(1 - \theta)^{N_T}. $$

The binomial coefficient does not involve $\theta$, so the maximizer ignores it, which is why the proportionality is all we keep. In practice nobody maximizes this product directly. We maximize its logarithm, the log-likelihood, for two reasons that will follow you through the rest of ML: the log pairs beautifully with the exponentials that live inside most distributions, and it turns products over data points into sums, which are kinder to both calculus and floating point. Since the log is strictly increasing, the maximizer does not move:

$$ \ell(\theta) = N_H \log\theta + N_T \log(1 - \theta). $$

Try it numerically before any calculus. For $N_H = 55, N_T = 45$, evaluate $\ell$ at a few candidates:

Worked by hand: scanning the log-likelihood at N_H = 55, N_T = 45

ℓ(0.40) = 55 log 0.40 + 45 log 0.60 = −73.38
ℓ(0.50) = 55 log 0.50 + 45 log 0.50 = −69.31
ℓ(0.55) = 55 log 0.55 + 45 log 0.45 = −68.81
ℓ(0.60) = 55 log 0.60 + 45 log 0.40 = −69.33
ℓ(0.70) = 55 log 0.70 + 45 log 0.30 = −73.80

The values are negative (logs of numbers below 1 always are), and bigger is better. The scan peaks at 0.55, exactly the intuitive answer, and the curve falls away on both sides. Drag the same numbers around in the figure below.

The scan suggests the peak; calculus confirms it. Set the derivative of $\ell$ to zero:

$$ \frac{d\ell}{d\theta} = \frac{N_H}{\theta} - \frac{N_T}{1 - \theta} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \hat{\theta}_{ML} = \frac{N_H}{N_H + N_T}. $$

The maximum likelihood estimate is the empirical fraction of heads. Your instinct in the hook was a theorem all along. Feel the geometry of it here: the peak of the curve is not a decoration, it is where the derivative crosses zero:

Fig 2.1, the likelihood maximizer. Drag the black $\theta$ handle along the log-likelihood curve $\ell(\theta) = N_H\log\theta + N_T\log(1-\theta)$ (arrow keys work too) and watch $\ell(\theta)$ update; the grey marker sits at the analytic peak $N_H/(N_H+N_T)$. Edit $N_H$ and $N_T$: more flips at the same ratio keep the peak still but sharpen the curve around it, which is the "how sure are you?" question becoming visible. The code

Both the scan and the closed form are a few honest lines: 

import numpy as np

def log_likelihood(theta, nh, nt):
    return nh * np.log(theta) + nt * np.log(1 - theta)

def mle_bernoulli(nh, nt):
    return nh / (nh + nt)

thetas = np.array([0.40, 0.50, 0.55, 0.60, 0.70])
print(log_likelihood(thetas, 55, 45))
# [-73.38 -69.31 -68.81 -69.33 -73.80]
print(mle_bernoulli(55, 45))   # 0.55

Gaussian MLE: the same recipe on real-valued data

Coins are Bernoulli; measurements are Gaussian. Suppose we record daily high temperatures in Waterloo for ten May days and want a model of "a typical May day". The Gaussian family

$$ \mathcal{N}(x; \mu, \sigma) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) $$

has two parameters, and maximum likelihood handles them with the identical recipe: write the log-likelihood of the i.i.d. data, take derivatives with respect to $\mu$ and $\sigma$, set them to zero. The exponential melts under the log (this is the pairing the log trick promised) and the closed forms drop out:

$$ \hat{\mu}_{ML} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x^{(i)}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}_{ML} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \big(x^{(i)} - \hat{\mu}_{ML}\big)^2}. $$

The best-fitting Gaussian has the sample mean as its centre and the sample standard deviation as its width. Two formulas you have used your whole life turn out to be maximum likelihood estimates.

Worked by hand: the May temperatures

Data (°C): 11, 14, 17, 13, 18, 16, 12, 15, 19, 15.

μ̂ = (11+14+17+13+18+16+12+15+19+15) / 10 = 150 / 10 = 15.0
deviations² = 16, 1, 4, 4, 9, 1, 9, 0, 16, 0  →  sum = 60
σ̂ = √(60 / 10) = √6 ≈ 2.449

So the MLE model of a May day is $\mathcal{N}(x;\, 15,\, 2.449)$. The figure below lets you fight the formula: drag the bell away from these values and watch the log-likelihood drop.

The general recipe, and when calculus fails

Step back and see the pattern, because it is the whole method. One: assume a model family $p(D \mid \theta)$. Two: write the log-likelihood $\ell(\theta; D)$ of the observed data. Three: optimize, analytically if you can, numerically if you cannot. Bernoulli and Gaussian are the friendly cases with closed forms. Other families are not so kind: the Gamma distribution, whose density involves the gamma function $\Gamma(\cdot)$, has no closed-form maximizer, and setting derivatives to zero leaves you with equations no amount of algebra untangles. The cure is the tool you already own: gradient ascent on the log-likelihood, the mirror image of the gradient descent from the optimization lectures. Ascent maximizes an objective, descent minimizes a loss; same machinery, opposite sign. Nothing about probabilistic modeling exempts it from numerical optimization, and nothing about numerical optimization needs to change.

Check your understanding

Now generalize the coin to a die. A six-sided die follows a Categorical distribution with parameters $\theta_1, \dots, \theta_6$ summing to 1, and the MLE (derived the same way, with a constraint) is $\hat\theta_k^{ML} = n_k / N$, the fraction of rolls landing on face $k$. You roll three times and see $D = \{2, 4, 4\}$. What is the MLE?

MLE has no concept of "cannot overturn": it maximizes fit to the observed data only, and the observed data contains no ones, threes, fives, or sixes. Fairness would be a prior belief, and MLE carries no prior.
There is no remainder to distribute: $1/3 + 2/3 = 1$ already. The unseen faces get exactly zero, and that "exactly" is about to become a serious problem.
Right. $n_2 = 1, n_4 = 2, N = 3$ gives $1/3$ and $2/3$, and every unseen face gets probability exactly 0. The model now claims rolling a six is impossible. Hold that thought for section 3.
Check: why is maximizing the log the same as maximizing the likelihood?

We swapped $L(\theta)$ for $\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta)$ without ceremony. Why is that legal, and why is it smart?

Answer. Legal: the log is strictly increasing, so $L(\theta_1) > L(\theta_2)$ exactly when $\log L(\theta_1) > \log L(\theta_2)$. Order is preserved, so the argmax is preserved; the peak's height changes but its location cannot. Smart, twice over. Analytically, i.i.d. likelihoods are products of one term per data point, and the derivative of a 100-factor product is a nightmare, while the derivative of a 100-term sum is a sum of derivatives; the log converts one into the other, and it also unwraps the exponentials inside Gaussians and friends. Numerically, a product of 100 probabilities each around 0.5 is roughly $10^{-30}$, small enough to underflow toward zero in floating point, while its log is a comfortable $-69$. Every serious implementation works in log space for both reasons.

3. From estimation to prediction

Coins in a pouch

Estimation is rarely the end goal. We estimate parameters so we can predict, and prediction is where the running example of this lecture lives. A pouch holds four types of coins. For each type $k$ we have historical flip data $D_k = (N_{H,k}, N_{T,k})$, and we know how common each type is in the pouch: the type frequencies $\pi_k = p(C = k)$, which are priors over the type of a drawn coin. Now someone draws a coin without showing us which type it is, flips it ten times, and reports $D_{\text{new}} = (9, 1)$: nine heads, one tail. Which type is it?

This is a Bayes' rule question. We want the type with the highest probability given the new evidence:

$$ \hat{C}_{\text{new}} = \arg\max_k \; p(C = k \mid D_{\text{new}}) = \arg\max_k \; p(D_{\text{new}} \mid C = k)\; p(C = k), $$

where the denominator of Bayes' rule, the evidence $p(D_{\text{new}})$, is the same constant for every $k$, so the argmax ignores it. Each candidate type therefore gets a score with two factors: how common the type is, and how well it explains the new flips. The catch is that $p(D_{\text{new}} \mid C = k)$ depends on the type's unknown bias $\theta_k$. The simplest fix is the plug-in move: estimate $\hat\theta_k^{ML}$ from the historical data and plug it in as if it were the truth:

$$ s_k = \underbrace{p(C = k)}_{\text{type prior}} \cdot \underbrace{p(D_{\text{new}} \mid \hat{\theta}_k^{ML},\, C = k)}_{\text{fit to the new flips}}, \qquad \hat{C}_{\text{new}} = \arg\max_k s_k. $$
Worked by hand: one plug-in score

Take coin 2, with history $(N_H, N_T) = (80, 20)$ and frequency $\pi_2 = 0.20$. Its MLE bias is $\hat\theta_2 = 80/100 = 0.8$, and the new data is 9 heads in 10 flips:

p(D_new | θ̂₂) = C(10,9) · 0.8⁹ · 0.2¹ = 10 · 0.1342 · 0.2 = 0.2684
s₂ = π₂ · 0.2684 = 0.20 · 0.2684 = 0.0537

Do the same for all four types and compare. Coin 3, a rare type whose short history (9 heads, 1 tail) gives it MLE bias 0.9, scores $0.15 \times 0.3874 = 0.0581$ and wins the argmax: the MLE plug-in picks coin 3. Keep an eye on that thin 10-flip history; it is doing a lot of unearned talking.

The zero-frequency problem

The pouch hints that thin data misleads MLE. Here is the failure in its purest form. Flip a coin twice, see heads both times, and estimate:

$$ \hat{\theta}_{ML} = \frac{2}{2} = 1. $$

The model now asserts $p(T) = 0$: tails is not unlikely, it is impossible. Every future computation that multiplies by $p(T)$ gets zeroed out by two coin flips' worth of evidence. This is the zero-frequency problem (the die quiz in section 2 was the same disease: unseen faces got exact zeros), and it is not a corner case. In language modeling, most words never appear in a small corpus; in the pouch, a short history is one lucky streak away from an absolute claim. MLE has no mechanism to say "I have only seen two flips, let me hedge". The mechanism it lacks is a prior, and priors are the next section:

Fig 3.2, the zero-frequency trap, live. Flip the virtual coin (its hidden true bias is 0.7, so heads streaks are common) and watch $\hat\theta_{ML}$ and the estimated $p(T) = 1 - \hat\theta$ after every flip. While the record is all heads, MLE pins $p(T)$ to exactly 0. Switch on the Beta(2,2) prior and the estimate steps back from the cliff: $p(T)$ stays positive on the same data, and the two estimates converge as flips accumulate.

4. Priors, posteriors, and MAP

Beliefs before and after data

The zero-frequency failures share a root cause: MLE listens only to the data, and thin data speaks with unearned confidence. The Bayesian repair is to make the missing ingredient explicit. A prior $p(\theta)$ is your belief about the parameter before seeing data; combine it with the likelihood through Bayes' rule and you get the posterior $p(\theta \mid D)$, your belief after. The point estimate is replaced by a distribution that starts at the prior and gets reshaped by evidence.

What should a prior over a coin's bias look like? A uniform prior on $[0, 1]$ says "no idea", which is honest but inflexible: you cannot express "probably near fair" or "heads-heavy coins are common". The standard choice is the Beta distribution:

$$ p(\theta; a, b) = \frac{\Gamma(a + b)}{\Gamma(a)\,\Gamma(b)}\, \theta^{a-1}(1 - \theta)^{b-1}, \qquad \mathrm{Beta}(1,1) = \mathcal{U}[0,1]. $$

The two shape parameters $a, b$ act like phantom flips: $\mathrm{Beta}(2,2)$ gently favours fairness, $\mathrm{Beta}(5,1)$ leans heads-heavy, and the distribution grows more peaked as $a, b$ grow, the shape of increasing conviction. $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ recovers the uniform, so the flexible choice contains the ignorant one as a special case.

Conjugacy: the algebra does the work

Why Beta specifically? Look at its functional form next to the Bernoulli likelihood: $\theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}$ against $\theta^{N_H}(1-\theta)^{N_T}$. Same shape, powers of $\theta$ and $1-\theta$. A prior whose form matches the likelihood this way is called a conjugate prior, and conjugacy means the posterior stays in the family. Multiply and watch the exponents simply add:

$$ p(\theta \mid D) \;\propto\; p(\theta; a, b)\, p(D \mid \theta) = \theta^{N_H + a - 1}(1 - \theta)^{N_T + b - 1} \;\Rightarrow\; \theta \mid D \sim \mathrm{Beta}(N_H + a,\; N_T + b). $$

No integral was harmed. The posterior is another Beta whose parameters are "prior pseudo-counts plus real counts", which makes the phantom-flips reading exact: a $\mathrm{Beta}(a, b)$ prior behaves precisely as if you had already seen $a - 1$ heads and $b - 1$ tails before the experiment began. Updating on data is bookkeeping. See the algebra as shapes:

Fig 4.1, conjugate means the posterior stays in the family. The palest curve is the Beta prior, the mid-grey curve is the Bernoulli likelihood (normalized for display so the shapes are comparable), and the black curve is their product, renormalized: the posterior. It is exactly $\mathrm{Beta}(N_H + a,\, N_T + b)$, with parameters printed live. Slide the prior and the counts and watch a Beta times a Bernoulli remain a Beta, every time.

And the balance of power is exactly what you would hope. With little data, the prior's pseudo-counts dominate and the posterior hugs the prior. With a lot of data, $N_H$ and $N_T$ swamp $a$ and $b$, the likelihood dominates, and the posterior converges to a spike at the MLE. Priors are training wheels: load-bearing at the start, irrelevant at scale.

MAP: the most probable parameter

Once you have a posterior, the least imaginative way to use it is to take its peak. Maximum a posteriori estimation picks the parameter that maximizes the posterior, and taking logs splits the objective into two named parts:

$$ \hat{\theta}_{MAP} = \arg\max_\theta\, p(\theta \mid D) = \arg\max_\theta \big[\log p(\theta) + \log p(D \mid \theta)\big]. $$

Read it as MLE plus a regularizer: the log-likelihood pulls toward the data, the log-prior pulls toward prior belief, and the argmax balances the two. You have met this structure before. In the logistic-regression lecture we showed that L2 regularization is exactly a Gaussian prior on the weights wearing a loss function's clothes, and the regularized objective is MAP estimation. That throwaway remark is this section, now with the full machinery under it. For the coin with a Beta prior, the posterior is a Beta, and the mode of a Beta has a closed form:

$$ \hat{\theta}_{MAP} = \frac{N_H + a - 1}{N_H + N_T + a + b - 2}. $$
Worked by hand: MAP vs MLE, small data and large

Use the fairness-leaning prior $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 2)$ throughout.

Two flips, both heads $(N_H = 2, N_T = 0)$:

θ̂_ML  = 2/2 = 1    (tails declared impossible)
θ̂_MAP = (2 + 2 − 1) / (2 + 0 + 2 + 2 − 2) = 3/4 = 0.75

A hundred flips $(N_H = 55, N_T = 45)$:

θ̂_ML  = 55/100 = 0.55
θ̂_MAP = (55 + 2 − 1) / (100 + 2 + 2 − 2) = 56/102 ≈ 0.549

Small data: the prior hauls a reckless estimate from 1.0 back to 0.75. Large data: the prior nudges the third decimal place and otherwise stays out of the way. That is the behaviour you want from a regularizer, and here it is emerging from probability theory rather than being bolted on.

Two properties earn MAP its keep. First, with $a, b > 1$ the estimate can never reach 0 or 1, so unseen events keep nonzero probability and the zero-frequency trap cannot spring, which you saw directly in Fig 3.2. Second, as $N$ grows the counts dominate the pseudo-counts and MAP converges to MLE, so you pay for the safety only when data is scarce. And it changes real decisions: rerun the pouch with MAP plug-in estimates $\hat\theta_k^{MAP}$ in place of the MLEs, and the winner flips from coin 3 to coin 2. Coin 3's seductive 0.9 was a 10-flip mirage; the prior deflates it to $10/12 \approx 0.833$, while coin 2's 100-flip history barely moves ($81/102 \approx 0.794$). Flip the toggle in Fig 3.1 and watch the argmax change hands. 

def map_bernoulli(nh, nt, a, b):
    return (nh + a - 1) / (nh + nt + a + b - 2)

print(map_bernoulli(2, 0, 2, 2))     # 0.75    (MLE said 1.0)
print(map_bernoulli(55, 45, 2, 2))   # 0.5490  (MLE said 0.55)
Check your understanding

Back to the die with $D = \{2, 4, 4\}$. The multi-outcome conjugate of the Beta is the Dirichlet: a symmetric Dirichlet prior with $\alpha_k = 2$ for all six faces gives posterior $\mathrm{Dir}(\alpha_1 + n_1, \dots, \alpha_6 + n_6)$ and MAP $\hat\theta_k^{MAP} = \frac{\alpha_k + n_k - 1}{\sum_j (\alpha_j + n_j) - K}$. Compute it.

Right. The denominator is $\sum_j(\alpha_j + n_j) - K = (12 + 3) - 6 = 9$, and the numerators $\alpha_k + n_k - 1$ are 3 for face 4, 2 for face 2, and 1 for each unseen face. Check: $(3 + 2 + 1 \cdot 4)/9 = 1$. Every unseen face keeps probability $1/9$: no more impossible sixes.
That is the MLE again. The Dirichlet pseudo-counts do not cancel: each face starts with $\alpha_k - 1 = 1$ phantom observations, which is precisely what keeps the unseen faces alive.
The prior tempers the data, it does not erase it. Face 4 was rolled twice in three tries and its MAP estimate $3/9$ duly exceeds the uniform $1/6$. With $N = 3$ the prior is influential, not dictatorial.
Check: what exactly do a and b buy over the uniform prior?

The uniform prior is $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$, so the Beta family contains it. What do other settings of $a, b$ express, and what happens to MAP at $a = b = 1$?

Answer. The pair $(a, b)$ encodes both a location and a strength. Location: the prior leans toward $\theta$ values near $a/(a+b)$, so $\mathrm{Beta}(5,1)$ expects a heads-heavy coin and $\mathrm{Beta}(2,2)$ expects rough fairness. Strength: the total $a + b$ plays the role of a pseudo-sample size, and larger totals make the prior harder for data to move; $\mathrm{Beta}(20,20)$ and $\mathrm{Beta}(2,2)$ share a centre but not a stubbornness, which is why Beta grows more peaked as $a, b$ grow. At $a = b = 1$ the MAP formula collapses to $N_H / (N_H + N_T)$: a uniform prior contributes zero pseudo-counts, so MAP under it is exactly MLE. The zero-frequency protection specifically needs $a, b > 1$, phantom evidence for both outcomes.

5. Fully Bayesian prediction

Stop throwing the posterior away

Step back and notice what MAP did with the beautiful object we built. We constructed an entire posterior distribution, a full accounting of which parameters are plausible and how much, and then summarized it by one point and discarded the rest. MLE and MAP differ in which point they keep, but they share the deeper decision to keep only one. The fully Bayesian approach declines. Keep the whole thing,

$$ p(\theta \mid D) = \frac{L(\theta; D)\, p(\theta)}{p(D)}, $$

and when asked to predict, average the prediction over every $\theta$, weighted by how much the posterior believes in it. The result is the posterior predictive distribution: the distribution of new data with the parameter uncertainty integrated out rather than ignored. For a single future toss of our coin, the integral comes out in closed form (conjugacy again), and it is worth staring at:

$$ p(x_{\text{new}} = H \mid D) = \int_0^1 \theta \; p(\theta \mid D)\, d\theta = \frac{N_H + a}{N_H + N_T + a + b}. $$

That is the mean of the posterior $\mathrm{Beta}(N_H + a, N_T + b)$, not its mode. The best single-toss prediction and the most probable parameter are different numbers, because averaging over an asymmetric distribution does not land on its peak. For $N_H = 2, N_T = 0$ with the $\mathrm{Beta}(2,2)$ prior, the predictive is $4/6 \approx 0.667$ while MAP says 0.75 and MLE says 1.0: three estimators, three answers, spread exactly in order of how much uncertainty each one respects. For $N_H = 55, N_T = 45$ the three collapse to 0.548, 0.549, and 0.55: with real data behind you, the disagreement evaporates.

Set the counts and the prior; the panel computes $\hat\theta_{ML}$, $\hat\theta_{MAP}$, and the posterior predictive $p(x_{\text{new}} = H \mid D)$, plots all three on the posterior, and tracks them as $N$ grows at a fixed heads ratio. Small $N$: the three disagree, and the gaps are the uncertainty MLE pretends not to have. Large $N$: the posterior sharpens and the three estimators converge to the same value.

Predicting a whole dataset, and the pouch's final verdict

For one toss the predictive was a tidy ratio. For several future tosses the same integral produces ratios of the Beta function $B(\cdot, \cdot)$ (the normalizer from the Beta density). In the pouch, give each coin type its own posterior from history $D_k$ under a $\theta_k \sim \mathrm{Beta}(2,2)$ prior; the probability that type $k$ would produce the new data $(9, 1)$ is then

$$ p(D_{\text{new}} \mid D_k, C = k) = \binom{10}{9}\frac{B(2 + N_{H,k} + 9,\; 2 + N_{T,k} + 1)}{B(2 + N_{H,k},\; 2 + N_{T,k})}, $$

a genuine average of $p(D_{\text{new}} \mid \theta_k)$ over the entire posterior for $\theta_k$, no plug-in anywhere. Multiply by $\pi_k$, take the argmax, and the fully Bayesian pouch also picks coin 2, agreeing with MAP and disagreeing with MLE, and for the more principled reason: coin 3's short history leaves its posterior wide, and integrating over that width dilutes its score, exactly the caution a 10-flip record deserves. So the final scoreboard of the running example:

  • MLE: represent each $\theta_k$ by $\hat\theta_k^{ML}$; predict with the plug-in $p(D_{\text{new}} \mid \hat\theta_k^{ML})$. Picks coin 3.
  • MAP: represent each $\theta_k$ by $\hat\theta_k^{MAP}$; plug-in prediction again. Picks coin 2.
  • Fully Bayesian: keep $p(\theta_k \mid D_k)$ whole; predict by integrating $\int p(D_{\text{new}} \mid \theta_k)\, p(\theta_k \mid D_k)\, d\theta_k$. Picks coin 2.

One sentence each, worth memorizing: MLE selects the best fit to the data; MAP selects the most probable parameter; Bayesian inference retains all plausible parameters. The price climbs with the principle: MLE and MAP are cheap plug-in methods, while Bayesian prediction is more complete but may require integrals that, outside conjugate families like Beta-Bernoulli, have no closed form and demand costly numerical approximation. Conjugacy is the happy case where the integral is free; the wider Bayesian world runs on approximating it.

Run the whole pipeline

def posterior_predictive(nh, nt, a, b):
    return (nh + a) / (nh + nt + a + b)

# the full Beta update, end to end
def beta_update(nh, nt, a, b):
    post_a, post_b = nh + a, nt + b          # conjugate update
    mle  = nh / (nh + nt)
    mode = (post_a - 1) / (post_a + post_b - 2)
    mean = post_a / (post_a + post_b)
    return post_a, post_b, mle, mode, mean

print(beta_update(2, 0, 2, 2))    # (4, 2, 1.0, 0.75, 0.667)
print(beta_update(55, 45, 2, 2))  # (57, 47, 0.55, 0.549, 0.548)

Check your understanding

Finish the die story. With the symmetric Dirichlet prior ($\alpha_k = 2$) and $D = \{2, 4, 4\}$, the Dirichlet-Categorical posterior predictive is $p(x_{\text{new}} = k \mid D) = \frac{n_k + \alpha_k}{N + \sum_j \alpha_j}$. What is the predicted probability of rolling a 4, and of rolling a 6?

Those are the posterior modes. The predictive is the posterior mean: numerator $n_k + \alpha_k$ without the $-1$, denominator without the $-K$. Mode and mean of a Dirichlet differ just as they did for the Beta.
That is the MLE plug-in, zeros and all, and it wagers that a six is impossible after three rolls. The whole point of averaging over the posterior is to decline that bet.
Right. $p(4) = (2+2)/15 = 4/15 \approx 0.267$ and $p(6) = (0+2)/15 = 2/15 \approx 0.133$, and the six probabilities sum to $15/15 = 1$. Note the ordering across the three methods for face 4: MLE 0.667, MAP 0.333, predictive 0.267, increasingly humble as more uncertainty is respected. This mean-not-mode smoothing is exactly what a Bayes classifier wants from its class-conditional estimates, a point the Naive Bayes lecture will cash in.
Check: why is the posterior predictive more robust than MLE or MAP when N is small?

All three estimators are formulas over the same counts. Where does the robustness actually come from?

Answer. From refusing to bet everything on one parameter value. With small $N$ the posterior is wide: many values of $\theta$ explain the data almost equally well. MLE and MAP both collapse that spread to a single point, so any error in the point becomes an error in every subsequent prediction, and MLE's point can even sit on the boundary (probability exactly 0 or 1), the most brittle place possible. The posterior predictive instead averages the prediction over the whole posterior, so plausible-but-not-maximal parameters get their proportional say, extreme claims get diluted by the surrounding mass, and no unseen event is ever assigned zero. The Beta-Bernoulli formulas make the contrast concrete at $(N_H, N_T) = (2, 0)$ with $a = b = 2$: MLE 1.0, MAP 0.75, predictive 0.667, and only the predictive is the actual expected outcome of the next toss under everything you believe. As $N$ grows the posterior narrows and all three converge, so robustness is bought exactly when it is needed and costs nothing when it is not.

6. Summary and method card

One story, told three times with increasing honesty. The likelihood reads the model formula backwards, scoring parameters by the data instead of data by the parameters. MLE takes the peak of that score and inherits its brashness: perfect on big data, reckless on small, and capable of declaring possible events impossible. A prior fixes the recklessness by making prior belief explicit, conjugacy makes the fix free (Beta pseudo-counts plus real counts, exponents adding), and MAP takes the peak of the resulting posterior: MLE plus a principled regularizer, the same object our L2 penalty secretly was. The fully Bayesian move is to stop taking peaks altogether: keep the posterior, integrate predictions over it, and let the width of your uncertainty flow into the humility of your forecasts. This framing, probabilistic models with priors and posteriors as the backbone of machine learning, is the programme championed by researchers like Michael I. Jordan and Zoubin Ghahramani, and it powers far more than coins: the moment a model outputs a probability, someone chose an estimator for its parameters, and everything in this lecture applies.

Method card · Learning Probabilistic Models
AssumptionThe data $D$ come from a probabilistic model $p(D \mid \theta)$ with unknown parameters; prior knowledge enters through $p(\theta)$.
Model classLikelihood, posterior, and posterior-predictive distributions; conjugate pairs (Beta-Bernoulli, Dirichlet-Categorical, Gaussian) where updates are closed-form.
Learning / selectionMLE $\arg\max_\theta p(D \mid \theta)$; MAP $\arg\max_\theta p(D \mid \theta)\, p(\theta)$; Bayesian prediction integrates over $p(\theta \mid D)$.
PredictionPlug-in $p(x_{\text{new}} \mid \hat\theta)$ for MLE and MAP; posterior predictive $\int p(x_{\text{new}} \mid \theta)\, p(\theta \mid D)\, d\theta$ for fully Bayesian.
StrengthsPrincipled and interpretable; priors mitigate sparse-data failures like zero frequencies; Bayesian prediction propagates parameter uncertainty into forecasts.
LimitationsMLE can overfit small samples; MAP still keeps only one parameter value; full Bayesian inference may require costly integration outside conjugate families.
Next stepGenerative classification: Gaussian discriminant analysis and Naive Bayes, which run exactly this machinery per class.

Where this sits in the course story: the pouch was a preview of the next two lectures. Scoring each class by "prior times fit", with the fit's parameters estimated by the methods above, is generative classification. Replace coin types with classes and coin flips with feature vectors, make the fit a Gaussian, and you have Gaussian discriminant analysis; make the features conditionally independent and you have Naive Bayes, where the zero-frequency problem and its Dirichlet cure return as headline acts.

What is the difference between $p(D \mid \theta = 0.5)$ read as a probability and $L(\theta; D)$ read as a likelihood, given that both come from the same Binomial formula?

Same formula, no extra term. Priors enter with MAP, two sections after the likelihood is defined. The distinction is entirely about which symbol is the variable.

Quiz 2

A rare disease test has been run on 3 patients from a new population, all negative. Your model needs $p(\text{positive})$ for this population. Rank the three estimators (with a Beta(2,2) prior for the Bayesian ones) by the value they output.

Only MLE is that absolute. Any prior with $a, b > 1$ keeps unseen outcomes alive, which is the entire reason to use one here.
MAP and the predictive both use the posterior but read different summaries of it: the mode has a $-1$ and $-2$ in it, the mean does not, and with $N = 3$ the difference is far from negligible.

Quiz 3 (transfer): L2 regularization is a Gaussian MAP prior, prove the correspondence

In the logistic-regression lecture we minimized $J_{CE}(w) + \lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2$ and called the penalty "a prior wearing a loss function's clothes". Using this lecture's MAP machinery, make that sentence precise: what is the prior, where does $\lambda$ come from, and what is the MLE analogue?

Answer. Start from this lecture's MAP objective, $\arg\max_w [\log p(D \mid w) + \log p(w)]$, and choose a Gaussian prior on the weights, $p(w) \propto \exp(-\lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2)$, a bell centred at zero that considers small weights more plausible than large ones. Take the negative log to turn maximization into minimization: the likelihood term $-\log p(D \mid w)$ is exactly the cross-entropy loss (that was the likelihood story of the logistic-regression lecture), and the prior term becomes $-\log p(w) = \lambda \lVert w \rVert_2^2$ plus a constant the argmin ignores. So the regularized objective is MAP estimation, term for term. The strength $\lambda$ is the prior's inverse width: a large $\lambda$ is a narrow, opinionated Gaussian that fights the data hard, a small $\lambda$ is a broad, permissive one, and $\lambda \to 0$ flattens the prior toward uniform, at which point MAP collapses to MLE, which is exactly unregularized training. The coin made this same trade visible in miniature: pseudo-counts are to $\theta$ what $\lambda$ is to $w$, and "regularization" and "prior" are one concept with two wardrobes.

Quiz 4 (transfer): when is a plug-in estimate fine, and when do you need the posterior?

You are shipping two systems. A: a click-through-rate model for a high-traffic ad slot with millions of impressions per day. B: a triage model for a new hospital ward with a few dozen labelled cases, where predictions feed a cost-sensitive decision about escalation. For each, plug-in (MLE or MAP) or full posterior predictive, and why?

Answer. A: plug-in, and MLE at that. With millions of observations the posterior is a spike, the three estimators agree to several decimals (the convergence you watched in Fig 5.1), and integrating over a spike buys nothing while costing compute on every request. This is the regime where MLE's simplicity is a pure win. B: the posterior, and not optional. With dozens of cases the posterior over the relevant rates is wide, a plug-in point hides that width, and the decision downstream is cost-sensitive: escalating too rarely and too often have very different prices, so the decision needs $p(\text{event})$ with the parameter uncertainty included, which is precisely the posterior predictive, and ideally the full posterior so different cost thresholds can be applied honestly. Note the general rule hiding in the pair: the value of Bayesian machinery scales with the ratio of decision stakes to data volume. Big data, cheap decisions: plug in. Small data, expensive decisions: integrate. And if you must ship a point estimate in regime B, MAP with a sensible prior is the minimum acceptable hedge, for the same reason coin 2 beat coin 3.

Quiz 5 (transfer): design a prior and defend its numbers

You are modelling the probability that a brand-new email service's messages are opened. Industry experience says open rates cluster around 20 to 30 percent, and you will have only a handful of sends before the first report is due. Design a Beta prior: choose $a$ and $b$, justify both the location and the strength, and say what you would change if the service were instead a minor variant of an existing one with months of data.

Answer. Location first: the prior mean is $a/(a+b)$, and industry experience centres near 0.25, so we want $a/(a+b) \approx 0.25$, a 1-to-3 ratio of $a$ to $b$. Strength second: $a + b$ acts as a pseudo-sample size, and our belief is worth something but not much, since a new service may genuinely differ; something like 8 pseudo-observations is a reasonable weight against "a handful of sends". That gives $\mathrm{Beta}(2, 6)$: mean 0.25, both parameters above 1 so no zero-frequency cliffs at either end, and weak enough that 30 real sends dominate it. Check the behaviour at the edges: with 5 sends and 0 opens, the predictive is $(0+2)/(5+8) \approx 0.15$, appropriately pessimistic but not zero; with 5 sends and 5 opens it is $7/13 \approx 0.54$, excited but far from 1.0. For the minor variant of a mature service, raise the strength, not just the precision of the location: months of data on a near-identical product justify something like $\mathrm{Beta}(25, 75)$ (mean 0.25, pseudo-sample 100), which will hold the estimate near 0.25 until a comparable volume of new evidence accumulates, exactly the stubbornness that history has earned.

Answer. The conjugate update is addition, the mode subtracts one per outcome, and the predictive is the normalized posterior counts:

That is the probabilistic turn. One formula read backwards gave us the likelihood; its peak gave us MLE and the honest confession that 0.55 was a theorem, not a guess. Thin data exposed the peak's recklessness, priors repaired it, conjugacy made the repair free, and MAP reconnected the whole story to the regularization we had been doing all along. Then the real move: keep the distribution, integrate the prediction, and let uncertainty flow through to the forecast instead of being amputated at the parameter.
